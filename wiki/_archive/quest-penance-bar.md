# The Shrine's Ledger

*Draft fiction, not canon. Canon lives on the wiki. Derived pages: [[penance-bar|Penance Bar]], [[sorrowbell|Sorrowbell]], [[corvin-ashe|Corvin Ashe]], [[sula-marn|Sula Marn]].*

The causeway pinch narrows to one cart's width before the fishing town of Penance Bar starts, the whole span of it climbing out of reach of the tideline itself, below, blessings written in ash smears every doorstep the crew passes, day-old and reapplied at dusk, the tang of burnt oil thick enough to catch in the throat.

Father Corvin Ashe met the crew of [[uncertainty|the Uncertainty]] at the door of Sorrowbell, the shrine crowning the headland where the causeway ends, before they'd finished climbing, already talking.

> [!dialogue] Corvin Ashe
> Ah! You must be the noble heroes who answered my call? Tyr’s blessings upon me.

Inside, the chalk tally on the nave wall ran past the last bench and onto bare stone. The first mark went up three weeks back, the night something first dragged itself up out of the reef, and a fresh mark had joined it every night since.

> [!dialogue] Corvin Ashe
> Something's coming across the reef every night now, worse each time, and I've buried three of my own parishioners since the first crossing. I know what's calling them.

He settled into an empty seat, elbows to his knees, and didn't lift his head.

> [!dialogue] Corvin Ashe
> There's a coven working the treeline past the fields. Witches. Keep to themselves. Never set foot in this town in the three years I've been Bishop. Whatever they're doing out there, it's opened a door, and it isn't closing on its own.

His hands hadn't stopped moving since the door, wringing themselves dry of a wet that wasn't there. His eyes went to the fresh mark on the tally wall and away again, fast.

> [!dialogue] Corvin Ashe
> Whatever heresy they're committing is bringing this hellspawn upon the village, and it must end. Find their grove. Find what they're doing out there. Stop it, whatever it takes, before this village runs out of time.

Reeve Sula Marn, an elderly elven woman, caught them on the causeway before they'd reached the fields, her dog trotting a half-step behind.

> [!dialogue] Sula Marn
> He sent you to the grove? Be careful, witches aren’t always what they seem.

The grove sat half a day's walk past the last worked field deep into the interior forests, a stand of old growth the plow had gone around for longer than anyone in Penance Bar could recall. A woman with dirt worked into the creases of her knuckles was splitting kindling on a stump when the crew came through the trees, and she straightened without reaching for anything.

> [!dialogue] Halda Wren
> Welcome to Sandy Grove. I'm Halda Wren. Ask what you came to ask.

Nothing behind her matched what Ashe had described. A drying rack of herbs, common ones: yarrow, willowbark, the kind any midwife on the beach used and none of them credited to a coven. A cold ring of stones held three seasons of ash and nothing else. Children slept in the lean-to, stirring at the strangers' voices only until they heard Halda's, then settling back under.

> [!dialogue] Halda Wren
> We have traded willowbark for salt fish with this village longer than that shrine's had a priest in it. We don't set foot past the fields because we were never asked to, not because we're hiding what we do out here.

Smoke was already rising off the beach by the time the crew came back through the fields. What had been three separate arguments on the sand that morning, over whether the crossings were the witches' doing, had folded into one answer, moving as a single body off the tideline, up the causeway, on toward the treeline beyond the fields.

The shrine door stood open when they reached the point. The light coming through it burned a wet, deep red no tallow candle ever threw. They went in low along the near wall and settled into the shadow past the last bench.

Ashe stood at the basin, back to the door, still talking to the stone bowl the way he'd been talking to it every night this week.

> [!dialogue]
> **Corvin Ashe**: Are the witches dead? Get back out there. They must die before the sun goes down.
>
> **The Shape**: The sun is already down.

He turned at that. The thing in the doorway had the rough shape of a man and none of the rest, skin giving back less light than it took, two dull red points where a face keeps its eyes.

> [!dialogue]
> **Corvin Ashe**: You cannot enter the house of Tyr!
>
> **The Shape**: Tyr is not here. This is an empty box.
>
> **Corvin Ashe**: Tyr is in all his temples.
>
> **The Shape**: Your God's love is not unconditional. He does not love us, and he does not love you.
>
> **Corvin Ashe**: I have done Tyr's bidding. My life's work is in his name.
>
> **The Shape**: Your life's work makes Tyr puke.
>
> **Corvin Ashe**: I am the Bishop of Penance Bar!
>
> **The Shape**: Your God knows we wouldn't be here without you. This is all your fault, isn't it?
>
> **Corvin Ashe**: The widow Voss, the first parishioner I buried, was a witch! Committing Heresy!
>
> **The Shape**: Lies? In your house of God? No wonder he's abandoned you.

It gestured, and more shapes were already creeping through the door behind it, red points blinking on in the dark past the threshold.

> [!dialogue]
> **The Shape**: But we love you. Let me kiss you.
>
> **Ashe**: No! Get back! Get away from me!

The shape leaned in close enough that the candlelight caught the shine of something like teeth, and it kissed him on the forehead. Ashe didn't scream. What came out of him wasn't built for a Human throat. His skin went black in a spreading map along both hands first, the same hands that had wrung themselves dry all morning, and the map kept spreading, finding every place his crimes had ever touched him, until the thing standing at the basin wore Corvin Ashe's face over something that had never been a man.

It looked toward the shadow past the last bench, toward exactly where they crouched, and it smiled with a mouth that had too many teeth in it now to close all the way.

> [!dialogue] The Shape
> Killed the witches yet?

They ran. Out the shrine door, off the point, down the causeway at a dead run, past the causeway's own row of tally-notched rail posts, the outdoor twin to the chalk wall, that would take no new crossing tonight, not after what they'd just broken at the shrine. The crowd was already through the fields, close enough now to hear the flat tramp of feet all moving the same direction at once. Past them, at the treeline, Halda Wren was still splitting kindling, not yet knowing what was coming for her.

The crew reached the front of the crowd before the crowd reached the trees.

What stopped the crowd was three familiar faces from the beach, fishing folk who'd spent three years scrubbing nets and burning oil at Ashe's word, hearing it shouted back at them by the crew's own voices as they ran past: the shrine had failed him the moment it mattered, no god had ever answered him at that basin, and the coven past the fields had never done anything but split kindling and trade willowbark for salt fish. Some of the crowd broke off first and stood still before the rest caught up to why. A torch went into the dirt and stayed there, guttering out on its own.

Halda Wren didn't come out from the treeline until every torch on the field had done the same. She looked at the crew for a long moment, then past them, toward the shrine on the point, where the bell hadn't rung all evening and wasn't going to ring again.

> [!dialogue] Halda Wren
> Three years of that. Every lie fed something. He never told them what they were feeding.

Behind her, in the dark past the trees, the ash ring where nothing had ever burned still held nothing but three seasons of old grey ash, and every stone in the ring was exactly where it had always been.
