# Magic as Commodity

## At a Glance

**Core truth.** [[shattered-sea|The Shattered Sea]] treats simple magic as mass-market technology while scarce, dangerous, or politically potent effects remain guarded.

**Why it matters.** Market access and production difficulty matter more than an item's official rarity when the DM decides who can buy, guard, or exploit magic.

**Scope.** This is a setting-wide economic backdrop, not a single character's arc.
## Current Truth

Enchanting in [[shattered-sea]] works the way manufacturing does. Anything simple enough to standardize gets standardized, and anything a paying market wants eventually reaches a shelf. A frying pan comes with *create bonfire* built into the base for camping. A stone reaches exactly one other stone, handed over free with a sale, a shopkeeper's own goodwill token. Self-cooling iceboxes, self-lighting lanterns, a dozen other conveniences nobody blinks at.

Ordinary people are the actual market, not adventurers. Most enchanted objects in the world exist for a fishmonger or a dockhand, and most of them never come up at the table. Any one of them still gets its own page the moment a scene actually names it, the same as any other entity in this wiki. Nothing here is exempt from that. It's just background texture until a story reaches for it.

What resists that treatment is anything the market can't supply on demand. A component from one specific dead monster. A caster's one-off working nobody else can replicate. A relic-fragment torn off something that made it, once, and will never make another. Effects with real combat or political weight stay guarded too, regardless of whether that raw-material problem applies: a shop sells a self-lighting lantern to anyone. It does not sell a wand that reliably kills people to anyone.

The line isn't the item's official rarity. A narrow, single-purpose Sending Stone is nominally no rarer than a combat wand, and costs a fraction of the price, because one is a product and the other is a weapon.

Nothing in this economy stays truly priceless. A piracy license bearing a real Crown seal would never reach an open market. Neither would a fragment torn from something no one else can touch. Both still carry a number: what the one buyer who wants it badly enough would pay. The sale never happening in the real world doesn't make that number fictional. It makes the number speculative.

This is atmospheric texture with no single PC thread pulling on it: it's the world's standing economic backdrop, not one character's arc.

**Customs & Traditions.**

Two tracks, cutting across the standard rarity ladder instead of following it. The market's own standing institution for how magic gets priced and reaches a buyer:

- **Commodity.** Mass-produced, mechanically simple, no scarce power source, real everyday demand. Roughly 1-25 gp. Most magic in the world sits here, though a page still exists for any piece of it a story actually names.
- **Guarded.** Needs a scarce or unique power source, or carries real combat or political weight, or simply resists mass production. Compressed toward the low end of the traditional Magic Item Rarity bands, given how saturated this world already is with the commodity tier. Uncommon roughly 60-300 gp. Rare roughly 500-2,000 gp. Very Rare 5,000-50,000 gp. Legendary starts at 50,000.
- **Speculative.** Anything nominally "priceless." Artifacts, unique relics, politically radioactive documents. Still gets a real gp figure, set by asking who would actually want it and what it's worth to exactly that buyer. Never left blank.
### Limits

* **Exception.** A simple product can be common even when a similar combat effect is rare or guarded.
* **Unknown to the world.** The page does not price every named magic item or establish every market's local practice.
* **Impossible or forbidden.** No item is literally without a value, but some sales remain speculative or politically impossible.

## At the Table

* **Players notice.** Fishmongers, dockhands, and shopkeepers use simple enchanted goods without treating them as adventurer treasures.
* **This explains.** Why a self-lighting lantern can be sold openly while a combat wand remains guarded.
* **This enables.** The party can buy practical magic, investigate who can reproduce a rare effect, or negotiate a speculative price.
* **This warns of.** Official rarity alone does not reveal whether an item is mass-produced, guarded, or politically radioactive.
* **Relevant now.** Any named magic item becomes a concrete market question when a scene reaches for it.

### Party Knowledge

**Known.** Simple magic can be bought as an ordinary good, while more powerful effects may be guarded.

**Suspected.** The party may suspect that an item's real price depends on its buyer and use rather than its rarity label.

**Misunderstood.** A nominally common item is not automatically safe or available if its effect carries political or combat weight.

**Last changed.** No session is recorded.

### Common telling

> “If it can be made twice, someone will sell it. If it can kill, someone will guard it.”

## If This Is Changing

* **Current pressure.** Markets are expanding simple magic while scarcity and political danger keep stronger effects behind guarded channels.
* **Actors.** Makers, shopkeepers, ordinary buyers, adventurers, and owners of scarce components shape the market.
* **Their aims.** Makers want repeatable profit, buyers want useful tools, and powerful owners want control over dangerous effects.
* **Without interference.** Standardized magic becomes cheaper and more ordinary while unique or potent effects remain guarded.
* **Visible sign.** A named item moves from open shelf to guarded sale, or a new manufacturer makes a formerly unique effect repeatable.

## Consequences

* **Because this is true,** the DM can price and place magic by production, demand, and danger instead of rarity alone.
* **If exposed,** a supposedly priceless relic may acquire a concrete buyer and a speculative price.
* **If disproved,** the setting's distinction between commodity, guarded, and speculative magic would need revision.
* **If changed,** open markets, shop inventories, and political leverage around magic would shift.
* **If exploited,** the party can sell access to a scarce effect, locate the bottleneck in its production, or use a mundane magic good as cover.
