# Half a Mile

*Draft fiction, not canon. Canon lives on the wiki. Derived pages: [[blight|Blight]], [[aruhe|Aruhe]], [[karath|Karath]], [[grung-clans|Grung Clans]].*

The Grung came with the toxin at the same hour every morning.

In the first month she learned how unattainable her goals became under that sedating, delirious toxin. The camp's fence line, and the water, and once, the kitchen knives, all out of reach. It went in warm at the base of the skull, and by the time the sun cleared the camp's ridge her hands were numb and belonged to somebody patient and slow, somebody who obeyed. She
learned the hours by how lucid she felt.

The antidote came after, a tiny swallow, enough to keep her mind present, stand her at a bench, never enough to think with. Her once graceful mastery of the weave, reduced, to something that felt as a tavern brawl.

Mystra had given the Weave to mortals like a promise. She tore at it like a wild dog instead, and the magic came anyway, wrenched loose instead of drawn, and that was how she raised what grew in the breeding pens behind the long huts: vermin bred to the size of hounds, an abomination against Nature and the Weave both.

They couldn’t have existed without her. That was the part that kept her awake at night.

They had taken her off a beach four islands east of here, at night, out of a camp that
had never even seen the small amphibious [[grung-npc-statblock|Grung]] before nor could conceive the horror of what a Grung Raid entailed.

Like her, a fellow captive, he had been there longer, and it had cost him more. He no longer believed Mystra or Nature would forgive him. Some mornings she found him
with his forehead against a post, lips moving, apologizing. The words came too quiet to catch and fitted no prayer she knew. She settled privately on the pens, on the warped things grown in them. She never asked him, in case he said yes. On the days the toxin came weak, he found her in the dark, and they whispered about fire, open water, escape.

Fire first. The long hut. The whole clan would protect it.

Then the eastern path to the ridge and the cliffs.

Leap left, avoid the rocks.

Then the water.

Half a mile of channel, and on the other side of it an island, they keep running, escape east to home.

*And if it goes wrong,* he said once, *I die trying, not caught*

She agreed.

---

The lantern went through the hut's doorway underhand. The oil caught with a soft
‘thump’ sound and then with a roar, and for one moment before the shouting started the whole compound was gold and silent, and she saw his face, grinning in golden flickering light. They ran.

The jungles of Karath greeted them with open arms.

She was delirious, half a day past the antidote, running on a body that had been failing since spring, and yet she swore the jungle itself opened for her. Branches she should have taken across the face lifted. Root tangles she should have gone down in lay flat. A curtain of vine, ancient before her own lifetime, drew back when she reached it, and behind her the [[grung|Grung]] fought the
same forest, ducking, cutting, swearing in their wet clicking speech, losing ground to it.

She ran with her mouth open and her chest full of knives, awake for the first time in four months. She would sooner die in the next three minutes than ever feel so asleep again.

The trees thinned. The ground went pale and stony. Ahead of them the world stopped in a clean teal line, and past it moon and open air and the long silver back of the channel, when she heard the arrow. A thin sound.

Then the noise he made behind her when it found his shoulder.

She glanced back mid-stride to see him stumble, one leg buckled and caught, but he kept his momentum. The shaft stood out of the moonlight, canted back toward his spine. His wild grin unabated. He got a hand on it, snapped the fletching off with a movement so casual it frightened her, and kept running. Four more strides. Then five. They went off the edge of Karath together without breaking pace.

A second of wind then the ocean hit them like a floor.

---

Salt in her sinuses. Her ears roaring. She surfaced into a night that had
rearranged itself, sky and sea traded top for bottom. She found his hand by luck, one breath between them, before she yanked him back under.

Above them the surface was already stitching itself with white lines and sharp points. The
arrows came down through the twilight in long slow arcs through the moonlit water. One passed close enough that she felt the water move on her cheek. Another buried itself in the
sand below them. She watched it, stupidly, for half a heartbeat before she remembered to swim for her life.

They swam east, surfacing only for a brief gasp in the dark trough between swells. Freedom sat too close now to risk their luck any further.

On the third breath she noticed the trail of blood leaking from his shoulder. The wound had opened properly in the water and came off him in a slow red ribbon, thinning and trailing behind them, lit by the moon all the way down into black water where things with teeth already knew. She took his good arm across her shoulders and together they braved the channel.

Half a mile.

She swam it on her side, one arm over, one arm hauling, his arm across her
collarbone and her fist shut in his sleeve. The swell lifted them both and put
them down again. Salt got into the split of her lip and stayed there. The reef
beneath them went from black to grey to a strange luminous pallor and she used
it to steer by, because the beach ahead had stopped changing size an hour ago
and she had given up looking at it. Her legs went somewhere below her and came
back warm, finding sand, packed and giving under the heel, and the shock
of it went up through her like a struck bell, and she laughed, one wet ruined
bark of a laugh, loud, and she clapped a hand over her mouth. Knee-deep in
the surf of a nobody's island, with her legs shaking under her, and the rest of
her life standing open in front of the two of them.

She turned to tell him, but he was twenty feet back, face down, bobbing in the surf.

---

She went back in and got him. He weighed more out of the water than he had in
it, far more, and she had nothing left to lift with, so she took him under the
arms and went backwards up the sand on her heels, a foot at a time, sitting
down twice and getting up twice, until he was clear of the surf.

She healed him. She yanked the Weave into submission, and it came when she pulled, ugly and enormous, more than the wound called for, and the hole in his shoulder closed under her hands and the skin knit clean, and his chest stayed still throughout, stillness pinned where breath belonged.

She healed him again.

She turned him on his side and pressed the water out of him, and it came, a
long grey rope of it into the sand. She took that for an answer. The third
casting gave her back the same whole shoulder under the same breathless silence.

She sat back on her heels with both hands still flat on him and said the private name she'd always called him by, and when nothing in him moved to it she said the rest, loud, into his face, on
an empty island in the middle of the night.

*You promised,* she said. *You promised we'd get away.*

The one promise he ever made her, on a night when honesty was still cheap, he had kept to the letter. She knew it, and she said it anyway, twice more, into a face that had stopped arguing.

She prayed, which she had not done in years, and prayed badly. She begged, which
came easier, then stopped begging and demanded, both hands flat on his
breastbone, telling the Weave out loud and in the imperative what she wanted
from it, until her nose bled onto his throat. She cursed Mystra by name.
She cursed the toxin, and the archer.  

Somewhere in the small hours she stopped, having done everything four times
over. She lay down and put her head on his chest and listened.  She stayed there and wept without sound, her shoulders shaking against his ribs, and no one came.

Dawn found her still there.

---

She took him up at first light and carried him inland, looking for ground to put
him to rest.

The island came out to meet her. The flowers along the treeline had opened in
the night, and every one had opened black-petaled, a whole slope of them with their
faces turned toward her passage. No bird sang the length of that walk, and
behind her the sound of the sea thinned away to nothing. A wolf stopped mid chase in the gap between two trees and lowered its head as she came level with it, and did not raise it again
until she had gone by. The [[deer|deer]] it has been chasing at the edge of a clearing did the same. And the undergrowth, the vine and thorn and knotted green that should have taken her an hour with a blade, parted. It went on parting, opening a road ahead of her a step at a time and closing behind her when she had used it.

It walked with her into a grove the size of a threshing floor, level, with light
coming down through the canopy in columns, and at the centre of it a tree she
recognized before she had finished arriving, because impossibly the fruit hanging in its branches was the fruit he used to eat green off the ground, a month early, every year, before any of this.

She sat down under it with him and did not get up for a long time.

Then she dug. She had no blade and no shovel and the ground was thick with root,
so she went at it with her hands, and the nails came away from two fingers, and
the dirt got into the beds of them and stayed. It took the rest of that day and
most of the night. She put him in herself, and filled it in herself. She
worked in silence the whole way through.

Afterward she built a shelter against the tree's north side out of deadfall and
green cord, and lived in it.

---

The island fed her. Fruit came down within reach on the days she was too weak to
climb for it. Rain stood in leaves that had folded over in the night to hold it.

*You'd have hated this,* she told him, the first time a fruit came down into her
hands on its own. *All just given.*

Her legs came back to her across one season and her mind across the next, and
both arrived worse than they had left: legs slower under her, mind quicker to flinch.

The fury came in after them, grief gone hard and short-fused. She woke one morning with her hands already shut, and lay there in the dark of the shelter with her fists against her sternum,
working out how long they had been like that.

After that it lived in everything. Splitting deadfall, she would find the wood
still in her hand a long time later, because the pens had come back to her, the
joints, the too-many joints, the wet sounds after dark, her own hands at the
making of them. She would be down at the water and see the brown ribbon coming
off him and thinning. He had been four strides short of the cliff when the arrow
found him. She had let go of him twenty feet from the sand. She had the rest of
her life to lie in the dark and put those two numbers next to each other.

In the third summer a sail came round the eastern reef and stood off the
shallows, and she went down on her face in the treeline and watched it. Three
Grung on the deck, one of them up the mast with a hand over his eyes, working
along the treeline from the north point to the south and back again, in no
hurry, twice. They did not land. They came about and went. She stayed where she
was until the light failed, before returning to his tree to speak to him.

*They'll find us here. They'll take us away again.*

Her mind whispered to her a ritual, something taboo and extreme, more of a legend than anything else, but a possibility. A way to protect this place forever.

She dug the second hole beside his. It took two days. She went down until the sides stood above
her shoulders and the cut root ends came through the walls white and weeping.
Then she climbed out and put the little she still had in order: she ate, and
drank the rain, and slept one night with the shelter open to the grove, and in
the morning she pulled the shelter down so that nothing of hers would stand over
him.

Then she got in.

She lay on her back with her arms at her sides and the sky in a long grey strip
above her, and she began the words of it, and the island took her body before she was halfway
through. The dirt came in at the sides on its own. It closed over her feet and
her hips and settled onto her chest with a weight she had not expected, warm,
heavy as a hand held down. Roots came through the wall by her ear and went
past her face, unhurried, and she felt one of them pierce the place in her
wrist where her pulse still hummed. There, she made her vow.

*Come and look upon my garden of plenty, all who love life. But take a single fruit from my island, fish a single river, forage a single plant, or trap a single beast, and Nature's fury finds you whole: the dirt takes you back.*

The Grung still bring boats round the eastern reef when the water lies flat. They do not put a single foot on that sand, not since the last Grung who tried. They have their own name for the island now, it means ‘*hungry isle*’.
