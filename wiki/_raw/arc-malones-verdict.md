# A Job Well Done

*Draft fiction, not canon. Canon lives on the wiki. Derived pages: [[aleksander-malone|Aleksander Malone]], [[sarns-landing|Sarn's Landing]], [[blackrule|Blackrule]].*

Ensign Corah Vance had written eleven reports in her three years with the Crown's inspection service. All eleven were requisitions, weather logs, the dull arithmetic of tithe and cargo. She had never before had to describe what a blade looks like coming out the other side of a man, and she found, writing it, that there was no requisition-form language for it at all. She wrote it plain instead. Plain was the only register left to her by the time she sat down at the desk.

[[sarns-landing|Sarn's Landing]] had asked for nothing. That was what she kept coming back to, after. A fishing village of maybe sixty souls on the [[crown-islands|Crown Islands]]' outer reef, tithe paid on time, its record clean for years. Then the kelp beds started dying, two winters running, and a man who called himself a shepherd of mercy started walking the shoreline with a lantern that never needed oil, and the sick started getting well. Nobody in Sarn's Landing asked where the wellness came from. Vance understood that, sitting in the boat going out. You do not interrogate the thing that saves your daughter's leg. You say thank you and you light a candle for it and you do not ask its name.

Someone asked its name eventually. A Crown chaplain passing through on inspection got close enough to smell the shepherd's lantern-oil, and to know that nothing on that island rendered anything that smelled like it. That report was the one that brought Aleksander Malone to Sarn's Landing, and Vance, and four marines who did not speak the whole crossing.

He came off the boat already praying, not the murmured kind, the kind pitched to carry, and he had the shepherd identified before his boots were dry, not by sorcery, he told her later, by watching who in the crowd flinched at the word *God*. The shepherd did not last the hour. Vance had braced for that part. She had not braced for what came after. Malone turned from the dead man in the shrine doorway to the villagers who had gathered to watch. His face did not change at all. Gently, almost kindly, he asked which of them had taken the cure and never asked its price.

Fourteen hands went up, eventually, under that gentleness. He thanked every one of them for their honesty before he started, and meant it. Vance believed him, which was the thing she could never make the report explain properly. He was not cruel. Cruelty would have been easier to write down. He worked through the fourteen in order, the same motions every time. He rested a hand on each shoulder and murmured the absolution. Then the blade, and he was already reaching for the next before it fell. Fourteen times, exactly the same way, and no less holy on the last than the first. Some of them thanked him back, because by then the lantern-light and the fever dreams and the fear had them believing they deserved it too.

The fourteenth was an old fisher, Halvard Oss, who had let his dying wife take the shepherd's cure without asking, because she was dying and he loved her and the alternative was watching her die anyway with clean hands and an empty house. Malone knelt to be level with him, an old man on his knees on his own shoreline. Vance wrote what he said next down word for word afterward, because she needed someone else to read it and tell her whether it sounded as monstrous as it had felt. What he said was, *May God have mercy on you, for I confess I find I have none left today.*

Then he stood, and wiped the blade, and asked Vance, entirely without irony, whether the garrison had biscuit to spare for the villagers still standing, because it looked like a hard winter and no one here had done anything wrong.

That was the sentence that broke something in how Vance wrote her report. Not the killing. Dravosi law made room for killing: it had ceremonies and commissions and courts martial built for exactly that, and she had watched worse done with less remorse under a privateer's letter. What broke her was that he could stand in blood to his elbows and believe without a flicker of doubt that everyone left breathing had done nothing wrong. The fourteen had needed exactly what they got. The forty-six had needed biscuit. Both were mercy to him, filed under the same word, spoken in the same gentle voice.

Her report did not recommend a commendation. It recommended, in the careful language an ensign uses when she is not senior enough to recommend anything at all, that command review Cleric Malone's future sailings for proportionality. It recommended, too, that whoever signed his orders next weigh exactly what they were asking a witness to carry. Command read it, argued about it for the length of one closed session Vance was never told the outcome of, and then quietly wrote the policy that has governed him ever since: heresy confirmed before he sails, and the shape of the mercy no longer left to him alone.

Sarn's Landing rebuilt its shrine within the year, bare stone, unlit. The tithe still comes in on schedule. But the smuggling lane that opened off that reef eighteen months after Malone's boat pulled away ran quiet and well-organized, unusually well informed about which Crown patrols worked which nights, and it never did get fully traced back to its start. Nobody in [[fort-crestwall|Fort Crestwall]]'s records ever connected it to a shoreline where fourteen people confessed to a stranger for the crime of wanting their families to live.
