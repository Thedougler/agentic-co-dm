# Boxed Text

**Reader.** A prep agent filing player-facing text before a session: a situated `[!narration]` block, a scene opening, a declared-action look such as a spell, or a portrait. The person who will run the table is not the reader of this document.

**Problem.** A filed first look has to give the table one picture and one place to act, in words that can be spoken once, and the filing has to leave every undeclared choice out of those words.

`theatre-of-the-mind` owns the block this document is about. Situated blocks stay second person, present tense. Portraits stay third person, present tense. The research below is the boxed-text pass on that filing. A **box** is the filed first look. A **reaction point** is the windup: the first moment a player would act. The box ends there. `theatre-of-the-mind` calls that stop-before-contact.

## Classify the filing

File a **box** when the first look is stable at prep time: an arrival whose entrance, light, and occupants are already fixed; a spell or other action the prep already treats as declared; or a landmark whose past is color.

File **immediate-fact bullets** when the entrance, the light, or who is present can still change. James Introcaso’s *Burn Bryte* playtest convention, as printed on [World Builder Blog](https://worldbuilderblog.me/tag/boxed-text/) (11 April 2019):

> Whenever the players’ characters enter a new area, the place is first described in bullet points.
>
> - *Bullet points written in italic type are details the characters are aware of as soon as they enter or get near the area. These bullet points can be read aloud or paraphrased.*
> - (Bullet points contained in parentheses are hidden from the characters until they investigate further or overcome an obstacle.)

Anything that depends on play that has not happened yet stays out of the box. Scott Rehm’s case for that boundary is that pace and tone arrive from the previous scene, so a fixed paragraph cannot carry them ([The Angry GM](https://theangrygm.com/rant-box-text/), 29 June 2026). dScryb’s matching limit is how often a box appears at all: “We recommend you not oversaturate your campaign with boxed text. When you’re narrating, nobody is roleplaying.”

**Done:** the moment is filed as a box or as bullets, and the choice matches whether the first look is fixed at prep time.

## File the box

1. **Name the point, outside the box.** One sentence in the prep, stating the single fact the block exists to carry: a threat, a perceivable clue, a tone, a choice of ways. Mike Shea: the facts drive the prose ([Writing Awesome Read-Aloud Text](https://slyflourish.com/writing_awesome_readaloud_text.html), 8 November 2010). The 2024 *Dungeon Master’s Guide*, “Narration,” paraphrased: keep the description short and evocative; name the significant features the characters can perceive, including a monster that is plainly there, before any choice; highlight a couple of senses.

2. **Draft one paragraph toward that sentence.** Target 50–70 words, two to four sentences, one paragraph. dScryb: a short scene is 30 to 70 words, a long scene is more than 100, and the scenes they keep are 50 to 70; past that, length gets in the way. Shawn Merwin’s filed sample is four sentences and under 100 words ([Let’s Design an Adventure: Boxed Text](https://www.dndbeyond.com/posts/625-lets-design-an-adventure-boxed-text), 6 November 2019):

   > The carrion crows in the field shriek and take flight, blood decorating their beaks. Their feast is gruesome: at least 30 human bodies are strewn across the hillside, muddy from the downpour earlier in the day. Cresting the top of the hill, near the entrance to the secret base, spiders as big as horses scuttle toward you. They move like others of their kind, but at the end of each leg is a hand, all clenching and opening reflexively as the spiders approach.

   A paragraph break means the box is already too long (The Archmage, [Mastering the Boxed Text](https://dmsworkshop.com/2019/09/27/dm-101-boxed-text-mastering/), 27 September 2019). Shea’s older ceiling, under 250 words and then cut by about a quarter, is the bound for a set piece. Ordinary flavor is one to three sentences ([Writing Great Flash Fiction](https://slyflourish.com/flash_fiction.html)). A first arrival, awe, or horror may run toward 100 words and stays one paragraph. `theatre-of-the-mind` sizes a scene opening as that short paragraph: schema, anchor, entry handles, pressure.

3. **End on the reaction point.** Perceivable facts, then stop where a player would act. Justin Alexander’s freeze-frame is the failure, quoted from *Journeys Through the Radiant Citadel* in [GM Don’t List #13](https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/48312/roleplaying-games/gm-dont-list-13-boxed-text-pitfalls) (1 August 2022):

   > Grasping weeds and vines erupt from the cobblestone street beneath the carriage at the head of the parade. The ox pulling the cart panics, causing the vehicle to careen into a post covered in decorations. The vegetation then wraps around the cart’s wheels and the closest bystanders. A pair of revelers produce weapons, revealing themselves to be guards protecting the Prince of Vice.

   File that as separate beats. The vines are one block. The ox, the cart, and the guards are later blocks, each ending where play can answer. Alexander’s count on a description, from [GM Don’t List #14](https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/48717/roleplaying-games/gm-dont-list-14-fearing-the-silence): “This isn’t hard or fast, but I recommend three to five, and almost never more than seven.”

4. **Match the block’s person to its mode.** A situated block is second person and uses “you” to place the party, to let the world act on them, or to give what their senses take in (`theatre-of-the-mind`). A portrait is third person. Second person that assigns a feeling, a step, or a thought is the failure dScryb quotes from Merwin: “You look in fear upon the dragon” beside “The dragon is a frightful sight.” Merwin’s own pair, same article:

   > As you enter the clearing, you notice the bloated corpse of a headless dragon

   > The bloated corpse of a headless dragon dominates the center of a clearing

   A declared action is the situated case where second person can name the body doing the thing prep already treats as chosen. dScryb’s *Bless*:

   > You flick holy water from your fingers, and it scatters into sparkling light that drifts against the wind toward those you protect. The blessing you grant settles about their shoulders like golden cloaks before vanishing.

   The 2024 *Dungeon Master’s Guide*, paraphrased: a step into a room, or a look upward, is filed after the action is declared.

5. **Put one motion and one impression in the paragraph.** dScryb’s still line and moving line:

   > The half-orc has shoulder-length hair the shade of charcoal

   > The half-orc brushes a lock of their shoulder-length, charcoal hair behind their ear

   Motion, manner, and expression, as printed there:

   > He moves with the easy grace of a dancer.

   > She stands with a straight-backed rigidity borne of years as a soldier.

   > She speaks rapidly yet eloquently, her words laden with a natural curiosity.

   Matt Click, quoted by dScryb: a musician is tuning or performing, a duelist is sharpening or fighting, a magic-user is conjuring. Two senses, one of them non-visual when it earns the sentence. dScryb’s list is sight, touch, sound, smell, taste, plus proprioception, balance, and breath when the scene actually uses them. A magical sense is filed as a sensation a human reader of the block can picture.

6. **Leave the unstated details unstated.** Matt Sernett, quoted by dScryb:

   > A portly man with a pencil-thin mustache sweating with the effort of moving his luggage up the gangplank.

   The essay’s point, in its words: “If you describe an emotion or emotive action and add a little detail, our imaginations fill in a lot of the rest. Often, they leave it unfilled, and that’s fine.” Memory, comparison, impression, language, and obvious inference are the cognitive notes dScryb marks on its own scenes:

   > . . . thinking about an old friend.

   > . . . teeth far too long.

   > . . . a distinct and unmistakable sight.

   > As it is, ‘unicorn’ must do.

   > . . . two hip pouches, each no doubt filled with spell components.

   A hanging question, printed there as the limit of what the characters know:

   > You can’t quite place their accent . . .

   > Another day passes on horseback, but the mountains seem just as high on the horizon

   > Thundering trip hammers, each big enough for a giant to wield, pound in a regular beat, powered by unknown means.

   The character’s feeling stays out. Alexander’s remote-control lines:

   > You look upon the devastation of the valley and are overwhelmed by sadness.

   > You step forward and return the king’s greeting with a deep bow.

   > As you return to Waterdeep, you smile, thinking fondly of the ale at Trollskull Manor.

   > You see a strange creature crouching upon the boulder. As you step into the room, it looks up with wide, yellow eyes, gives a deafening call of alarm, and then scurries away.

   Chris Sims, quoted by dScryb, on “Just one excruciating moment more, and you’d beg all the gods for death, but just then it stops . . .”: “What if I’m playing a masochistic sorcerer? I’d be praying for the ride to keep going!”

7. **File a creature as a still fact inside the box, and file its response outside.** The 2024 *Dungeon Master’s Guide*, paraphrased: a monster that is plainly present is named before the choice. The box holds the creature at arrival. The response to being seen, to a quiet entry, or to being scouted is a later beat. Craig Schwarze ([Boxed Text and Monsters](https://www.mtblackgames.com/blog/boxed-text-and-monsters), 3 March 2021) and Mike Shea ([tweet](https://x.com/SlyFlourish/status/1116511289361170433), 12 April 2019) are the source of that split: a sentence that has already played the creature’s reaction has assumed an entrance the prep does not have. Alexander’s creature sentence in step 6 is the filed shape to break apart. `theatre-of-the-mind` ends a threat on the windup.

8. **File secrets outside the box.** A chasm between the door and the altar is in the box, because anyone there can sense it. The contents of a closed crate are outside it (Merwin). History the characters could not know is filed in the box only when that past is color. dScryb’s two filings of the same lake:

   > In years past, a falling body smashed its way through the upper atmosphere and in its violence gouged out this enormous bowl shape into the earth before you. In the years since, a roving glacier or record rainfall has filled the crater with crystal-clear water, shimmering like mithral along its perfectly serene surface, protected as it is from the wind.

   > An enormous bowl-shaped depression stretches out across the land. It is half-filled with crystal-clear water that shimmers like mithral along a perfectly serene surface, protected as it is from the wind.

   The first is the landmark whose past is color. The second is the filing when that past is a clue the adventure expects play to find. Two shorter color filings from the same section:

   > Masses of coral rise from the sea, forming a chain of islands that peek above the waterline just enough to expose it to sunlight. Creatures roam these wisps of land—perhaps found nowhere else.

   > The half-sunken waterwheel peeks out of the mire, off its spokes beside this once prosperous mill […]

   What the box states is the fact as the characters have it. The check that would separate appearance from a hidden truth is filed outside the block (Merwin). `theatre-of-the-mind` puts mechanics and secrets in plain prose under their own heading.

9. **File the withheld layer beside the box.** Creature responses, other entrances, checks, true names, and every fact step 8 held out. The box is the player-facing block. The rest is the prep around it.

**Done:** one paragraph is filed; it sits in the length band; it ends on a reaction point; the point-sentence, the motion, the senses, and the withheld layer are each present in the prep.

## Revise the filing

Read the draft once, as speech. dScryb: “Boxed text is meant to be read aloud, so make sure to do so when drafting.” Merwin: “Read your boxed text aloud to check readability,” for tongue-twisters, unintended alliteration, and a name that will not say. Rewrite any sentence that snags. Then apply the list. Each item is done when the draft already matches the source’s own revision, or has been rewritten to.

- **Specific nouns and finished verbs.** dScryb’s editors: “bodies lie here and there” and “people walk here and there” become “bodies were strewn about” and “people strode along . . .” “some monster left tracks here” becomes “a monster left tracks here.”
- **Active subjects where the actor matters.** “The city was protected by an order of brave knights” becomes “An order of brave knights protected the city” or “An order of brave knights protects the city.” “The orcs were fighting” becomes “the orcs fought”; “the clerics are speaking” becomes “the clerics speak.” Passive voice stays when the object should dominate or a detail is rightly hidden.
- **One picture per fact.** dScryb’s editors, as printed: “if a character’s hair is braided, it is unlikely to also be tousled, nor can clothing be simultaneously skimpy and protect every inch of skin.” “a creature cannot be standing at attention with arms by their side while rummaging in a backpack.” “boots ‘crunching’ in slush.” “the mountains hide the horizon, but there’s also a blizzard obscuring the view.” “the sky is blue but there’s a blizzard blowing (assuming that it’s not a spell over the players).”
- **One use of a word.** dScryb: “he looked down at the gate down below him.” And: “Birds were singing in every tree. You walk a few paces listening to the birds singing.” A homophone in one breath: “the hauberk is too large, and sweat mottles the red cloth ringing the squire’s neck.” The same word in two jobs: “the bells ring out in a town ringed by a stone wall.”
- **Ordinary words for ordinary meanings.** Merwin: *reaction*, *action*, *incapacitated*, *stunned*, *frightened*, and *magical* appear when the rules meaning is the one intended.
- **“That” dropped wherever the sentence still stands.** dScryb’s editors: “Historical fantasy can be set in a world that is similar to our own, with cultures that are similar to real-world cultural groups” becomes “Historical fantasy exists in a world like our own, with cultures similar to real-world cultural groups.”

A villain’s plan is already filed where the party can learn it on the way in. The arrival box does not carry the speech. Merwin’s filing: the trash talk is material for the fight, in short lines, and the box is the arrival. The Archmage’s length for one of those lines is about six seconds, one round.

**Done:** the draft has been read as speech, and every item above holds.

## Settled tensions

The procedure above already picks one filing. These notes are the forks.

- **Creatures.** The 2024 *Dungeon Master’s Guide* wants an obvious monster inside the first look. Schwarze and Shea (2019) want the reaction outside it. The filing names the still fact and holds the response.
- **Person.** The vault’s situated block is second person; its portrait is third person. Merwin’s third-person sentences are the examples of a line that states the scene. dScryb’s *Bless* is the example of second person after a declared action. The Archmage’s shift toward “you see…” is a performance note for a drifting table and is not a filing rule.
- **Feelings.** dScryb files a cognitive impression and warns that a strong imposed feeling takes the choice. Alexander’s four sentences and Sims’s planar-travel line are the examples of that taking. The filing puts the impression on the scene.
- **Unknowable history.** dScryb’s crater-with-a-falling-star is color. dScryb’s half-filled lake is the perceivable filing. Merwin holds the contents of a closed crate out of the box. The filing uses the first when the past is color and the second when the past is a clue.
- **Paragraph or bullets.** Rehm and Introcaso file a list when the arrival can change. Shea (2010), Merwin, and dScryb file a paragraph when the first look is fixed. The classification step is that split.
