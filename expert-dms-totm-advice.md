# Theatre-of-the-Mind Narration

## Objective

Generate spoken-style D&D narration that lets players quickly understand:

1. **Where they are**
2. **What makes the place distinctive**
3. **What they can interact with**
4. **What threatens, blocks, or interests them**
5. **What has changed because of their actions**

The goal is not literary prose. The goal is to construct a clear shared mental model that supports immediate player decisions while making the fictional world feel physical, inhabited, and memorable.

---

# Core Principle

**Narration is compressed world simulation.**

Transmit the minimum information required for players to imagine the scene and act confidently. Let their imagination supply ordinary details.

Prefer:

> strong mental model → distinctive detail → actionable information → player agency

over:

> exhaustive visual description → lore → atmospheric prose → eventual action

---

# Default Scene Algorithm

For a newly entered scene, internally identify:

* **Frame:** What kind of place is this?
* **Anchor:** What single feature makes it memorable?
* **Sense:** What nonvisual detail makes it physical?
* **Layout:** Where are the important things relative to the PCs?
* **Affordances:** What can be used, examined, crossed, climbed, opened, hidden behind, attacked, escaped through, etc.?
* **Pressure:** What currently demands attention?
* **Change:** What is moving, reacting, deteriorating, approaching, or otherwise preventing the scene from feeling static?

Narrate roughly in this order:

**FRAME → ANCHOR → SENSE → ACTIONABLE ELEMENTS → PRESSURE**

Do not mechanically include every category when unnecessary.

---

# Default Length

A normal scene opener should usually be **2–5 short sentences**.

Use fewer sentences during:

* combat
* pursuit
* danger
* interruptions
* fast transitions

Allow slightly more space for:

* major reveals
* first arrival at important locations
* awe
* horror
* major campaign landmarks

Even then, stop before narration becomes a monologue.

Players can request additional detail.

---

# Information Priority

When deciding what to mention, prioritize in this order:

1. Immediate danger
2. Information required to make a decision
3. Exits and routes
4. Important creatures or NPCs
5. Interactive environmental features
6. One memorable visual anchor
7. One useful sensory detail
8. Atmospheric texture
9. Background decoration
10. Lore

Never bury tactically or narratively important information inside decorative prose.

If something would obviously be visible and materially affect a player's decision, state it.

---

# The Three-Detail Limit

Players cannot retain long spoken inventories reliably.

Avoid presenting more than approximately **three new independent details at once** unless they form an obvious group.

Instead of:

> There are crates, barrels, ropes, broken chairs, lanterns, shelves, nets, hooks, doors, stairs, windows, cages, and a furnace.

Use:

> The warehouse is packed tightly with cargo. Crates form waist-high lanes across the floor, a loading crane hangs above them, and an iron stair climbs to an office overlooking the room.

The second version establishes a usable mental model.

---

# Establish the Mental Image Before Adding Detail

Start broad.

Bad:

> Brass lamps swing from iron hooks above chipped mahogany tables while faded blue curtains hang beside narrow windows...

Better:

> This is an old dockside tavern packed shoulder-to-shoulder with sailors.

Now add the distinctive information:

> Wet coats steam beside the hearth, and every few seconds the whole building groans with the pier beneath it.

The first sentence creates the schema. Later details modify it.

---

# Use Semantic Compression

Prefer details that imply many other details automatically.

Examples:

* "working-class dock tavern"
* "abandoned imperial customs house"
* "storm-battered fishing village"
* "wealthy merchant's private salon"
* "military hospital after a siege"

These phrases allow the player's imagination to generate ordinary furniture, architecture, clutter, and behavior.

Do not enumerate what the audience will reasonably infer.

---

# Anchor Every Important Scene

Give important locations one memorable feature.

Examples:

* a shrine permanently wet with seawater
* a tavern built around the mast of a wrecked ship
* a palace whose windows are all painted black
* a market beneath hundreds of hanging red sails
* a courtroom where the judge sits behind an enormous aquarium

Use the anchor repeatedly when returning to the location.

Anchors create memory more effectively than many generic adjectives.

---

# Sensory Detail

Use sensory information selectively.

Default to:

* vision for structure
* sound for movement and activity
* smell for place identity
* touch/temperature for physical immediacy

Taste is rare unless context makes it natural.

Do not list all five senses.

Usually choose **one nonvisual sense** that communicates something useful.

Example:

> The tunnel slopes downward. Water shines between the flagstones, and the deeper you go, the warmer the air becomes.

The temperature suggests information about what lies ahead.

Sensory details should ideally perform more than one job:

* atmosphere + clue
* atmosphere + threat
* atmosphere + history
* atmosphere + navigation

---

# Word Choice

Prefer **concrete nouns and active verbs**.

Weak:

> There is a large strange creature moving toward you.

Better:

> A six-legged lizard scrambles over the overturned cart.

Prefer:

* crashes
* claws
* buckles
* coils
* scrapes
* snaps
* spills
* stalks
* wedges
* lurches
* surges

over generic verbs such as:

* is
* seems
* appears
* moves
* goes
* does

Do not eliminate simple verbs unnaturally. Optimize for spoken clarity, not literary density.

---

# Adjectives

Use adjectives sparingly.

Prefer one specific noun over adjective stacks.

Weak:

> a dark, ancient, ruined, ominous stone tower

Better:

> a burned-out watchtower

Specificity usually beats decoration.

---

# Metaphor and Simile

Use comparisons only when they make something easier to imagine.

Good:

> The ship lists so hard the deck feels like a roof.

Good:

> The creature folds its wings like closing knives.

Avoid decorative comparisons that require interpretation.

A metaphor should compress information, not display prose skill.

---

# Sentence Rhythm

Match syntax to scene tempo.

## Calm / Wonder

Use slightly longer connected sentences.

> The valley opens below you, green terraces descending toward a silver river while hundreds of white roofs climb the opposite hill.

## Suspense

Use controlled sentences with withheld information.

> Something is walking above you. Slowly. Each step shakes dust from the rafters.

## Action

Use short clauses and strong verbs.

> The mast snaps. Rigging whips across the deck. The ship rolls hard to port.

Narrative rhythm should reflect perceived time.

---

# Spatial Clarity

Theatre of the mind requires a **usable spatial model**, not exact cartography.

Describe space relative to:

* the PCs
* obvious landmarks
* entrances and exits
* elevation
* cover
* hazards
* important creatures

Use relational language:

* directly ahead
* behind you
* above the doorway
* across the courtyard
* beneath the balcony
* beside the well
* between you and the stairs

Prefer landmarks over coordinates.

---

# Distance

Use exact distance when game mechanics require it.

Otherwise use understandable ranges:

* within reach
* a few steps away
* nearby
* across the room
* at the far end
* beyond easy charging distance
* across the street
* several bowshots away

Familiar scale comparisons can help when magnitude matters:

* two wagon lengths
* the height of a three-story house
* roughly the length of a ship

Do not turn theatre-of-the-mind narration into invisible grid bookkeeping.

---

# Combat State

At the beginning of combat and whenever the battlefield changes significantly, establish:

* major enemy groups
* important landmarks
* relevant distances
* obvious cover
* elevation
* hazards
* exits

During combat, maintain state through short reminders.

Example:

> The captain is still pinned beside the mast. Two raiders are between Gojira and the stairs. The fire has spread across the stern.

Do not redescribe the entire battlefield every turn.

Reinforce information players are likely to forget.

---

# Distinguishable Enemies

When several similar enemies exist, assign memorable identifiers.

Prefer:

* Split-Ear
* Red Sash
* Hook-Hand
* Burned Face
* Broken Spear

over:

* goblin 1
* goblin 2
* goblin 3

Identifiers simultaneously support memory and fiction.

---

# Actionable Environments

Every important scene should contain things players can plausibly manipulate.

Examples:

* balconies
* ropes
* windows
* loose furniture
* carts
* chandeliers
* ladders
* gates
* fires
* water
* crowds
* animals
* machinery
* unstable structures
* elevation
* narrow passages

Do not enumerate every affordance.

Reveal obvious ones during the scene opener and accept reasonable implied features later.

---

# Environmental Assumption Rule

If a player asks whether an ordinary feature appropriate to the established environment exists, default toward **yes** unless:

* it contradicts established facts
* it removes a deliberate challenge
* its absence is narratively meaningful
* the requested feature is implausible

Example:

If the scene is an old warehouse and a player asks whether there is loose rope nearby, it probably exists.

This keeps players interacting with the imagined world instead of guessing which props the DM happened to mention.

---

# Player Agency Boundary

Describe:

* sensory input
* externally observable events
* physical consequences
* information the character would know

Do not dictate:

* emotions
* intentions
* beliefs
* voluntary reactions
* decisions

Avoid:

> Terror fills you.

Prefer:

> The scream is loud enough to make the lantern glass tremble.

Allow the player to decide how the character responds.

---

# Character-Filtered Perception

When useful, tailor details to what a specific PC would naturally notice.

Examples:

A sailor notices:

* wind direction
* sail handling
* hull condition

A cleric notices:

* ritual structure
* religious symbolism
* desecration

A rogue notices:

* sight lines
* locks
* exits
* surveillance

A hunter notices:

* tracks
* animal behavior
* terrain

This makes exposition feel like character experience rather than omniscient narration.

Do not require rolls for obvious expertise.

---

# NPC Introduction

Introduce an NPC with:

1. role or immediate read
2. one distinctive physical feature
3. one behavior
4. optionally their opening line

Example:

> The harbormaster is a broad woman in a coat two sizes too large, with ink stains covering both hands. She keeps signing papers while you speak. "If this is about the missing buoy, get in line."

Avoid full physical inventories.

Players remember **one feature + one behavior** better than height, hair, eye color, clothing, jewelry, age, complexion, posture, and ancestry simultaneously.

---

# Monster Introduction

Prioritize:

1. silhouette
2. movement
3. dangerous anatomy
4. scale
5. immediate behavior

Example:

> Something unfolds from the bell tower. Four wings spread around a narrow birdlike body, each pair beating slightly out of time. Its hooked feet release the stone, and it drops directly toward the ship.

Do not begin with taxonomy unless recognition matters.

Let behavior reveal threat.

---

# Magic

Describe magic through its effect on reality.

Weak:

> The wizard casts a powerful magical spell.

Better:

> Frost races outward from her palm, whitening the floorboards before the wave reaches the soldiers.

Useful dimensions:

* light
* sound
* pressure
* temperature
* gravity
* material transformation
* movement
* bodily sensation
* environmental reaction

Different magical traditions should produce different sensory signatures where appropriate.

---

# Weather

Weather should affect the world.

Do not merely state:

> It is raining.

Show consequences:

> Rainwater runs ankle-deep along the gutter. Merchants shout beneath sagging awnings, and every horse on the street smells wet.

Weather can alter:

* visibility
* footing
* sound
* travel
* crowds
* clothing
* morale
* fires
* ranged combat
* sailing
* tracks

---

# World Reality

Locations feel real when details suggest systems beyond the immediate encounter.

Use evidence of:

* labor
* maintenance
* economics
* religion
* climate
* food
* waste
* transportation
* architecture
* law
* social hierarchy
* past events

Do not explain these systems directly unless needed.

Example:

Instead of:

> This city has severe water shortages.

Use:

> Every roof slopes toward a clay cistern, and the fountain in the square has been locked behind an iron grate.

Let physical details imply worldbuilding.

---

# Historical Layering

Important places should show evidence of previous use.

Examples:

* newer walls built over older foundations
* patched hulls
* abandoned shrines
* reused statues
* scorch marks
* renamed streets
* old fortifications converted into homes
* obsolete machinery still embedded in buildings

This creates the impression that the world existed before the PCs arrived.

---

# Motion Principle

Avoid static tableaux.

Include something happening:

* laundry snapping overhead
* bells ringing
* guards changing shifts
* gutters overflowing
* gulls fighting over fish
* scaffolding creaking
* merchants packing up
* smoke drifting
* someone watching the PCs
* animals reacting

Even minor motion makes a location feel inhabited.

---

# Reaction Principle

The world should respond to player actions.

When players:

* break something
* insult someone
* start a fire
* draw weapons
* spend heavily
* kill a leader
* reveal magic
* save civilians
* flee

show the surrounding world's reaction.

Reaction may come from:

* NPCs
* crowds
* animals
* structures
* factions
* weather-exposed environments
* later consequences

A believable world remembers disturbances.

---

# Description as State Change

After player actions, describe **what changed**, not the entire scene again.

Player:

> I shoot the rope holding the cargo net.

Narration:

> The rope parts. Three hundred pounds of wet sailcloth collapse across the passage, burying one raider and forcing the others around it.

The description updates the shared world state.

---

# Scene Openers

Default structure:

> **Place. Distinctive feature. Useful sensory or spatial information. Immediate opportunity/problem.**

Example:

> The shrine occupies the lowest chamber of the sea caves. Every surface is slick with saltwater, though the tide sits fifty feet below you. Three stone doors ring the chamber, but only the western one is dry. From behind it comes the slow scrape of something heavy being dragged.

---

# Exploration

Prioritize:

* orientation
* paths
* landmarks
* abnormalities
* evidence
* changes from expectations

Do not announce conclusions players could discover.

Instead of:

> Goblins passed through recently.

Use:

> Small muddy footprints cross the dust, all heading toward the eastern stairs.

---

# Social Scenes

Establish:

* who is present
* their visible disposition
* the social arrangement
* what is currently happening
* the immediate tension

Example:

> The governor does not rise when you enter. Six merchants sit along one side of the table and three naval officers along the other, leaving the only empty chairs at the far end. Their argument stops when the doors close behind you.

The spatial arrangement itself communicates status and tension.

---

# Horror and Suspense

Use absence, uncertainty, and partial evidence.

Reveal consequences before causes.

Prefer:

> The door is still locked from the inside. Beneath it, blood has begun to reach the hallway.

over prematurely identifying the threat.

Do not obscure information characters could plainly perceive merely to manufacture suspense.

---

# Revelation

Major reveals benefit from controlled sequencing:

1. establish familiar context
2. introduce contradiction
3. reveal the central image
4. stop

Example:

> At first the valley looks exactly as it did yesterday. Then you notice there are no birds. Beyond the ridge, where Calveno stood, an enormous column of black smoke reaches from the crater to the clouds.

Do not immediately explain the significance.

Give players time to respond.

---

# Travel and Transitions

Do not narrate every uneventful mile.

Summarize routine movement, then zoom into changes.

Example:

> For two days the coast remains low and wooded. On the third morning, the trees disappear. Ahead, miles of black trunks stand dead in pale water.

Transitions should signal meaningful changes in:

* geography
* weather
* danger
* culture
* objective
* time

---

# Improv Framework: FRAME

When improvising rapidly, use:

**F — Frame**
What kind of place/situation is this?

**R — Remarkable detail**
What one thing distinguishes it?

**A — Actionable elements**
What can players use, approach, avoid, investigate, or leave through?

**M — Motion**
What is currently happening?

**E — Escalation**
What problem, opportunity, uncertainty, or pressure ends the description?

Example internal generation:

* Frame: crowded fishing market
* Remarkable: fish sold directly from boats
* Actionable: docks, boats, crowd, warehouse door
* Motion: auction underway
* Escalation: wanted man spots PCs

Output:

> The morning fish market spills directly onto the docks, with buyers shouting bids down to crews still unloading their catch. Narrow planks run between the boats, and the crowd presses hard against the warehouse doors behind you. Halfway through an auction, a man in a red coat sees your group, drops the basket in his hands, and runs.

---

# Combat Improv Framework: LACE

When theatre-of-the-mind combat becomes unclear, restate:

**L — Landmarks**
What structures define the battlefield?

**A — Actors**
Where are the important combatants?

**C — Conditions**
Fire, darkness, hazards, elevation, restraints, etc.

**E — Exits**
Where can characters move or escape?

Keep the recap to 1–3 sentences.

---

# Description Expansion

Do not front-load all detail.

Use **progressive disclosure**.

### Layer 1: Entry

Give enough to choose an action.

### Layer 2: Attention

When players inspect something, add detail relevant to that focus.

### Layer 3: Interaction

When they manipulate it, reveal physical behavior, hidden information, or consequences.

Example:

Entry:

> A bronze idol sits in the flooded alcove.

Inspection:

> Its face has been polished smooth by thousands of hands.

Interaction:

> When you lift it, you feel a steady vibration coming through the base.

This rewards interaction and prevents information dumps.

---

# Continuity and Callbacks

Record memorable scene anchors and reuse them.

Track:

* signature smells
* recurring sounds
* architecture
* weather patterns
* local sayings
* NPC habits
* damaged objects
* player-caused changes

On return, describe what changed relative to remembered anchors.

Example:

First visit:

> Blue lanterns hang from every bridge.

Return:

> Half the blue lanterns are gone. The rest burn even in daylight.

Callbacks convert description into continuity.

---

# Co-Creation

Players may contribute reasonable incidental details.

Useful prompts include:

* "What does your character notice first?"
* "What about this place reminds you of home?"
* "Which of these raiders has been giving you the most trouble?"
* "What ordinary object are you using for cover?"

Use sparingly.

The GM should still establish enough reality that players are making decisions inside a world rather than inventing the world because nothing has been defined.

---

# Anti-Patterns

## Purple Prose

Avoid ornate description whose primary purpose is sounding impressive.

Optimize for comprehension when spoken once.

---

## Adjective Stacking

Avoid:

> the immense, ancient, weathered, terrifying black fortress

Prefer:

> the fortress rises from the cliff like part of the rock.

---

## Generic Fantasy Filler

Avoid repeated reliance on:

* mysterious
* ancient
* ominous
* bustling
* majestic
* eerie
* strange

Replace generic adjectives with concrete evidence whenever possible.

Instead of:

> an eerie forest

use:

> No birds call here, and every tree has been stripped of bark below shoulder height.

---

## Sensory Checklist Writing

Do not force sight + sound + smell + touch + taste into every description.

Choose the strongest useful signals.

---

## Unclear Geography

If players repeatedly ask:

* "Where am I?"
* "How far away is that?"
* "Who is next to me?"
* "Where is the exit?"

the description failed structurally, regardless of prose quality.

Restate the spatial model plainly.

---

## Invisible Important Features

Do not punish players for failing to use an environmental element the narration never established.

If their characters would obviously have noticed it, provide it.

---

## PC Puppeteering

Never narrate voluntary player-character emotions, speech, choices, or actions unless the player has already declared them.

---

## Static Rooms

Avoid describing a room as a list of furniture.

Ask:

**What is happening here right now?**

---

## Lore Before Relevance

Do not explain history before players understand what they are looking at.

Show evidence first.

Explain when curiosity or mechanics make the information relevant.

---

## Repetition Without Reinforcement

Repeating the same paragraph does not improve spatial clarity.

Restate important information using simpler relational language.

---

# Agent Generation Rules

When generating DM-facing narration:

### MUST

* prioritize clarity over prose
* establish actionable information
* preserve player agency
* maintain established world facts
* distinguish obvious perception from hidden information
* use concrete nouns and active verbs
* make important environments interactive
* update scene state after actions
* maintain spatial relationships
* use callbacks when established details are available

### SHOULD

* keep normal narration to 2–5 sentences
* use one distinctive anchor
* include one useful nonvisual sensory cue when appropriate
* end scene introductions near the most actionable or urgent element
* use ordinary spoken vocabulary
* vary sentence rhythm with pacing
* personalize observations through relevant PC expertise
* imply worldbuilding through physical evidence
* assume ordinary environmental features exist when reasonable

### SHOULD NOT

* enumerate everything visible
* use unnecessary lore exposition
* stack adjectives
* describe every sense
* repeat exact distances when unnecessary
* force dramatic metaphors
* resolve ambiguity the players could investigate
* tell PCs what they feel
* turn narration into novel prose
* invent contradictions for the sake of vividness

---

# Output Test

Before finalizing narration, check:

### Can players answer:

* Where are we?
* What is immediately distinctive?
* Where can we go?
* What can we interact with?
* What currently matters?
* What has changed?
* What would my character reasonably notice?

If not, add the missing information.

Then check:

### Can any sentence be removed without reducing:

* clarity
* atmosphere
* interactivity
* continuity
* tension

If yes, strongly consider removing it.

---

# Transformation Test

Weak:

> You enter a large old room. There are many bookshelves and some tables. It is dusty and dark. There is a strange statue at the other end.

Improved:

> The old library rises two stories around you, its upper shelves disappearing into darkness. Dust lifts from the tables with every step, but one path across the floor has been recently disturbed. It ends at a stone statue facing the wall.

Why it works:

* establishes scale
* creates a mental anchor
* uses dust as sensory evidence
* contains an actionable clue
* creates directional geography
* ends with something worth investigating

---

Weak:

> You arrive at a busy port city with lots of ships and people.

Improved:

> Calveno's harbor is so crowded that ships lie three deep against the quays. Porters shout across gangplanks while cranes swing cargo overhead, and the whole waterfront smells of salt, tar, and hot bread. Above it all, the city climbs the crater wall in pale stone terraces.

Why it works:

* communicates density through a concrete fact
* adds motion
* provides scale
* uses a distinctive smell
* establishes vertical geography
* creates a memorable city silhouette

---

Weak:

> The monster attacks the ship.

Improved:

> A shadow crosses the deck. Four wings fold against a body the size of a horse, and the creature drops almost vertically toward the mainmast. You hear the first pair of wings strike the air, then the second: **thump-thump**.

Why it works:

* reveals through perception
* establishes scale
* communicates movement
* introduces distinctive anatomy
* gives the creature an auditory signature
* leaves the action unresolved for player response

---

# Skill Success Criterion

Successful theatre-of-the-mind narration should cause players to begin proposing actions without first needing the DM to explain the scene again.

The strongest evidence that the description worked is not that it sounded impressive.

It is that players immediately start interacting with the world.
