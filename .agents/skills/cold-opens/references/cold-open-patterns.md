# Cold Open Patterns

Load this reference when choosing the shape of a cold open, building the five-check ratchet, handling hostile POVs, or solving an ending that lacks impact.

The main workflow lives in `../SKILL.md`; the craft behind it in `cold-open-method.md`.

## Archetypes

### Crisis Run

**Use when:** the audience should immediately experience something already going wrong.

Examples:

- city under attack;
- ship sinking;
- magical disaster;
- prison breach;
- monster incursion;
- collapsing ruin;
- battlefield retreat.

**Hook shape:** action-heavy Hook / Cliffhanger-shaped Hook.

Typical ratchet:

1. understand the danger;
2. survive its first impact;
3. protect something important;
4. discover the danger is worse than expected;
5. escape, endure, or witness the decisive event.

Best handoffs:

- urgency;
- scale;
- dread;
- evidence of enemy capability.

### Looming Threat

**Use when:** showing the threat directly would reveal too much, but the players should feel it approaching.

Examples:

- animals fleeing a forest;
- inexplicable tides;
- abandoned watchtower;
- distant lights;
- vanished patrol;
- impossible weather;
- ritual signs appearing across a settlement.

**Hook shape:** Development-shaped Hook, usually foreshadowing.

Ratchet information rather than raw danger.

Each check should reveal a more consequential symptom while preserving the underlying answer.

Best handoffs:

- dread;
- mystery;
- anticipation.

### Discovery

**Use when:** someone elsewhere finds something the PCs will eventually care about.

Examples:

- relic;
- body;
- hidden chamber;
- message;
- shipwreck;
- magical anomaly;
- secret route;
- impossible survivor.

The physical process of reaching, extracting, interpreting, or escaping with the discovery supplies interaction.

Do not explain the discovery completely unless the purpose of the cold open is the revelation itself.

Best handoffs:

- curiosity;
- dramatic irony;
- future objective.

### Witness

**Use when:** an event matters more than the temporary character.

The POV character provides human scale while the audience witnesses:

- a coup;
- assassination;
- arrival;
- catastrophe;
- supernatural manifestation;
- betrayal;
- army movement;
- transformation.

The POV character may escape, fail, disappear, or die. Their fate remains interactive even when the witnessed event itself is fixed.

Best handoffs:

- scale;
- emotional grounding;
- proof that something happened.

### Distant Ally

**Use when:** the table already cares about an NPC whose independent story is moving.

Give the ally an immediate objective connected to their established role.

Use the sequence to demonstrate agency: the NPC is doing something rather than waiting offscreen for the PCs.

Best handoffs:

- attachment;
- worry;
- anticipation;
- changed relationship context.

### Message Run

**Use when:** information needs to move through a dangerous world.

The fixed trajectory is the attempt to carry, send, protect, decode, or transmit the message.

Checks determine:

- speed;
- integrity;
- interception;
- casualties;
- whether a copy survives;
- who learns the information along the way.

The message itself need not reach its destination during the cold open.

Best handoffs:

- urgency;
- dramatic irony;
- incoming trouble.

### Infiltration

**Use when:** a spy, scout, thief, or operative can expose part of a hidden situation.

Checks can cover:

1. entry;
2. navigation;
3. observation;
4. complication;
5. extraction or final discovery.

Information is the main reward.

Failure increases exposure, suspicion, injury, or incomplete information rather than automatically ending the infiltration.

Best handoffs:

- clue;
- revelation;
- enemy characterization.

### Last Stand

**Use when:** the audience should understand what something cost.

The endpoint may be strategically fixed: the wall falls, the ship is lost, the ritual completes, reinforcements do not arrive.

The players still determine:

- who is saved;
- how long the defenders hold;
- what evidence survives;
- whether the enemy pays a price;
- whether a warning gets out;
- what the final survivor sees.

A predetermined loss is compatible with meaningful play when the **stakes inside the loss remain variable**.

Best handoffs:

- grief;
- respect;
- urgency;
- scale.

## Hostile POV

Use a hostile or villain POV when seeing the world through that character reveals something more effectively than hearing about it later.

### Preserve motive, expose method

Established characters retain their established goals.

Give players agency over:

- tactics;
- improvisation;
- immediate priorities;
- style;
- which obstacle receives attention;
- acceptable costs where the NPC could plausibly choose;
- reactions to unexpected complications.

Keep established strategic commitments fixed when changing them would contradict the character.

Example:

The antagonist **will** steal the relic because that operation is established.

Players may determine:

- silent entry or social deception;
- whether witnesses are spared;
- whether evidence is planted;
- how much risk the antagonist accepts;
- which exit route is used.

The table controls **how**, not whether the antagonist becomes a different person.

### Protect secrets

A villain POV does not require exposing the villain's entire internal plan.

Players need enough information to perform the immediate scene.

Useful techniques:

- give the immediate objective but not its ultimate purpose;
- refer to a superior by title rather than identity;
- show orders without explaining their source;
- let players witness procedures whose significance becomes clear later;
- present a coded object or ritual as familiar to the POV without narrating its full lore;
- reveal enemy competence before enemy motivation.

### Hostile-POV test

Before using a hostile viewpoint, answer:

> What becomes more interesting because the players experience this from inside the opposition?

If the answer is merely “they learn the villain's plan,” prefer another delivery method unless that revelation is itself the intended handoff.

## Five-check ratchet

The default five checks have different dramatic jobs.

### 1. Orientation

Teach the situation through action.

Good questions:

- Can they get their bearings?
- Can they reach the immediate objective?
- Can they recognize what is wrong?
- Can they respond to the first obvious danger?

Keep stakes meaningful but recoverable.

### 2. Pressure

Turn the situation against them.

Introduce:

- pursuit;
- time pressure;
- environmental danger;
- opposition;
- failing equipment;
- wounded ally;
- narrowing route.

The situation should now require commitment.

### 3. Complication

Change what success means.

Examples:

- another person needs saving;
- the route is compromised;
- the objective is not what they expected;
- someone recognizes them;
- the enemy arrives early;
- the apparent rescue is a trap;
- the safe option sacrifices something important.

This is usually the best place for a choice with a cost.

### 4. Crisis

Expose the true severity of the situation.

Examples:

- overwhelming enemy appears;
- escape route collapses;
- ritual reaches its final stage;
- ally turns;
- disaster becomes irreversible;
- the threat's scale becomes visible.

The cold open should now feel like it is racing toward a conclusion.

### 5. Payoff

Determine the state of the ending.

Ask something whose result visibly colors the final image:

- Does the warning get sent?
- Who escapes?
- What evidence survives?
- Does the enemy notice them?
- Do they recover the object?
- How badly are they injured?
- Do they understand what they saw?
- Does anyone else witness it?

The final check should matter even when the camera is about to leave.

## Consequence dimensions

A check result should usually change one or more of these.

### Condition

- injured;
- exhausted;
- poisoned;
- frightened;
- stable;
- trapped;
- separated.

### Time

- ahead of danger;
- barely on time;
- delayed;
- enemy gains ground;
- countdown advances.

### Information

- exact knowledge;
- partial clue;
- misleading fragment;
- identity noticed;
- evidence lost.

### Resources

- equipment intact;
- spell or charge consumed;
- vehicle damaged;
- supplies lost;
- weapon recovered.

### Collateral

- civilians safe;
- structure damaged;
- ally injured;
- witness lost;
- fire spreads.

### Exposure

- unseen;
- suspected;
- identified;
- tracked;
- alarm raised.

### Position

- favorable route;
- dangerous route;
- isolated;
- surrounded;
- high ground;
- escape path preserved.

### Relationship

- trust gained;
- resentment created;
- debt incurred;
- loyalty tested;
- betrayal suspected.

A strong failure consequence changes the state without merely saying “you fail.”

## Failure patterns

### Success with cost

The immediate action succeeds, but:

- it consumes time;
- equipment breaks;
- someone is hurt;
- evidence is left behind.

### Partial information

The character learns enough to create a useful question but misses the full answer.

### Larger response

The action works, but something larger notices or responds.

### Sacrifice

The character can preserve one priority at the expense of another.

### Worse position

The trajectory continues from a more dangerous state.

### Collateral consequence

The character succeeds personally while the surrounding situation deteriorates.

## Group participation with one character

The fiction contains one POV character; the **table** supplies collective decision-making.

Write checks so everyone can participate in resolution without implying several fictional bodies.

Useful prompts:

- ask the group which immediate approach the character takes;
- have players contribute observations before the roll;
- let the table decide which cost the character accepts;
- use the campaign's established group-check procedure;
- rotate who gives the final description or choice when appropriate.

Keep resolution fast. The cold open is a shared performance of one viewpoint, not a temporary multi-PC party.

## Ending shapes

### Revelation

The last successful observation changes what the players think is happening.

Use when information is the handoff.

### Escalation

The apparent problem is revealed as part of something larger.

Use when anticipation is the handoff.

### Escape

The POV survives, but the final image shows what followed them, what they lost, or what remains unresolved.

Use when relief should immediately turn into concern.

### Catastrophe

The scene reaches an irreversible event.

Use when showing the cost directly is more powerful than hearing about it later.

### Recognition

The POV or audience recognizes a person, symbol, place, object, or implication.

Cut quickly after recognition.

### Message

A warning, command, recording, sending stone, flare, magical signal, or other communication leaves the scene.

Whether anyone receives it may remain unresolved.

### Doorway

Something opens, arrives, awakens, emerges, lands, surfaces, or crosses a threshold.

Cut before explanation.

### Apparent death

Use only when uncertainty itself matters.

Do not narrate a confirmed outcome when the campaign intends the fate to remain genuinely unresolved.

## Hard-cut patterns

Keep the transition short.

Examples of structural shapes:

```text
Final image.
Beat.
CUT TO:
The main PCs in immediate contrast.
```

```text
A question is asked in the cold open.
CUT TO:
A main PC unknowingly doing something related to the answer.
```

```text
Catastrophic noise / darkness / impact.
CUT TO:
Quiet ordinary morning with the PCs.
```

```text
NPC sees the threat approaching.
CUT TO:
The PCs discussing an apparently unrelated problem.
```

Contrast is often stronger than explanation.
