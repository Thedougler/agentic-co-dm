# Rules and place types

This is a compact mechanical palette for D&D 5.5e (2024 / SRD 5.2.1). Look up
exact wording in the operational [SRD 5.2.1](https://www.dndbeyond.com/sources/dnd/srd-5-2-1)
and the public [Everything's a Dungeon tip page](https://www.dndbeyond.com/posts/1460-everything-is-a-dungeon)
when needed. Do not paste proprietary book text; use brief paraphrase and a
pointer.

## Mechanical palette

- **Difficult Terrain:** make slower movement a meaningful route cost, not a
  universal tax. Pair it with concealment, resources, elevation, noise, or a
  shortcut so players can choose.
- **Cover:** state which edges, objects, elevations, and lines of effect provide
  cover. Let players create, remove, or trade cover where the fiction supports
  it.
- **Search / Study / Influence / Utilize:** use the 2024 action vocabulary when
  it fits. Describe what is observable before a roll; a declared action resolves
  depth, access, control, or advantage rather than deciding whether the place
  contains a clue at all.
- **DC scale:** choose a DC that matches the task and fiction; a practical table
  shorthand is 5 very easy, 10 easy, 15 moderate, 20 hard, 25 very hard, 30
  nearly impossible. Modify the situation with tools, time, help, position,
  knowledge, or a cost instead of inflating DCs to negate a capability.
- **Fail with consequences:** on a failed check, preserve a required revelation
  or route and charge time, exposure, position, resources, trust, injury, or
  pressure. Offer a risky success when it is exciting and clear.
- **Hazard format:** `telegraph → trigger → save/check or choice → effect →
  counterplay → bypass → aftermath`. State area, duration, damage/condition or
  other impact, and how the hazard changes after it fires.
- **Rest and travel:** make shelter, distance, visibility, weather, and faction
  action matter. Do not make a nominally safe room safe by fiat.

## Spatial problems by tier

| Tier | Spatial problem to emphasize | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | readable local routes, terrain, cover, social boundaries, short clocks | giant scale that makes every choice abstract |
| 2 | layered access, vertical routes, environmental costs, rival claims | level-locking every interesting path |
| 3 | region-scale networks, mass movement, planar thresholds, durable faction change | pretending one door or DC can contain high mobility |
| 4 | reality, world, or multiversal consequences; scale of approach and escape | arbitrary immunity to high-level travel and agency |

Higher-tier parties can bypass more obstacles. Reward that capability with speed,
information, exposure, cost, or new consequences; do not silently declare flight,
teleportation, burrowing, divination, darkvision, stealth, social authority, or
mobility impossible. If a capability is limited, show the setting reason and what
it can still accomplish.

## Place-type adjusters

### Settlement

Design claims, services, neighborhoods, routines, laws, prices, rumors, status,
and relationships. A settlement is not a hostile dungeon with houses as rooms.
Give it public and private routes, competing obligations, people who move, and
consequences for violence, theft, rescue, or reputation. Use faction offers and
social affordances as much as physical obstacles. A quiet street may still be a
route, witness network, refuge, market, or pressure point.

### Wilderness region

Use weather, visibility, terrain, supplies, trails, landmarks, wildlife, local
knowledge, and changing conditions. Build a region graph of approaches and
return routes; give navigation clues and alternative costs. A wilderness node
should answer what can be gathered, crossed, observed, avoided, protected, or
changed. Flight can shorten travel while increasing exposure or losing ground
information; it should not be cancelled without a reason.

### Dungeon or ruin

Emphasize bounded topology, history visible in structure, inhabitants with
territory, pressure, loops, bypasses, retreats, and consequences. Distinguish a
ruin's durable geography from mutable occupants. For deep dungeon or megadungeon
graphs, hand off to `dungeon-design` rather than duplicating a campaign-scale
procedure here.

### Landmark or encounter site

Give one dominant promise, one strong approach question, a few significant
nodes, and a clear return state. A bridge, shrine, tower, grove, or battlefield
needs a relationship and a consequence, not a full dungeon map. Make the site
interesting without enemies: a route, clue, resource, bargain, ritual, view,
repair, or decision must remain.

### Planar or reality-warped place

Define one reliable rule of strangeness, one limit or price, and one way players
can test or learn the rule. Keep orientation possible through repeated cues,
landmarks, local customs, or a changing but legible topology. Surreal is not the
same as random: state what changes, when it changes, and what remains invariant.
Give characters a way to exploit, bargain with, resist, or redirect the rule.

## Audit

- Are mechanics brief enough to run without replacing the fiction?
- Is the opening sign visible before the commitment or roll?
- Are cover, terrain, movement, and action choices applied consistently?
- Does each required secret have multiple clue vectors?
- Does the place reward the party's actual capabilities?
- Could the party negotiate, explore, alter, or return even if enemies vanish?
