# Resolution cards

Card shapes follow Mike Pondsmith, *Scripting the Game* (R. Talsorian, 2020;
transcription at `RTG-Scripting-the-Game-v1.2-agent-readable (2).md` in the repo
root), paraphrased for 5e play. A Resolution is the **tag line**: a tiny
afterscene that ties up plot ends or reveals the possibility of a sequel —
the escape craft flying away, Rick and Renault walking into the fog. It
follows only a Climax and is the result of every beat before it. It can
overturn the Climax's apparent result when the overturn grows from
established fiction.

Pick the card the Climax's actual outcome produced, not the planned one.

Each card: **Shape** — what the afterscene is; **Build** — what the page must
contain beyond the table-ready anatomy; **Fair play** — how the card keeps
earned results standing.

## Happy Ending
- **Shape:** The party wins the day and resolves the situation: the city saved, the captive home, everyone riding into the sunset. Hopefully the most common Resolution.
- **Build:** Who benefits and how it shows; the reward, named (owner links, coin, favor, title, XP or milestone); the cost still visible in the scene; each PC's moment.
- **Fair play:** The joy is what the party's choices bought; losses stay lost.

## Villain Is Killed
- **Shape:** The villain is dead, the party gets its reward, and the world rearranges around the gap.
- **Build:** The body and what it carries (items, papers — owner links); the power vacuum and who moves into it; the reward and XP or milestone.
- **Fair play:** The death stands; any successor follows from factions already on the page.

## Villain Surrenders
- **Shape:** The party wins and the opposition gives up.
- **Build:** The terms offered; what the villain knows and will trade; the party's options (arrest, mercy, exchange, trial, execution, release) and each option's consequence.
- **Fair play:** The party sets the terms; the villain acts on established interests afterward.

## Villain Escapes
- **Shape:** The villain is beaten but not in custody — a secret bolt hole, or it was never them at all (a double, a simulacrum). They get clean away, with no trail to follow for now. A fine way to reuse a good villain; not every time.
- **Build:** The escape means, planted in an earlier beat (the hidden tunnel on the map, the ring of blinking, the body double seen at dusk); what the villain lost; what they will want next.
- **Fair play:** The escape uses means the fiction established; a party that closed that exit during the Climax closes it here.

## Heroes Captured
- **Shape:** The party lost badly, and killing them outright is not the interesting ending: they are captured and the session ends in the dungeon, the cell, the hold. The next session picks up from captivity.
- **Build:** Why the opposition wants them alive (ransom, interrogation, sacrifice at the new moon); where they are held; what was taken from them and where it is kept; the first crack in the captivity.
- **Fair play:** Capture follows a real defeat; captivity leaves choices, and the next session's Hook starts there.

## Heroes Escape
- **Shape:** The party is on the ropes after a Climax it fought well, and an interruption lets them get away — the earthquake, another force attacking the opposition, a door opening where there was none. Use it rarely and with care.
- **Build:** The interruption, drawn from something the session already established (a ticking clock, a third party, the catastrophe in the background); what the escape costs; what the opposition now holds.
- **Fair play:** The interruption was already in motion in the fiction; the losses stay.

## Ending Cliffhanger
- **Shape:** The party beat the enemy — and just as the celebration starts, a new danger arrives. The sequel is set up with the tension still on.
- **Build:** The concrete new threat and why the Climax's outcome caused or exposed it; what the players perceive; the one question it leaves.
- **Fair play:** Tonight's central question stays answered; the new danger opens the next question.

## Greater Threat
- **Shape:** Getting rid of the villain did not end the problem — a far greater threat stands behind them. The invaders were the advance fleet; the mastermind was a flunky.
- **Build:** The greater threat (owner page); the evidence that exposes it, drawn from what the Climax uncovered; what it means for the party's next choices.
- **Fair play:** The victory still counts; the greater threat follows from established fiction.
