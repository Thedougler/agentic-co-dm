# Villain front

A front is an active plan the players can see, interrupt, redirect, or exploit.
It is not a script for the villain to win.

## Core fields

- **Public face / private identity:** What the world sees; what is withheld.
- **Want:** Concrete outcome sought now.
- **Fear or need:** What makes the want urgent or distorts judgment.
- **Leverage / means:** People, resources, secrets, authority, or force.
- **Limit:** A boundary, weakness, dependency, oath, or scarce resource.
- **Contradiction:** The pressure that can split the villain’s choices.
- **Allies and liabilities:** Who helps; who can defect or be protected.
- **Tell:** A repeatable sign of presence or strain.
- **Plan clock:** Steps, timing, visible threshold, and what resets or accelerates it.
- **Escalation threshold:** The player action that causes a harder response.
- **Stop condition:** Evidence, loss, bargain, or moral line that could end it.

## Plan step fields

For each step record: **trigger, objective, location or target, resource spent,
visible sign, consequence if unopposed, player interruption, fallback, and
villain reaction**. A fallback changes the situation; it is not “the villain
wins anyway.”

## Escalation sequence

Use only the steps pressure earns; skip or reorder them when play does.

1. **Rumor:** People, symbols, odd absences, or disputed accounts point toward a pressure.
2. **Trace:** A resource, agent, message, damaged site, or pattern makes it actionable.
3. **Minor move:** The villain tests defenses, buys time, recruits, steals, or frames someone.
4. **Costly response:** The party’s intervention forces a visible sacrifice or counter-move.
5. **Public consequence:** A faction, place, ally, or resource is changed in a way the table can act on.
6. **Confrontation:** The villain’s objective and the players’ objective collide with several outcomes.

## Reaction loop

After each meaningful player choice: (1) state what the villain knows, including
uncertainty; (2) choose the smallest response that protects the plan; (3) spend a
resource or expose a tell; (4) advance, delay, or alter the clock; (5) offer the
players a new interruption point. Do not make the villain omniscient or repair
all consequences off-screen.

## Presence forms

Show the villain before a final confrontation through a consequence, rumor,
agent, altered environment, intercepted message, public policy, survivor, gift,
warning, or brief encounter. Each appearance should reveal want, means, limit,
or contradiction and give players a way to act.

## Betrayal rules

Use betrayal when incentives and prior behavior support it, not merely for a
surprise. Seed at least two readable signs and one plausible alternative. Make
the betrayal interruptible; let players test loyalty, change the deal, expose
the pressure, or prepare. A trusted NPC may remain loyal, partially defect,
trade information, or leave without becoming evil. Do not make “everyone was a
traitor” the campaign’s only twist.

## Multiple conclusions and redemption

Write at least three live endings, such as defeat, escape, compromise, exposure,
alliance, sacrifice, or negotiated surrender. For redemption, specify conditions
that play can satisfy: acknowledged harm, restitution, a costly refusal, and a
credible future constraint. The villain can reject redemption. No conclusion
should depend on a cutscene or a single required check.

## Villain audit

- Can players state the villain’s want from evidence in play?
- Are plan steps visible, interruptible, and able to fail forward?
- What does the villain know, and what do they merely fear?
- Is there a limit, contradiction, stop condition, and resource economy?
- What changes if players ignore the front?
- Can allies, victims, and factions make independent choices?
- Does the confrontation have objectives beyond reducing hit points?
- Are defeat, escape, compromise, exposure, and redemption all conditional?
