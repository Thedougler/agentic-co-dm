# Person first looks

Verbatim table prose from named DMs. Copy the move, not the setting or wording.
Attribution stays with the line. These are analysis examples, not player-facing
sentences to echo.

Read this file when the job is an NPC, a person the party can talk to, a first
meeting, a Face, an innkeeper, a bartender, or anyone already doing something
when the door opens. Check [examples.md](examples.md), [voice.md](voice.md),
[boundary.md](boundary.md), and [surfaces.md](surfaces.md) on every pass. Steal
one object or creature move — a hand-fact, a body already in motion.

When the job is the room they stand in, start in [places.md](places.md). When the job is a monster, a fight turn, or identical enemy bodies, start in [experts.md](experts.md). When the job is an object in their hands, start in [humans.md](humans.md).

## First look

Give the person a reusable Face, their established current objective, and an
action or physical cue. Seat them in the scene only when the surrounding place
is part of the requested surface — behind the bar, at the kitchen table,
coming through the far door. Stop on a playable change: they look up, the glass
stops, the hand stays on the map. Do not invent a hidden motive to fill the
objective; dialogue gets its own want-now, tactic, utterance, and answerable
turn contract in [surfaces.md](surfaces.md).

Matt Mercer (Polygon, with Deborah Ann Woll, 2026) — two or three visuals, how they hold themselves, one smell, already moving:

> You peer upon a somewhat hunched, scrappy-looking wanderer in earthtone leathers and a thick, red scarf, their wide eyes darting around with their scanning head movements, a grin of yellowed teeth pulled open beneath a scruffy, sunburned face. As they make note of your troupe, they approach with unexpected haste, a smell of lantern oil and harsh whiskey wafting from them.

Mercer’s rule for that line: two to three distinct visual attributes, how they hold themselves, and a scent or the feeling their presence evokes. Ask what they were doing before the players arrived.

Deborah Ann Woll (same interview) — visual, auditory, hook. Length is the point:

> I generally like to introduce characters with a strong visual, a strong auditory, and a strong hook or action. Visual: What is their most striking physical feature? Auditory: a vocal tic or placement. Hook: what are they doing or representing that makes them interesting to the party?

Her worked Face from *Tales from Woodcreek*: Miss Vickers as spectacles, a clipped voice, and the sharp crack of a yardstick against a desk. Woll’s bound: a few strong details the table can latch onto will outperform a long explanation.

Matt Colville (@mattcolville, 2019) — third person is a finished description. No accent required:

> You don't have to act out anything, just describe what they do and say and how they say it. "The dwarf describes the quest he's on, he seems nervous." Some of the best DMs I've played with rarely spoke "in character."

Official sample play, 2024 *Player’s Handbook* — room first, then people placed, then one of them acts:

> This tavern might have been finely appointed once, but it has grown shoddy with the passage of years. A blazing fire in the hearth barely gives any warmth to the room. You see a man behind the counter, methodically cleaning glasses. Three older women huddle together at one table. And a handsome young man sits by himself at a corner table, sipping a glass of wine. He looks up as you enter, but the other people ignore you. “Welcome to Barovia,” he says.

RPG Thoughts, “Describing the Scene” — the person arrives as sound from the next room, then a body:

> You hear some shuffling steps from the next room and an old man enters. What do you do?

Same talk, blacksmith as heat and a hammer already working:

> As you step in you’re blasted with a blaze of heat from the open furnace. Sweat starts to pour against your brow. You hear the banging and clanging of the blacksmith’s hammer.

## Face

One fact the table can point at later. Repeat it. Do not rewrite the portrait.

u/Navezof, *100 Memorable NPC Appearances* (Dndspeak, 2023) — the Face is what others notice on contact. Look, dress, speech, walk, or the feeling of them. Striking enough to remember without the name:

> They are horribly burnt all over their body and face, by fire or acid. It can be hard to look at them.

> They have on their face and body the stigmata of terrible fights, broken features, scars from chin to brow, a missing nose or ear.

> They have shoulders as wide as they are tall and arms as big as a tree trunk. Even their neck is oversized. It's a miracle their outfit is not breaking under the pressure.

> They have exceptionally long and narrow limbs, reminiscent of a huge spider.

r/DMAcademy — keep the same three words on every visit:

> The Kings advisor is sickly looking, pallid, and hunched.

Matthew Mercer, short on how a blacksmith appears — town first, then the Face, then the voice follows the face:

> it's a rugged mining town. This blacksmith, for some reason, it speaks to me as like a large handlebar mustache. And then from thinking of him for a minute, I visualize him in my head and I see like, you know, big, bushy, red eyebrows, maybe like a bald head that he keeps dirt smeared, looks really scary, but he's got like a soft heart.

Taking20 / Rune Forge — the generic seller is not a first look. Give the body a verb while they talk:

> if you're meeting with the blacksmith sometimes we'll actually be like he's sharpening a knife you know as he's talking to you or the person is counting gold out

## They are already doing something

The job title waits. The cake, the glasses, the yardstick are the description.

u/Keldr, r/DMAcademy, “Describing NPCs' Actions Evolves the World”:

> When the party first meets the blacksmith, he is in his house instead of at work. They knock on his door and find that he is in the middle of preparing a cake for someone’s birthday.

Same thread — zoom out when the talk is done by describing the body leaving:

> You’ve made all the arguments you have to the queen, but she furiously stalks back to her throne and waves you off without looking at you.

Dndspeak, *100 Interesting Tavernkeepers* — occupation, habit, one live worry:

> Davis Owensby - A retired farmer who converted his old barn into a tavern. This tavern keeper brews all his own beers with hops that he grows. He will sit and talk about farming for hours, if you let him. He is worried about his cows going missing, and suspects the ogres in the hills are to blame.

James Haeck — a person already emptied out. No anatomy dump. The mantra is the Face:

> The dwarf takes no notice of your presence. He simply stares, glassy-eyed, into the distance. He repeats a quiet mantra in a barely perceptible whisper, endlessly. "I'll make it back to her. I'll make it back to her. I'll make it back to her."

Matthew Mercer, Critical Role C1E1 — drunk champion as one body on the bar, already out:

> She points towards the very edge of the bar and you see one older grizzled dwarf, long gray hair pulled into straight braids in the back, big bushy beard that’s all shoved up into his face because he’s asleep, drunk as a skunk, on the edge of the bar.

## What to copy

- Seat them when the surrounding place matters — behind the counter, at the far table, in the house instead of the forge, edge of the bar.
- One Face a player could point at next session — red scarf and yellowed teeth, spectacles and a yardstick, pallid and hunched, burnt, sharpening a knife. The Face is the anchor. Costume inventory is texture. Do not spend five visual facts on a first meeting, and do not give eyes, ring, and scarf each their own sentence.
- What they are doing and what they are trying to do now — cleaning glasses, cracking the yardstick, baking a cake, approaching with unexpected haste. Use only an established objective; fold the Face onto that verb: an older woman too busy to chat, fancy gold ring turning on her finger while she wipes the same glass.
- One non-sight sense when it has a source — lantern oil and harsh whiskey, clipped voice, hammer on steel.
- Repeat the same Face words on the return visit.
- Third person is allowed. “He seems nervous” is a finished line.
- Stop. The next line is theirs, or the first thing the NPC says.
- A return visit repeats the same Face words and names only what has changed.
