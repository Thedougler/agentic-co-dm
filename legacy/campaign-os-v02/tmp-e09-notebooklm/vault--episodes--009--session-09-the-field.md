## I. What the Island Keeps

The reef took the hull on its second impact. Orrek went into the water at the same moment Pol did, but the distance between them when they found bottom was enough that he spent the first minutes not knowing if his companion had made it. The water was warm and dark and heavy with wreckage, a broken spar turning in the current, a sail billowing slow and pale as a jellyfish down below where the light could not reach. He kicked for the surface. His lungs said no and his legs said yes and he trusted his legs.

He came up gasping, saltwater burning his sinuses, and there was shore, thirty feet of narrow sand between the surf and the first growth of salt-scrub, and Pol was already there, forearms on his knees, spitting blood from a split lip. The hull had caught him on the way out, a graze across the cheekbone. They looked at each other across the surf and Orrek felt something that was not relief exactly, but close to it.

The beach was too quiet.

The water ran and the surf hissed and the thorn-scrub rustled in its own small wind. But the sounds came at the wrong intervals, like a room where someone has just stopped talking, the sound of a silence that replaced something. They pulled themselves above the tide line and sat breathing, and the quiet pressed in from both sides.

The island looked like every other island in the Teeth, and his eye kept refusing to hold on it. Not the plants or the stone or the water pooling in the flat places (those were right enough, a jungle island like a dozen others). But the geometry sat at an angle his skull would not settle into. Every shadow fell at the right size and in the right place, except for something at the edge of the treeline, where the salt-scrub thickened before giving way to the canopy. Something that Orrek's eye skated past three times before his body noticed it first.

The shapes at the treeline were watching.

Whatever had gathered at the edge of the trees was taking its time, which was worse than if they had come directly. They were large, and there were more than one kind. The eyes of something enormous and pale-mottled watched from the shadows, still, calm, patient in a way that did not feel like an animal's patience at all. He had heard, before boarding the fleet at [[karath|Karath]], that [[aruhe|Aruhe]] was a cursed place. He had heard it the way soldiers hear things they do not believe because they have not been to where the thing is true.

Pol said, in a low voice, that they needed fresh water. He said it in the tone of a man trying to impose normal military logic on something that would not respond to normal logic. A spring ran somewhere close. The sound of it came from almost behind them, barely audible over the surf.

The island recognized them. Not by sight, not by the purple of their caste markings or the cut of their guards' armor still dragging wet around them. By scent. The creatures at the treeline had gathered before either of them made a sound, and the peripheral shapes were already in position along the left of the beach before Pol had finished his sentence. None of that had been a response. They had arrived in something that was already underway.

Pol moved toward the spring.

The treeline erupted.

It came out of the shadows in three shapes at once and there was no way to read which one to run from first. The first was massive and pale, a bulk that crushed the salt-scrub flat as it pushed through. The second was smaller and faster and came wide to cut off the gap between Pol and the beach. Orrek ran. He ran toward Pol and not away from the creatures, and he would think about that afterward for the rest of his life, which lasted another three hours and seventeen minutes.

He was close. Close enough that the displacement of air from the pale creature's second movement put sand in his mouth, and he heard Pol's voice make one sound, not a word, just a sound, and then he heard the sound it stopped making. He came up on one knee and the beach was behind him and the creatures were between him and the treeline and nothing remained in the space where Pol had been except a gouge in the sand and a stillness that settled over it like an answer.

The creatures did not come for him immediately. They settled back. The pale one withdrew to the shadow of the first tree and put one great foreleg down and watched him from there, with the patience again, the unrushed waiting. Along the shoreline to his left, near the water but not in it, others held a line. They were not hunting. They were managing. The geometry of the island belonged to them, and he was learning it for the first time.

He saw the sail.

A shape of canvas out past the reef gap, far, upwind, moving south on the main channel. He moved toward it, toward the water, toward the surf line, toward the thing the creatures were ranging to prevent him from reaching. He got partway. He got close enough that the surf was below his knees when the pale creature stood and halved the distance between them in one motion, and he stopped.

They watched each other.

The sail moved on down the channel and did not slow. He watched until the white above its hull was a suggestion and then until nothing remained. He stood in the surf with the water moving around his legs, not cold, and the creatures watching, and the island behind him doing what it always did: waiting.

He thought about the legend, the one they passed around in barracks about the previous landing party, the one nobody took seriously because they had not been to where the thing was true. The story treated the island as though it hated [[grung|Grung]]. He understood now that it did not. The island had no opinion about him at all. Hungry the way stone is heavy, without volition, without anything behind the eyes that would bargain or appease. The pale creature watched him from the treeline with the calm of something that had never once been surprised by what walked onto this beach, and Orrek watched it back, and neither of them moved.

They found him at the treeline three hours after that, where he had gone when the beach became too exposed to hold and he had run the only calculation left: whether being close to the forest was better or worse than standing in the open. He had known the answer when he did it. He did it anyway, because the other option was standing in the surf watching the sun move, and there was only so long he could stand there watching the sun move.

The island kept him. The beach emptied. The waves moved the wreckage back and forth along the shingle, and one board that had once been part of a hull turned in the surf and did not come to rest, knocking soft against the sand, back and forth, until the tide changed.

The women sheltering in the wrecked [[vethka|Vethka's]] hull heard none of it from where they watched the reef gap. They heard the surf and the birds and each other's breathing, and beyond that the things moving in the upper brush. They did not go north toward the treeline. They had not gone north since the first night.

## II. South Out of La Vasca

The [[uncertainty|Uncertainty]] came off the [[calveno-la-vasca|La Vasca]] cradle ahead of Cobb's own estimate, which put the crew into the [[central-strait|Central Strait]] just ahead of the season's first real southerly. The festival left its marks on [[calveno|Calveno]] that the shipyard's scaffolding could not cover: the crater-side streets were still damp from the cleanup, and someone had chalked a rough outline of Otar on the wall of the Waveservant Shrine until the shrine keeper washed it off. They left it all behind in an afternoon. By evening the headland of Calveno was a silhouette and by full dark nothing remained.

The southerly filled the sails on the second day out, and Delmar had the watch when something large and white appeared above the morning horizon aft, not a cloud, moving with deliberate direction. [[master-kyzil|Kyzil]] came on without hailing. He dropped out of the sun like something thrown and folded his wings at the stern rail, one foot on the taffrail and one on nothing at all, perfectly balanced in a wind that was kicking up whitecaps, and looked past Delmar to where Crissdalynn was working lines at the mast.

He had spent two weeks in Calveno saying nothing to her about the dreidel. He said it now.

He said it quietly, not privately (there were other people on deck), but in a way that treated the information as already known to the right people and requiring only the correction of any misapprehension about its sensitivity. He told her to keep it safe. He told her not to tell anyone she had it. He said *anyone*, and he said it a second time when she asked who specifically, and the second time landed as a correction.

She asked him why.

He looked at her for a moment and then past her, out over the water, with the same expression he brought to difficult questions in the spar, composing his answer out of the parts of the truth he had decided to share. He said there were people who wanted it. He said the dreidel was old enough that people who should have ignored it had not. He said [[high-eyrie|the High Eyrie's]] masters were not, in this instance, necessarily the safe choice.

He named no one. Not the Countless. Not Vantyrus. He named nothing at all.

He folded his wings once, adjusted his hood (right over left, a tell anyone who had watched him long enough might catch) and said the hardest thing a sentinel ever learned was the difference between what the order knew and what the order ought to know. Then he leaped from the taffrail into the wind and was a shape dwindling north before the sentence finished its echo.

The crew had what they needed: someone hunted the dreidel, and their own navigator's mentor did not trust his own order with it. Whatever he had decided not to name was the part he had not worked out himself. The shape of the threat lived in what he had not said.

---

Three days south of Calveno they ran into the first piece, a single oar end with the blade painted in the pattern common to working vessels out of La Vasca's fishing quarter, and then more: boards, a broken spar, a waterlogged sack of trade goods bumping upright in the swell. Then a Human voice. Then three of them, on debris, not sinking, not floating, holding on.

They were Calveno, a merchant's husband and wife and their nephew who had been working passage south. The husband was in bad shape, salt sores on his hands and a head wound he could not account for beyond *the hull went*. They had been in the water a day. They did not know where they were or how far they had drifted or in what direction. They knew the storm. They knew how fast it had come up and how little time there had been and how the ship had been there one moment and not there the next. They did not know about any island. They knew the storm had come from the east and driven them west before it broke, and they knew they had been heading south when it caught them.

From a storm out of the east driving a fleet west, from the debris field running the wrong direction for Karath water, from the simple navigation of where a current could carry a wreck in two days of drifting, the calculation resolved. West of Karath, east of [[amberfall|Amberfall]], in the reef approach off [[verdant-teeth|the Verdant Teeth]]: the island nobody landed on, the island the Grung would not approach, where the only way through the reef was one narrow gap.

Four hundred and eighty-seven people had left Calveno in the Grung fleet. Two hundred and ninety reached Karath. The rest existed only as storm arithmetic and a debris field in the Central Strait that carried the cargo markings of Calveno working families.

The Calveno citizens had not stopped looking for their people. The Uncertainty had not stopped being their ship.

---

The Crown vessel was a Knight Squadron hull, running clean pennants and inspection colours, and it crossed their bow in the main channel on the morning of the fifth day with enough deliberation that the Uncertainty's choice was to heave to or take fire. They hove to. The gig that came across was military, six oarsmen pulling in unison, and the two figures in the sternsheets did not match. The taller one sat with the deliberate bearing of a man who had spent considerable time deciding what his posture communicated. The other one sat like a weapon someone had folded into a coat.

[[corvin-knighton|Corvin Knighton]] came aboard first, because that was what the encounter called for, a family's face on a Crown mandate. [[aleksander-malone|Aleksander Malone]] came behind him, scanning the deck with the efficient, evaluating attention of a man reading a room for who would confess and who would fight first.

Corvin was looking at the hull.

He did not make it plain. He looked at the crew, looked at the rigging, made the conventional gestures of a man going through inspection forms. But his eye had gone to the bow shape on arrival and had not entirely left it. The Uncertainty was blue-black, her pennants were Calveno, her registry number was a Calveno number, and nothing about her announced Crown cutter to anyone who did not know what a Crown cutter's hull looked like from an inspection gig at sixty yards. Corvin knew. He had grown up around that hull line. He said nothing about it.

The inspection's stated purpose was intelligence on [[shepherd-grigori|Shepherd Grigori]], the man who had traveled south out of [[crown-islands|the Crown Islands]] healing the sick and gathering followers among noble families, working his way by report into [[midchain|the Midchain]] shipping lanes. Malone's version of the question was more direct: had the Uncertainty's crew encountered him, and had they received any account of his movements more recent than the last port? He asked the last one twice, and he watched the silence after it, watching for the pause that meant thought, the pause that meant someone deciding how much of what they knew they wanted to share with an inquisitor in a Crown coat.

Corvin leaned against the stern rail with his hand near his blade and said nothing. He was watching Malone more than he was watching the crew. His presence kept Malone's attention oriented where it belonged, kept the shape of the thing at an inspection and not whatever the other shape would be if it turned. He did not look again at the hull.

Malone received the understanding that the Uncertainty had been in dry dock in La Vasca for a week and held information about Calveno's affairs and no information about anyone's movements south. He received it once, which was all a straight answer needed. If anyone on deck had known where Grigori was, or said anything that suggested sympathy with his work or willingness to shield him, the thing behind Malone's eyes made clear what would follow. Nobody said anything that invited consequence. The gig came back across and the Crown hull fell off downwind.

Corvin's uncle did not know his son was dead. Corvin knew and had chosen to keep that knowledge private, because a living heir close to the succession was one position and a grieving heir was another, and Corvin had spent his whole life distinguishing between positions. [[capn-gorgeous|Cap'n Gorgeous]] had been vain and not particularly gifted, and Corvin had loved him the way you love someone you know you will outlast, with a complicated tenderness that does not interfere with what comes after. He had not been looking for the killers.

He had noticed the hull.

He would not say that aloud, either.

---

[[knifes-wake|The Knife's Wake]] came up on their port quarter in the dead middle of the night watch, running dark with her gunports closed until the distance was fifty yards. The false trade pennant went down as the real one went up, narrow and black, sharp as the name. Her gunports opened along the rail like teeth, boarding hooks appeared over the bulwark, and she was already committed to the angle, close enough that pulling off would cost her the boarding advantage she had closed for.

The Uncertainty's wake was darker than the water around it, blue-black on black, and the helmsman had eleven minutes of warning between the first hail from the lookout and the range where it mattered. Eleven minutes was enough. The crew that had come aboard at Calveno to fight the Otar mess stood on deck now, the guns loaded, and the Uncertainty's hull sat at exactly the geometry her builders had drawn: the range where every gun of hers bore and a Tier 1 pirate cutter could not survive the exchange.

The Knife's Wake was competent enough to read the math. She had come for a working ship that did not expect her and had found something else. Whether she fought or ran was her captain's calculation to make, with the seconds the range and the wind left him to make it in.

The calculation had a wrinkle the crew of the Uncertainty could not see from the rail. The Knife's Wake had taken a retainer in Calveno, a third paid up front by a factor whose name her captain had not asked for, because the fee was good and the asking was the kind that made fees smaller. The terms were simple: any vessel out of La Vasca, take what you liked, but the hold and the cabins got searched before anything burned, and what the factor wanted brought back whole was a small old thing that spins. Her captain could not have named the thing or the man who wanted it. He knew that the fee was three times what the job should cost, and that men who paid three times the rate for a thing that small were men whose business you did not stay curious about. If the boarding went well, his crew would be in the cabins turning out sea chests while the cargo sat unwatched on the deck, and anyone paying attention would notice the plunder was not what the pirates had come for.

## III. Sparhold

The bell was already ringing when the Uncertainty's hull cleared [[sparhold|Sparhold's]] harbor mouth. The tolls came slow and evenly spaced from inside the walls, not a watch bell and not an alarm but something ceremonial and definitive that the dock crew heard without appearing to hear, the practiced not-hearing of people who already knew what the bell meant and had opinions about it they were keeping to themselves.

A runner came down from the watch hall before the crew had cleated their lines. He posted a chalked notice on the market slate at the end of the dock, facing ships coming in. It said [[house-renzetti|House Renzetti]] had rung the challenge bell against [[house-kailani|House Kailani]], that each house had named a champion, and that the duel would take place in the betting ring outside the watch hall. Time of duel: the third morning bell.

Sparhold's harbor had the density of every fortress-market on the strip (too many hulls for the dock space, crates and coils everywhere, the smell of spar-timber from the yards behind the second wall) but it had organized itself around the notice on the slate the way communities organize around something they cannot change. Everyone watched it sideways. Nobody mentioned it directly.

---

Witnesses ringed the betting ring when the challenge ran, which meant the whole town was there or near enough. House Renzetti fielded [[nico-renzetti|Nico Renzetti]] as their champion, which was legible from halfway across the ring. He stood straight and still and wore the expression of a man who had accepted an argument he had lost. He had not wanted this. The argument had happened in private, as family arguments do, and its outcome was him in the ring with a blade on his hip. He was competent, that was clear too, but competent as a trained fighter and not an eager one, and in a fight to the death that difference showed in the third minute.

[[rangi-kailani|Rangi Kailani]] was the other kind. House Kailani's champion had the easy economy of movement that came from being in pressure before and learning what relaxed felt like by coming out the other side of what tense felt like. He watched Nico across the ring without the studied blankness of someone preparing himself and without the opposite performance of false ease. He was not performing. He was just there.

What happened between the ringing of the starting bell and the ending of the fight was whatever the crew of the Uncertainty had shaped beforehand, by word, by action, or by conspicuous inaction. The ring remembered. The house that came out of it remembered better.

---

The ledger surfaced on the third morning, when a crew argument between two of [[petro-amaru|Petro Amaru's]] timber men got loud enough in the street outside the chandlery to attract the passive audience that forms around any public fight in a small town. One man threw the ledger down. The other snatched it back. The throw-down and the snatch-back happened too fast for either of them to prevent whoever was nearby from getting a look at the pages that opened when it hit the stone.

Crissdalynn caught a glimpse of the open pages before the second man pulled the ledger back. What she saw made immediate sense: the cut-line marked in the ledger did not match the coast she had been studying since they docked. She knew the coast by sight, the way she knew every coast, and the ledger's line ran inland by a gap too wide for rounding error, too consistent for two different surveyors measuring two different things. Someone had paid a surveyor to measure the wrong thing.

Jean-Claude knew the Human architecture of exploitation when he saw it. A low-caste Grung defector spending her bribe money to buy freedom for kin still enslaved under the clans pulled him toward the harder question: what happened when the story came out. [[ussa|Ussa]] already knew what happened if the bribe came to light. She had weighed it against what the money bought and kept taking it. That calculation deserved a hearing before anyone else decided what to do with it.

---

The duel decided who ran Sparhold when the crew needed an answer. The Green Cut decided whether Sparhold would give it. Whichever family won the ring now held the question of the timber yards, and the timber yards held the question of the raids, and the raids held the question of the people still not accounted for: the hundred and forty-nine who had not reached Karath and the dozens unaccounted for in the debris field and the women sheltering in a wrecked hull on Aruhe's beach watching the reef gap for a sail.

Ussa knew Karath's patrol patterns. She had been a scout. She knew the channel between Aruhe and Karath better than any pilot working the strip, because she had run it at night in both directions and knew which rocks the patrol boats avoided and why. Getting to Aruhe without a Grung patrol spotting the approach required exactly the knowledge she carried, in her position, for her own reasons.

The morning after the duel, someone in Sparhold would know whether she would help.

## IV. Kalowe

**The Middle**

Five days southwest of Calveno put them in the open [[midchain|Midchain]], water in every direction and the channel markers on the chart no more solid than the confidence of whoever had drawn them. On the third day a sail appeared on the southern horizon and held station, neither closing nor falling away, just waiting.

The tribute mark was on the Uncertainty's hull. The pennant of a ship out of Calveno with Waveservant Shrine record flew beside it. The red hull on the southern horizon, the one the dock sailors called the Dead Lady in the tone people use for things they want to believe are rumour, waited to see if it needed to do anything. It did not come closer. It waited long enough that the choice was clear, and then, satisfied or choosing to appear so, it fell off to the east and vanished.

The Uncertainty made [[kalowe|Kalowe]] on the fifth evening, in the last of the light, with the reef gap lit by the Waveservant Shrine's lamp on the causeway.

---

**The Docks**

Kalowe knew Delmar's face before the mooring lines were across. Not everyone, but the dock labor that had worked through Calveno, the chandlery hands who had seen the crew through La Vasca victualing, and one [[chain-council|Chain Council]] member's man who had been watching the reef gap since noon when the causeway lookout sent word south that the Uncertainty's silhouette was coming up from the strait.

The Chain Council's Denial front had four segments and none of them filled. The easiest way to fill the first was to identify early the man the Council needed to blame, and [[admiral-fisk|Delmar Fisk]] (absent, findable, already on record as the fleet's admiral, already confirmed by his own confession as someone who knew the Council's sponsorship) was the easiest blame in the Midchain. The Council member's man said nothing about any of this. He introduced himself as a courier handling portside matters, which was true in the way true things can be true without being honest, and said the Council would welcome a private meeting at their convenience. He said *their* convenience in the tone of a man who meant the Council's.

---

**The Closed Session**

The Chain Council met in the fort that had belonged to the man they still called the Grask, the one their dock bosses had killed together two centuries back because none of them trusted the others enough to try it alone. The fort's walls were the same stone. The fear was the same shape.

The Council's closed-session position was that Admiral Fisk had acted on bad intelligence. The intelligence had been bad because someone had provided it in bad faith. That someone was not, in the Council's official account, any of the twelve signatories who had voted to commission the fleet, but a contractor who had represented the commission with terms that proved to be the terms of a [[tessarine-concordat|Tessarine]] play the Council had been too credulous to recognize. The contractor could not answer for it, because he no longer existed in a form that could contradict anything.

[[perrin-black-jaw|Perrin's]] name did not come up in the session. His community's debts did, not directly, but the Tessarine Containment front moved like weather. The pressure came from every direction at once, the steady reminder that someone had extended credit, that calling it remained an option, and that the people whose names sat on the debt instruments understood none of what that meant.

The evidence could surface. The scapegoat story could harden into the Midchain's permanent version of events, which would make it the only version most people ever heard. All three paths ran through the room. The Council's preferred one ran through Delmar Fisk.

---

**Anger on the Docks**

A delegation from the coastal settlements waited outside the fort by the time the session ended. Nobody had told them there was a session. They had come to Kalowe because Kalowe was where the Council met and where money moved and where anyone who had opinions about what the Council had spent on the Pearl commission and what the Grung raids had cost the same communities in the same period could say those things to someone who ought to hear them.

Jean-Claude had been watching the docks since they arrived. The information traveling on the Kalowe docks that afternoon was fresh: a coastal fishing crew in from the Verdant Teeth waters had seen, on the way up, debris on the reef approach off a small island east of Karath. Wrecked hulls, or what remained of them, broken on the reef and dragged to the beach by the tide. The cargo markings, what they could read at the distance they had been willing to approach, carried Calveno fishing marks.

On the beach, one of the fishers said, there had been smoke.

## V. Fathomrush

**The Compass**

The eastern crossing started normal and went wrong at the compass. The drift was gradual, the kind that reveals itself only when you check your bearing against a star that should be in one place and is slightly in another, and then check again to make sure the star has not moved. The stars had not moved. The compass had drifted two degrees, then four, then eight, in the direction of [[the-drowned-maw|The Drowned Maw's]] approaches.

The eastern water at the edge of the charts had its own relationship with instruments. Old pilots marked it on the sailing directions they passed hand to hand and never filed with any authority: *past the [[thoon-adar|Thoon Adar]] gap the compass loses interest and the current makes its own decisions.* The current was doing something under the surface that had nothing to do with the wind or the moon's pull, something that moved in the direction of the deepest part of the channel.

Perrin felt it before the compass showed it. Not in any way he could name, because naming it would mean naming his patron, and his patron required its own conversation. But the water to the east felt like something paying attention inside it, the way the Maw had felt when he made his unwitting bargain in [[vestra|the Vestra's]] sinking, and what paid attention in the Maw and what stirred in the eastern deeps were not the same thing, but they recognized each other across the distance, the way old wounds know when the weather changes.

The crossing was fine. The compass corrected when they bore north. Nothing came up from the water. But the knowledge that something pressed along the Maw-lanes against whatever anchored his pact sat in him for the rest of the leg, quiet, uncalled, waiting.

---

**The Taken Hull**

The second leg of the crossing brought them to a hull drifting empty in the Maw-lanes, fresh enough that the rigging still hung straight and someone had dogged the cargo hatch but not locked it. Everything on deck sat where a working crew would leave it, every line coiled and every hatch seated as though the crew had stepped below and never come back up. The log in its cradle in the helm alcove ended mid-entry in a clear hand that stopped at the end of a sentence that did not finish.

*[[taken-whole|Taken Whole]]* was the rumour that circulated in every dock tavern from Kalowe east, that something in the outer Maw-lanes took ships whole, crew and all, leaving the hulls drifting as proof. The proof was here. The choice of what to do about it lay here too: salvage, trap, or warning. Possibly all three. The log's last line was a navigation note with a bearing.

---

**Arrival**

[[fathomrush|Fathomrush]] announced itself by its skyline: riveted iron scaffold towers on both headlands, some climbing and others half-finished and rusting. One on the Voyvode side with three levels standing and the upper two abandoned where the funding had stopped mid-frame, another skeleton added to a skyline that already tallied every venture that had failed here. The memorial wall stood between the two piers at the waterline, plain wood, no company crest on it, claimed by neither company. New names had appeared since anyone in the crew's circuit had last been through. The most recent was still pale where the raw wood showed through the chisel strokes. Maybe a week old.

The Sunkline and Voyvode recruiters were at it in the dockside tavern when the crew walked past, not yet loud but with the fixed expressions of two people who have been having the same argument long enough that voices are no longer required. The argument broke into a real brawl before the Uncertainty's crew had been in port an hour, the sort that started from a recruiting pitch and escalated through accumulated grudge and company loyalty until furniture broke. [[catarina-davirelli|Catarina's]] whole problem with this town was that both sides had real data and both sides guarded it, and the brawl was a miniature of the larger rivalry: one venture honest and one fraudulent, running out the clock toward whichever cracked first.

[[branimir-karsovine|Branimir Karsovine]] was on the Voyvode dock when the fight ended, watching from his gate with the expression of a man who had already decided he would not intervene. He had sent someone down to the trial-dive site that morning. He knew the new name on the memorial wall. He did not appear to have strong feelings about the brawl, only about whether his rig would hold pressure at depth tomorrow, and whether the crew of the Uncertainty had any real reason to be in Fathomrush or were passing through.

---

**Two Ledgers, One Certificate**

Karsovine's trial notes were the real account, incomplete, annotated in the margins with specific failures and corrections and the names of three divers who had not come back up and what the rig telemetry said about why. The notes served understanding, which was what made them worth having to anyone who intended to do the thing and not merely appear to have done it. Getting them required something he could recognize as a reason, because the data was the only real thing he had and he did not hand real things to people who had not earned the conversation.

Sunkline's forged mid-shelf trial certificate sat behind a loose plank in the Last Berth's floor, in a document case that [[petera-ngata|Petera Ngata]] had declined to look at since the man who left it asked her to store it. She was not curious about it and she was not going to look. The loss of that certificate would eventually cost [[adriana-tarquine|Adriana Tarquine]] the company she had rebuilt the same pressure valve four times to protect, and Adriana Tarquine knew the valve's problem was the design, not the alloy, and had known it for two rebuilds, and had rebuilt it anyway, because the design was hers.

The eastern dive crews had news that neither company had thought to share beyond their own gates: they had run the wreck line from the Maw approaches two weeks ago and found, north of the usual drift, broken Grung hulls on the reef approach off a small island in the Karath channel, and smoke from the island's beach that should not have been there. The island had a reputation. The smoke meant something alive and desperate was making it.

Whichever heading the Uncertainty had taken from the strait, the signal waited at the end of it. Every port carried its own version, its own piece of the same picture. Each one, independently, pointed at the same three miles of reef and the same narrow beach and the same smoke, which meant the same people, and those people had now been on that beach for the better part of two weeks.

Aruhe was the calculation. The field opened in every direction, and every path across it led back to the same island in the end.
