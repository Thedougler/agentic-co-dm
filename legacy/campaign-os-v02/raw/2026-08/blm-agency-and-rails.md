## The tension the GM has to resolve

> "I want to be living in a story, immersed into a character that is not, to
> their knowledge, living in a story."

Players want a narrative arc and simultaneously want their character to be
unaware of being in one. Characters pursue their goals efficiently; players
want shape. Mulligan treats that gap as the GM's problem to solve, not the
players'.

## Players as water; the GM as irrigation

> "They are like water. They are going down the hill as fast as they can,
> seeking the path of least resistance."

The GM's job is described as irrigating the slope: placing obstacles so that
the route players take — always the fastest one available to them — arrives at
a shape worth watching. The obstacles come out of the characters' own
backstories, so the guidance is not felt as constraint. The distinction drawn
is against railroading: the water genuinely chooses its route, and the channel
is what the GM built.

## Backstory as the reliable hook

Mulligan characterises backstory material as "plot hooks you'll bite every
time," setting it against a free-floating mystery a party can decline. A hook
attached to something a character is already invested in — a sworn enemy
appearing — does not need the party to opt in.

The practical consequence: knowing what players are invested in is what makes
preparation predictable enough to be worth doing.

## Session Zero as the source of that knowledge

The roundtable frames Session Zero as where the GM learns character goals and
fears, so that the conflicts built afterward attach to things the players
already want to push on. Agency stays real; the shape is built in advance.
