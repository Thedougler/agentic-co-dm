## "Yes, And" does not transfer wholesale

Mulligan treats the first rule of improv as having limits at the table:

> "It's sort of like a first rule of improv. But 'Yes, And' isn't always
> appropriate for D&D."

## Where the prepared material has to hold

Some things are fixed before play and stay fixed, because their fixedness is
what makes the world real:

> "Sometimes there is a dungeon that has been pre-drawn, and the traps are
> either there or they're not there. We can fudge it a little bit; we can have
> some fun, we can make an NPC where maybe there wasn't one before. But
> occasionally, if you're making a big scheme for your evil villain, you don't
> always want to wing that, because it might start to sap away at the reality
> of the world."

The distinction drawn is between detail that can be generated on demand (an
NPC who was not there before) and structure that cannot be improvised without
cost (a villain's scheme, a dungeon's traps).

## Pattern as the shared mechanism

Asked what improv and tabletop have in common, he points at pattern rather
than spontaneity:

> "What I think the two actually have in common that doesn't get talked about
> as much is the strength of pattern in creating meaning and structure."

He describes reuse as a labour-saving technique, not only an artistic one:

> "Literally, I'm doing less work. That's a real trick in improv; you're doing
> a series of scenes over and over, and you keep bringing back the same joke.
> You bring back the same joke is a tool you are using to succeed."
