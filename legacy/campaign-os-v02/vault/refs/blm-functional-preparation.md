# Functional Preparation

Brennan Lee Mulligan describes his own preparation as "80% improv and 20%
planning." That ratio is not a claim that little preparation happens — on
Dimension 20 the production itself forces heavy prep: "In Dimension 20 we
need to fully map out all of the battlemaps in order to be built in time,
even the digital ones. So, there is a tremendous amount of planning that
happens." The ratio describes what the prep is *for*, not how much of it
there is.

## Reject outcome-based prep

The governing distinction is between outcome-based prep ("what happens if
players do X") and reactive systems. Outcome-based prep is rejected in
favor of preparing elements with enough internal logic to respond to any
choice put to them. Under this model the world acts autonomously and the
GM adjudicates rather than narrates a predetermined result.

## Prep as toys

The image Mulligan uses for this is toys rather than script: "Like toys,
you dump them out and the kids play with them, and then you have your
planning go towards something you can improvise with." Preparation is
aimed at producing objects that can be played with, not a sequence to be
followed — what gets prepared is the thing that can react, not the events
that will happen to it.

Stated directly: "I would rather have an NPC with a personality ready to
go, and then kind of wing it, rather than plan my session rigidly,
especially because you never know how your players will react." The
reason given is unpredictability at the table — a rigid plan assumes a
response that has not happened yet, while a prepared personality can meet
any response.

## What gets prepared ahead of time

The concrete list of what is built in advance: complete battle maps
(physical and digital), NPC names and personalities, city names,
appearances, and maps, and detailed worldbuilding elements. World-model
detail itself follows a "mile wide, inch deep" standard: tone and
character-response consistency matter more than logistical realism.

## The two failure modes

Session-note depth is framed as a balance between two failure modes,
not a target quantity of notes.

**Under-preparation** produces a lack of compelling plot, undeveloped
characters, and a hollow world.

**Over-preparation** prevents player characters from meaningfully
affecting the lore of the world. This second failure is the less obvious
one: past a certain point, more prep does not make a session better — it
makes an outcome already settled, closing off what a choice could still
change.

The stated method is to prepare enough to create set pieces and elements
for players to interact with, and stop short of meticulous planning that
would go unused. Flexibility and fluidity in the remaining space are what
let player-character actions shape the story, per the source.

Rather than steering toward a predetermined path, the stated method for
generating motivation is to make things happen in the world: events built
interesting enough to draw attention, each carrying a draw that offers
something to follow.

## Where "Yes, And" stops applying

Mulligan treats improv's first rule as having limits at the table: "It's
sort of like a first rule of improv. But 'Yes, And' isn't always
appropriate for D&D."

Some material is fixed before play and stays fixed, because its fixedness
is what makes the world real: "Sometimes there is a dungeon that has been
pre-drawn, and the traps are either there or they're not there. We can
fudge it a little bit; we can have some fun, we can make an NPC where
maybe there wasn't one before. But occasionally, if you're making a big
scheme for your evil villain, you don't always want to wing that, because
it might start to sap away at the reality of the world."

The line drawn is between detail that can be generated on demand (an NPC
who was not there before) and structure that cannot be improvised without
cost (a villain's scheme, a dungeon's traps).

## Pattern as the underlying mechanism

Asked what improv and tabletop share, Mulligan points at pattern rather
than spontaneity: "What I think the two actually have in common that
doesn't get talked about as much is the strength of pattern in creating
meaning and structure." He frames reuse as a labor-saving technique, not
only an artistic one: "Literally, I'm doing less work. That's a real trick
in improv; you're doing a series of scenes over and over, and you keep
bringing back the same joke. You bring back the same joke is a tool you
are using to succeed."

## Gaps the sources leave unstated

None of the four sources define where the line falls between "enough
prep to create a set piece" and "meticulous planning that goes unused" —
the boundary is asserted, not quantified. Nor do they specify how much
detail a villain's scheme needs before it counts as fixed structure
rather than improvisable detail; the dungeon-traps/NPC contrast is the
only worked example given.
