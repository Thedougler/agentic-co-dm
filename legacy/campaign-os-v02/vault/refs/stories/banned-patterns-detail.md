# Prose rules — the reasoning behind each

The adjudication record for [banned-patterns.md](banned-patterns.md)'s
instructions and for the Vale rules under `docs/vale-styles/CampaignOS/`. Each
entry below carries why a rule exists and the GM's own wording for it.

**Never read this while drafting.** It quotes bad prose to explain each rule,
and a catalogue of bad sentences read before writing makes those sentences
easier to write. Open a single entry when a finding is disputed or a linter
message is ambiguous, and read only that entry.

Every rule here lives in one of two enforcement halves:
[banned-patterns.md](banned-patterns.md) states it positively for the drafting
agent when it needs judgment, and Vale fires it on the line when it has a
mechanical tell. Provenance for all of them is in
[prose-aesthetic-source-note.md](prose-aesthetic-source-note.md).

**Trailing justification clauses.** Never close a sentence by explaining why
the action happened — "because that was the job", "because they are there and
reading them is what you do with writing", "because arithmetic done quietly is
arithmetic somebody can lie about". The scene already carries the reason.
Delete the clause; the sentence was finished before it.

**Announced stakes.** Never have characters state a wager, a game, a rule, or
the terms of their own dynamic out loud when the behaviour shows it. Two people
keeping score just say numbers; the reader works out that it's a contest. Any
line that exists to tell the reader what to notice comes out.

**"Wrong" as a descriptor.** Never call a thing, a sound, a body, or a feeling
"wrong" or invoke "wrongness" — it's a vague judgment standing in for the
concrete detail that should be there. State the specific physical fact that
reads as unnatural instead ("a whisper returns as a chord," not "the sound
comes back wrong").

**Tautological simile.** Never use a comparison whose vehicle just restates
the tenor ("it fell the way anything that heavy falls," "came up the way
water finds a crack"). A simile earns its place only when the thing compared
to is more vivid or specific than the thing itself — if it isn't, cut the
comparison and keep the fact. "It fell into the rubble it had made," not "it
fell the way anything that heavy falls."

**Forced metaphor.** Never reach for a figure whose vehicle doesn't actually
map onto the tenor — it sounds like it means something but the logic breaks
on inspection ("wore her shape the way a coat outlives the man who bought
it" is about durability; the tenor is a shadow wearing a stolen form —
different axis entirely). If the comparison can't survive being checked
literally, the idea it's reaching for goes in plainly instead.

**Negation-stacking.** Never define a thing by piling up what it is *not*
("It didn't announce itself. It didn't have anything to announce.") to fake
gravity — one negation per beat, maximum, ever. State what it did, once, and
move on. Distinct from [ai-tells.md](ai-tells.md)'s negative parallelism
("It's not just a sword — it's a legacy."): that's a rhetorical contrast
device, this is factual over-negation piled onto one image.

**Coy narration.** Never gesture at an action the POV can see instead of
naming it ("the plaza did the thing plazas do," "that was apparently also a
job someone had to do"). If the narrator knows what happened, say what
happened — playing coy with a reader who's already inside the scene breaks
the narrative contract, it doesn't build suspense.

**Narratorial hedging.** Never let the third-person narrator soften a fact of
its own story with "apparently," "seemed," or "somehow" — a narrator hedging
on the world it's telling reads as a writer who hasn't decided what happened.
State it as fact; if the uncertainty belongs to a character, put it in their
head, not the narration.

**Had-I-But-Known foreshadow.** Never drop an isolated, ominous standalone
sentence ahead of the scene that's about mood rather than information ("The
counting started before anyone meant it to.") — it front-loads dread the
scene hasn't earned yet. Open on the concrete act instead and let the dread
come from what's actually happening.

**Antithesis-mirror closers.** Never end a section on a chiastic "the X
anyone could see / the X nobody had seen" mirror-sentence, or an identity
tautology ("X had been X for two more seconds") — the symmetry does the
emotional work a real image should, and it's a crutch that shows most at
section ends, exactly where it's most tempting to reach for. Close on a
specific fact or image, not a rhetorical shape.

**"Same X" continuity filler.** Never open a beat/scene transition by
asserting sameness with what came before ("Same vault," "Same room, same
chairs" repeated beat to beat) — restating the unchanged setting is lazy
and carries no information. Name what's *different* since the last beat
instead: the count that's climbed, the water that's settled, the door
that's cleared — continuity comes from the delta, not the assertion of
no-change.

**Wry, retrospective narration over a life-or-death sequence.** A specific case
of the general register-weight rule ([register.md](register.md) § The weight is
not the writer's choice) — this is the correction on record for it. Never
narrate a scene where someone is dying, escaping, or drowning in the amused,
anecdotal register of a story told safely afterward — no "once, memorably," no
casual domestic simile at the moment of ignition, no "and that is the whole
account of what she did next," no arch narrator observing from a comfortable
distance. Stay in the body and in the present pressure: what her hands are
doing, what her lungs cost, what she can and cannot reach. Retrospective
distance is earned only after the danger is over, and even then it stays cold,
never amused. This governs humour placed *inside* a life-or-death beat; the
deadpan undercut landing after a dense or self-serious passage is a documented
move ([prose-aesthetic.md](prose-aesthetic.md) § Narrator and voice) and is not
this pattern.

**Pre-leaking a reveal through a monitoring detail.** Never let the POV character
stop checking, stop hearing, or lose track of the thing whose state is the coming
reveal — "his breathing, which she checked for at first and then stopped checking
for" hands the reader the death before the character reaches it, and the reveal
lands on someone who already knows. Keep every detail in the passage neutral as
to the outcome, or make it point the wrong way; the reader finds out when the
character does, not one paragraph early.

**Over-narrated confrontation dialogue.** Never pad a short, devastating exchange with heavy
narration between every line — when the source material (a script, a transcript, a scene the GM
is adapting) is mostly terse back-and-forth dialogue, match that economy instead of expanding
each beat into a paragraph of description. Trust repetition ("We love you. We love you.") and a
bare staging beat (one sentence, sometimes a fragment) between lines to carry the scene; a
narration block between every line of dialogue smothers the confrontation the terse original
built. Full rewrite over patch when this is the actual problem — trimming individual sentences
inside an over-narrated draft doesn't fix it, the connective tissue itself has to go.

**Abstract label as the reveal.** Never close a dangerous, vivid, or uncanny beat by naming it
with a dry analytical noun ("That's not devotion. That's architecture.") as if the abstraction
itself is the punch. A label like this drains the cool, dangerous image the beat just built and
replaces it with a term from a lecture. Land the same reveal in a concrete, physical image
instead — a leash, a collar, a hook, something the reader can see and feel — never a category
word standing in for one.

**Vague placeholder in place of the fact.** Never hold a place with "at some
point," "some kind of," "somehow," "in some way," "a sense of dread," "an
expression she never found a word for," or "whatever it was" when the POV can
see the thing. These are the shape of a detail with no detail in it, and they
invent a mystery about material the scene has already handed the narrator.
Name it: what it was, when it happened, what the body did. Vale's
`CampaignOS.VagueNarration` catches the phrase list mechanically — extend
tokens there, not this paragraph, when a new placeholder phrase turns up.

**A genuine unknown is not an exception to this.** A person who cannot identify
a thing does not stop at "something" — they reach for a name, offer the nearest
candidates they have, and reject them: *"A beast? A deer, maybe? I don't know —
something dragged him off into the forest."* That reaching is the character
thinking on the page, and it tells the reader what the thing was **not**, which
is real information. A bare "something" tells the reader nothing and reads as
the writer declining to imagine it. This holds in dialogue and in close
narration alike: render the failed identification — the size it would have to
be, the animal it resembles and doesn't, the mark it should have left and
didn't — never the placeholder on its own.

**Stacked adjectives in place of the image.** Never build a description by
chaining generic adjectives — "small and level and quiet," "solid and white and
giving," "close and dark and got no thinner." Three vague words joined by *and*
read as thoroughness while naming nothing; the reader gets a mood where an
image belongs, and the sentence gets longer while getting weaker. One concrete,
specific detail beats any chain: what the light does on it, what it costs to
carry, what it does to the hand. Trim to the single adjective that is doing
work, or replace the whole chain with the physical fact underneath it. Also
`CampaignOS.VagueNarration` (same rule as the placeholder ban above — its
triple-adjective regex enforces this one).

**"The way a man does X" simile.** Never characterize a look, a gesture, or a
manner by comparing it to a generic unnamed person performing an everyday task
— "looking at the island the way a man looks across a field he means to cut,"
"the way a person keeps a coin meant for spending later," "his arms going like
a man already swimming," "the way a man eats something he has decided not to
look at too closely." It is a stock construction that substitutes an invented
stranger for the actual person in the scene, and it recurs constantly once it
starts. Describe what the character's body is doing instead: where the eyes
travel, how long they stay, how many times they go back. The reader draws the
comparison; the narrator never supplies it.

**NPC crosstalk in front of the party.** Never stage two NPCs talking to each
other while the party stands there — an argument, a briefing, a back-and-forth
the players watch. It makes an audience of the table at the exact moment they
should be acting. One NPC addresses the party; every other present NPC gets a
clause naming what their hands are doing instead. Full rule, including the short
over-the-shoulder exchange that stays legal: [npc-guidance.md](../npc-guidance.md)
§1.

**Bureaucratic framing for a charged beat.** Never render a character's grief,
fury, conviction, or daring in the register of procedure, bookkeeping, or
administration — no ledgers, accounts, prices, deposits, debts, or dry abstract
nouns like "logistics" standing in for the thing itself. It makes the character
cold and small when the point is that they are burning, and it makes a desperate
act sound like a meeting. Two prisoners plotting an escape whisper about fire and
open water, never about "logistics." Name the emotion and the act it produces in
the concrete nouns of the act; if a pattern looks systematic from outside, let an
observer notice the pattern, never the character perform it. Vale's
`CampaignOS.BureaucraticRegister` carries the full word list mechanically —
extend tokens there, not this paragraph, when a new workplace word turns up.
