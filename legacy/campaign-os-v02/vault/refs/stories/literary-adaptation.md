# Literary Adaptation Framework

When adapting a novel, film, myth, or series into campaign content, follow
this four-step process: extract the source's core, translate it into
mechanics, build player entry points, then design where the campaign will
depart from it. The goal is to preserve what makes the source compelling
while transforming it into something that lives through player agency.

## Step 1: Extract the core

**Identify the thematic spine** — what is the story *actually about* beneath
the plot? The Dark Tower isn't about reaching a tower; it's about obsession
and the cost of single-minded purpose. Lord of the Rings isn't about
destroying a ring; it's about ordinary courage in the face of overwhelming
evil.

**Map the emotional core**, at two grains:

- The macro shape — chart the campaign's emotional arc, e.g. *Wonder →
  Unease → Dread → Resolve → Sacrifice → Catharsis*. This is the skeleton
  even when the plot changes.
- The beat level — strip individual scenes to the emotional truth under the
  plot mechanics: "Character confronts the thing they fear most." "The
  trusted ally is revealed to be compromised." "Everything the heroes fought
  for is rendered meaningless — until they find a new reason to fight." The
  *how* changes; the *what it feels like* stays.

**Catalog iconic moments** — scenes that define the source and must be
preserved in some form. These become the campaign's tentpole encounters: the
Battle of Helm's Deep, the Red Wedding, the reveal in the cave on Dagobah.
Players should feel the *weight* of these moments even in translated form.

**Identify player-agency friction points** — where does the source's
predetermined plot conflict with the need for player choice? A novel's
protagonist makes specific decisions; the players won't make those same
decisions. These friction points are the redesign priorities.

**If working chapter-by-chapter or act-by-act** (a full novel or series
adaptation), build a breakdown of plot events, character introductions,
setting descriptions, thematic developments, and tonal shifts, and tag each
element:

- **ESSENTIAL** — must appear in some form (thematic core, iconic moments,
  structural foundations)
- **ADAPTABLE** — can be modified significantly (specific plot mechanics,
  secondary characters, exact sequences)
- **ATMOSPHERIC** — flavor, not structure (world details, minor scenes,
  background color)

Along the way, build a **theme map** linking source themes to mechanical
expressions (e.g., "corruption" → gradual stat penalties, dark-gift
temptations, faction reputation shifts), and identify the source's narrative
structure so it can be mapped to a compatible campaign shape (structure
catalog: `.claude/skills/draft-story/references/story-structures.md`).

## Step 2: Translate into mechanics

**Convert themes into playable moral dilemmas** with no clean answers, using
the theme map above. "Power corrupts" becomes a magic item that grows
stronger with morally questionable uses. "Redemption is possible" becomes a
villain who genuinely wants to change — but can the party trust them?

**Translate literary locations to setting equivalents** that give players
footing. The source's haunted castle maps onto the campaign's existing
gothic region; its political court maps onto the capital the party already
knows.

**Redesign key characters as NPCs with independent agency** — goals,
resources, and plans that exist whether the players engage or not. The
novel's protagonist becomes an NPC ally (or rival) pursuing their own
version of the quest.

**Transform set-piece scenes into encounters with multiple outcome
paths** — combat, social, stealth, arcane, environmental. The source's
inevitable showdown becomes a situation with three ways in, two ways out,
and a wild card the players might invent.

## Step 3: Build player entry points

The PCs can't be the novel's protagonist — they need to be *their own*
protagonists in a world shaped by the source's events. Options:

- **Parallel characters** in the same world, pursuing related goals from a
  different angle
- The source's characters **reimagined with player agency** — same roles,
  different choices
- A world **after the source's events** — dealing with consequences and
  echoes
- **Originals in an adapted setting** — the world is the source, the story
  is new

## Step 4: Design the departure points

Decide where to honor the source and where to diverge from it.

**Faithfully echo** the source where emotional beats, thematic resonance,
and iconic imagery define the experience — the *feeling* of a moment
matters more than its exact plot mechanics. **Deliberately diverge** where
player agency demands it, where subverting expectations rewards players who
know the source, or where mechanical depth enriches the experience beyond
passive storytelling. These departure points are what make it a *campaign*
instead of a *retelling*.

Identify 3–5 concrete moments where player choice can fork the story away
from the source. For each:

- Design at least **two alternative paths** that maintain thematic
  integrity while creating genuine divergence
- Ensure both paths have meaningful consequences
- Plan how divergent paths might reconverge at later tentpole moments (or
  not)

## Handling players who know the source

**Subvert expectations deliberately.** If they know the betrayal is coming,
make the betrayer sympathetic first — or make a *different* character
betray them. Their knowledge becomes a tool for dramatic irony.

**Reward knowledge without requiring it.** Easter eggs and callbacks (built
into Step 4's divergence design) are gifts, not prerequisites — a player who
recognizes the reference should feel clever, not advantaged.

**Let them be wrong.** If they think they know what happens next because
they read the book, let the campaign surprise them. That surprise is the
moment they realize this story is *theirs*, not a retelling.
