# Genre Conventions — Fantasy and Horror

Genre is a contract with the audience. Players sit down to a fantasy campaign
expecting immersive world and consistent magic; to a horror arc expecting dread
that builds. Bend conventions freely, but breaking them without purpose loses
trust. Know the conventions before you subvert them.

## Fantasy

| Element | Convention |
|---|---|
| Focus | Magic, secondary worlds, mythic narrative |
| Worldbuilding | Deep and consistent — magic systems, cultures, history |
| Magic | Must have rules and costs |
| Stakes | Often epic (world-level), but personal stakes anchor it |

Players expect an immersive world, consistent magic, and a mythic or heroic arc.
Epic stakes only land when anchored personal: "the lich will unmake the coast"
is scenery until it becomes "the lich will unmake the fishing town where the
ranger's sister still lives."

**Sanderson's Laws** (govern magic in prose and at the table alike):

1. The ability to solve conflict with magic is proportional to how well the
   audience understands the magic. A ritual the players have watched fail twice
   can save the day; an unexplained deus ex machina cannot.
2. Limitations are more interesting than powers. What the court wizard *cannot*
   do — and what it costs when she tries anyway — generates more story than her
   spell list.
3. Expand what you already have before adding something new. A second use for
   the party's known relic beats a fresh artifact.

**Common pitfalls**: worldbuilding dumps that stop the story (distribute lore
through conflict and consequence, not lectures); magic without cost (if teleport
is free, why does anyone ride?); epic stakes with no personal anchor.

## Horror

| Element | Convention |
|---|---|
| Focus | Fear, dread, the unknown |
| Pacing | Slow build of dread, punctuated by shock |
| Atmosphere | Setting as character — darkness, isolation, wrongness |
| Threat | Often ambiguous or incomprehensible |

Players expect genuine unease, escalating dread, and a threat that feels
inescapable. This register powers dungeon atmosphere even in a non-horror
campaign: the best dungeons are horror sequences with treasure in them.

**The three fears** — sequence them deliberately across a scene or a level:

| Type | Mechanism | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Terror | Anticipation of threat | Something is behind the door |
| Horror | Confrontation with threat | The door opens |
| Revulsion | Physical disgust | What came through the door |

Terror is the strongest and cheapest — it runs on the players' imagination.
Spend it long, spend horror short, and spend revulsion once.

**Dread-building levers**: isolation (cut the exits, the light, the allies);
wrongness (one detail that shouldn't be — the swept floor in the sealed tomb);
escalation with pauses (each false calm makes the next spike land harder);
the incomprehensible (a threat whose rules the players can't yet model).

**Common pitfalls**: over-explaining the monster (explanation kills dread —
keep the threat's rules half-glimpsed as long as possible); jump scares without
buildup (shock spends dread you haven't earned); grimness with no purpose
(nihilism reads as GM indifference, not atmosphere).

## Blending

A horror dungeon inside a heroic-fantasy campaign works when each register's
core promise is honored in its own space: dread inside the dungeon, and the
heroic frame — the party can win, and winning matters — intact around it.
Identify which genre's ending convention dominates the arc and keep that
promise above all.
