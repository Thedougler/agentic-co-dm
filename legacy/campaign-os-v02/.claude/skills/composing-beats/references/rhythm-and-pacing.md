# Rhythm and pacing heuristics

The alternation grammar lives in `composition.md` (Rhythm,
Step 8). This file holds the table-time heuristics.

- The classic pacing heuristic is roughly **one Beat per half-hour of
  play**. Use it as an estimation aid, not as a reason to stretch,
  truncate, or force play.
- A ~4-hour session therefore carries roughly 6–8 active Beats along
  whichever path the table takes — prepare the nearest ones heavily and
  the far ones as pressure sketches (`composition.md` § Branch
  discipline).

## The session arc

Every session follows its own dramatic arc within the larger campaign.
Pacing is the difference between a session that feels like a story and one
that feels like a to-do list. When sequencing a session's beats, size and
order them against this arc.

**Scope vs. `.claude/skills/draft-content/references/run-guide.md` § Pacing**: this file is the
authoring-stage layer — the session's dramatic arc, table-time budgets, and
craft-level pacing tools. `.claude/skills/draft-content/references/run-guide.md` § Pacing is the DM-facing operational layer —
*which* threads/fronts to run this specific session and the register rules
that keep them from feeling scattered. Neither file is the sole owner of
the rhythm principles both touch (register variation, non-combat climaxes,
ending on a cliffhanger) — cross-referenced inline below.

### 1. Cold open (5–10 minutes)

Start in the middle of something. Recap through action, not summary.

**Do:** "Last we left off, you were knee-deep in swamp water with something
*large* moving beneath the surface."

**Don't:** "Last session you traveled to the swamp and talked to some NPCs."

The cold open should re-engage the players immediately. Drop them into
tension, sensation, or a decision. Even a quiet opening can have urgency —
"You wake to the smell of smoke" is a cold open.

Brennan Lee Mulligan's Strong Start Protocol is the standard the opener is
written against: an active scene demanding a response, never an
out-of-character recap, digging a hole the players can jump into. The
operational form — the five opener types and their anti-patterns — is
`.claude/skills/writing-hook-beats/references/hook-beats.md` § Strong starts (session openers).
An opener the table plays from a borrowed NPC POV before the PCs' own Hook is
a cold open instead: `.claude/skills/writing-cold-opens/SKILL.md`.

### 2. Rising action (60–90 minutes)

The main content of the session — exploration, investigation, social
encounters, travel, smaller combats. This is where the story builds.

**Key principles:**

- Build tension gradually — each scene should raise the stakes or deepen
  the mystery slightly more than the last
- Alternate between high-energy and low-energy scenes to prevent fatigue
  (operational rule: `.claude/skills/draft-content/references/run-guide.md` § Pacing § Rhythm Rules 1, "never repeat a
  register back-to-back")
- Plant seeds for the session's climax — foreshadow, build dread, create
  anticipation
- Let players drive the pace when possible — follow their curiosity

### 3. Climax (30–60 minutes)

Same rule stated operationally: `.claude/skills/draft-content/references/run-guide.md` § Pacing § Rhythm Rules 3, "the climax
doesn't have to be combat."

Climax craft: `writing-climax-beats` skill — owns beat construction,
pressure engine, handle design, escalation triggers, outcome dimensions,
and the validation gate.

The session's dramatic peak — a major combat, a critical revelation, a
gut-punch NPC moment, or a decision that can't be undone.

**The climax should feel earned.** Everything in the rising action was
building to this. The information they gathered, the allies they made (or
lost), the terrain they scouted — it all matters here.

Not every climax is combat. A climax can be:

- The moment they realize the ally was the traitor
- A moral choice with no good options
- A desperate negotiation with the clock running out
- The reveal that changes everything they thought they knew

### 4. Falling action (15–30 minutes)

Resolution craft: `writing-resolution-beats` skill — owns fallout
construction, consequence envelopes, gravity receipts, state deltas,
and the validation gate.

Process the climax. Let players react, plan, loot, argue, grieve,
celebrate, and breathe.

This is where character moments happen. The quiet conversation after the
battle. The argument about what to do with the prisoner. The moment someone
says "My character walks away from the group and sits alone." **Don't rush
this.** These are the scenes players remember as much as the fights.

### 5. Cliffhanger (5 minutes)

Same rule stated operationally: `.claude/skills/draft-content/references/run-guide.md` § Pacing § Rhythm Rules 4, "end on an
up-note or a cliffhanger, never a plateau."

Cliffhanger craft: `writing-cliffhanger-beats` skill — owns beat
construction, pressure patterns, escalation, and the quality gate.

## Pacing tools

### The ticking clock

Add a deadline to raise urgency. The ritual completes at midnight. The dam
breaks in three days. The assassination happens at the feast. The poison
kills in six hours.

Ticking clocks transform exploration from leisurely browsing into agonizing
triage. When time is finite, every choice about where to go and what to
investigate becomes meaningful.

**Use when:** the session feels low-stakes or aimless, or when you want to
force hard choices about priorities.

### The breather scene

Operational form of this tool: `.claude/skills/draft-content/references/run-guide.md` § Pacing § Pacing Failure Modes,
"Escalation Fatigue."

After intense sequences, give players a campfire conversation, a tavern
night, a shopping montage, or a quiet walk through the city. Let characters
*be* characters.

Breather scenes serve three purposes:

1. **Emotional processing** — players need time to react to what just
   happened
2. **Character development** — inter-party relationships deepen in downtime
3. **Contrast** — the next intense scene hits harder because of the calm
   before it

**Use when:** you've just run an intense combat, a major revelation, or a
high-stakes social encounter.

### The dramatic cut

End a scene at its peak moment and cut to a different location, character,
or timeline.

"The arrow flies toward your face — and we cut to what's happening in the
throne room."

Dramatic cuts build suspense, allow parallel storytelling, and keep energy
high across multiple storylines. They work especially well in campaigns
with split-party moments or competing timelines.

**Use when:** you want to build suspense, switch between split groups, or
prevent a scene from losing momentum by running too long.

### The information drip

Never dump exposition — reveal story information through NPC
conversations, environmental details, recovered documents, overheard
arguments, and physical evidence instead, and let players assemble the
picture.

The information drip respects player intelligence. Instead of "the
villain's plan is X," give them three puzzle pieces across three scenes and
let them put it together themselves. The moment of realization — when a
player says "Wait... does that mean..." — is one of the most satisfying
moments in tabletop gaming.

**Use when:** always. Exposition dumps are almost never the right choice.

## Pacing across multiple sessions

The session arc (Cold Open → Climax → Cliffhanger) nests inside the
campaign's own three-act shape — a story-shape choice owned by
`.claude/skills/draft-story/references/story-structures-classic.md` § Three-act structure,
which already maps Act I/II/III onto Session, Story Arc, and Campaign
scale.

### Avoiding the mid-campaign sag

One altitude down, the same sag hits a single session:
`.claude/skills/draft-content/references/run-guide.md` § Pacing § Pacing Failure Modes, "The Slow Middle." The middle of a
campaign is where pacing most often fails. Combat this with:

- **Tentpole encounters** — pre-planned dramatic peaks that punctuate the
  middle sessions
- **Escalation** — each session should feel like the situation is getting
  more urgent, complex, or personal
- **Character-driven sessions** — shift focus to PC backstories and
  inter-party dynamics when the main plot needs breathing room
- **Subplots** — weave secondary storylines that pay off during the middle
  act
