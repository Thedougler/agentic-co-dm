# Resolution Types

Consult this reference after a Climax outcome exists, or while preparing only the specific Resolution conditions that could plausibly arise from the current Climax.

These patterns derive from the Resolution examples in *Scripting the Game*.

They describe **fallout**.

They do not authorize the GM to force the corresponding outcome.

## General rule

Select the pattern that most accurately describes what the Climax produced.

A Resolution can contain more than one factual element:

- the villain dies **and** the town is saved;
- the heroes escape **and** the villain retains the artifact;
- the villain surrenders **and** a larger organization remains;
- the immediate threat ends **and** the victory creates a power vacuum.

Choose one primary pattern for structural clarity.

Use secondary elements only when they are actual consequences.

A pattern must never be used as a reason to alter the Climax outcome after the fact.

---

# Happy Ending

## Use when

The players successfully resolve the central situation and the immediate outcome provides genuine safety, relief, restoration, reunion, recognition, or another desired result.

"Happy" means the principal problem was successfully resolved.

It does not require a cost-free utopia.

## Prepare

Before play, establish:

- what can actually be saved;
- who benefits if the PCs succeed;
- what rewards or recognition are available;
- which relationships can meaningfully improve;
- what costs remain even after success.

## Compile

Show:

1. the objective that was achieved;
2. its visible effect;
3. the people or communities who respond;
4. relevant gravity receipts;
5. any cost that genuinely survived the victory.

## Agency guardrail

Let the world celebrate the heroes.

Do not declare that the heroes feel triumphant.

## Example

**Landing:** At sunrise, the harbor bells ring for ships rather than evacuation.

### What Changed
- [[Beacon of Rell]] burns again; the reef passage is navigable.
- [[Salt Council]] withdraws the evacuation order.
- The fleet the party preserved returns to commercial service.

### Gravity Receipts
- [[Ilya]] kept her promise to bring [[Captain Mora]] and his crew home alive.

# Villain Killed

## Use when

The antagonist actually dies as a consequence of play.

Death must be established rather than assumed because the villain lost.

## Prepare

Know:

- whether the antagonist has a credible means of surviving apparent death;
- what organization or pressure survives them;
- who benefits from their death;
- what authority, secret, resource, or conflict loses its owner.

Do not invent a survival mechanism afterward solely to preserve a recurring villain.

## Compile

Establish:

- that the antagonist is dead;
- what credible evidence of death exists;
- who reacts immediately;
- what assets, followers, secrets, or obligations remain;
- whether a power vacuum is created.

## Agency guardrail

Do not cheapen a player-earned kill with an unsupported resurrection, clone, illusion, body double, contingency, or retroactive escape.

If survival was genuinely established, the correct pattern may instead be **Villain Escapes**.

---

# Villain Surrenders

## Use when

The antagonist voluntarily yields, capitulates, accepts terms, or is forced into an explicit surrender during the Climax.

Being reduced to zero resistance is not automatically the same as surrender.

## Prepare

Know:

- what the antagonist values enough to surrender for;
- what leverage they retain;
- who can enforce custody or terms;
- what information they possess;
- which factions care what happens to them.

## Compile

Establish:

- the terms or circumstances of surrender;
- who presently controls the antagonist;
- what weapons, authority, assets, or leverage they relinquish;
- who responds;
- what disposition remains for the PCs or legitimate authorities to decide.

## Agency guardrail

Surrender does not grant the GM authority to decide whether the PCs forgive, execute, imprison, recruit, exile, or release the antagonist.

If that decision itself is dramatically unresolved, stop before narrating past it.

---

# Villain Escapes

## Use when

The antagonist loses or fails to secure the central objective but remains free and capable of future action.

Escape should be the consequence of:

- an established escape route;
- an ability already available;
- a player choice;
- a legitimate outcome of the Climax;
- a credible opportunity created by the situation.

## Prepare

If escape is genuinely possible, establish its means **before it is needed**.

Examples:

- a smuggler tunnel;
- a teleportation circle;
- a loyal extraction crew;
- a disguised vessel;
- a contingency spell;
- an escape pod;
- political immunity;
- an ally positioned nearby.

The existence of an escape route does not guarantee the villain uses it successfully.

Players can discover, block, exploit, or destroy it.

## Compile

Establish:

- what objective the villain nevertheless lost;
- how the villain escaped;
- what they abandoned;
- what resources or followers they retain;
- whether anyone can presently pursue them.

## Agency guardrail

The villain's survival must not transform player victory into "nothing mattered."

A defeated antagonist who escapes should normally leave the players holding some consequence of their success.

---

# Heroes Captured

## Use when

The Climax genuinely ends with the PCs in enemy custody or equivalent confinement.

Capture is a changed campaign state, not permission to narrate arbitrary future suffering.

## Prepare

Know:

- who wants the PCs alive;
- why death is not the captor's preferred immediate outcome;
- what location or mechanism can hold them;
- what the captors want from them;
- what happens to important possessions or allies.

## Compile

Establish:

- who controls the PCs;
- where they are;
- what immediately happened to relevant gear, allies, objectives, or evidence;
- what the captor demonstrably wants;
- what parts of the previous objective were lost, preserved, or remain unresolved.

Stop before deciding how the PCs respond to captivity.

Their next attempt to negotiate, deceive, escape, cooperate, revolt, or wait belongs to future play.

## Agency guardrail

Do not use capture as an invisible safety net that prevents meaningful failure.

If capture occurs, preserve the losses and altered position produced by that failure.

---

# Heroes Escape

## Use when

The PCs leave the decisive situation alive but without fully controlling its outcome.

This can represent:

- a successful retreat;
- escape from overwhelming opposition;
- extraction by an established ally;
- survival after a failed objective;
- flight from a disaster they could not stop.

## Prepare

Establish plausible exits, allies, terrain, transportation, or pressures.

Do not prepare a guaranteed deus ex machina.

## Compile

Show:

- how the PCs got clear;
- what they retained;
- what they lost;
- what objective remains in enemy hands or unresolved;
- what immediate pressure ended because they escaped.

## Agency guardrail

Survival does not erase failure.

If the PCs escaped after losing the central objective, preserve that changed world state.

If an external actor enabled the escape, it must be an established actor or an event that actually emerged during play.

# Ending Cliffhanger

## Use when

The adventure's central dramatic question has genuinely resolved and an **immediate new problem** appears in the aftermath.

The new problem must be distinct from the question that just ended.

Correct:

> The necromancer is dead and the ritual has stopped. As the party leaves, every grave bell in the neighboring valley begins ringing.

Incorrect:

> The necromancer is still performing the ritual, so the party must keep fighting.

The second is unfinished Climax.

## Prepare

Use only when an existing pressure could naturally produce the sting.

Seed the possibility through:

- established factions;
- unresolved consequences;
- known environmental danger;
- existing enemies;
- prior warnings;
- observable instability.

## Compile

Give the players enough information to understand that a new situation exists.

Do not resolve it.

## Agency guardrail

An Ending Cliffhanger creates **new uncertainty**.

It does not revoke the answer the players just earned.

Use sparingly. A campaign does not need to end every adventure by yelling "but wait."

---

# Greater Threat

## Use when

Resolving the local problem reveals that it was part of a larger existing pressure.

Examples:

- the defeated cell belongs to a larger organization;
- the stopped landing force was only one element of an invasion;
- the exposed conspiracy points to a higher patron;
- the destroyed machine was one node in a network;
- the defeated champion served a larger power.

## Prepare

The larger structure should already exist in the world's causal model.

Ideally, earlier evidence can be reinterpreted in light of the reveal.

The players do not need to have solved the larger mystery in advance.

The larger threat does need to be more than an ending invented because another adventure is desired.

## Compile

Establish only:

- what evidence reveals the larger scope;
- how the completed adventure relates to it;
- what the players' victory actually accomplished;
- what remains beyond the solved problem.

Do not automatically assign the new threat as the party's next quest.

The world may contain a larger danger.

The players retain the choice of what to do about it.

## Agency guardrail

Scale must not erase significance.

Good:

> The party destroyed one of the three known gate engines. The recovered plans reveal the other two.

Poor:

> The gate they destroyed never mattered because the real villain has infinite gates.

The first expands the campaign while preserving accomplishment.

The second deletes it.

# Combining patterns

Resolution patterns may overlap.

Common combinations include:

### Happy Ending + Villain Escapes

The objective is saved, but the antagonist remains available as future pressure.

The escape should not invalidate the successful objective.

### Villain Killed + Greater Threat

The antagonist genuinely dies, and their records reveal the organization behind them.

The dead villain remains dead.

### Heroes Escape + Greater Threat

The party survives a failed or partial Climax and carries information revealing the scale of the danger.

Preserve both the survival and the failure.

### Villain Surrenders + Ending Cliffhanger

The current conflict ends through surrender; a distinct established pressure interrupts the aftermath.

The surrender remains real.

# Sandbox consequence lenses

The source patterns describe broad ending shapes. For sandbox preparation, use these **lenses** to determine what fallout deserves attention without inventing additional resolution types.

## Ownership

Who controls the objective, place, evidence, title, prisoner, resource, or institution now?

## Cost

What was spent, destroyed, sacrificed, exposed, promised, or made unavailable to achieve the result?

## Exposure

Who now knows something they did not know before?

What becomes public?

What remains secret?

## Relationship

Which existing bond, loyalty, rivalry, debt, oath, or alliance has objectively changed?

## Vacuum

What role, territory, authority, market, office, or power structure has lost its previous holder?

Do not decide who eventually fills a vacuum unless that outcome is already determined.

## Obligation

What promise, reward, debt, bargain, prosecution, oath, or responsibility now comes due?

## Pressure

What established force remains capable of acting after the adventure ends?

These lenses diagnose fallout.

They do not require every Resolution to contain every category.

# Pattern selection test

Before choosing a Resolution pattern, answer:

1. What central question did the Climax answer?
2. What was the actual answer?
3. What did the players materially accomplish?
4. What did they materially lose?
5. Which antagonist states are established?
6. Which immediate consequences follow causally?
7. Which player-gravity anchors changed?
8. Does any remaining danger represent the **same unresolved question** or a **new future pressure**?

If question 8 returns "same unresolved question," return to Climax.

If the central answer stands and only consequences remain, proceed with Resolution.

The governing principle across every pattern is:

**Honor the answer. Show the fallout. Leave the next choice open.**
