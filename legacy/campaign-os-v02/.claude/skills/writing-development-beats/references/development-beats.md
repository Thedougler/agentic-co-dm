# Development Beat Reference

Consult this file when selecting a Development pattern, resolving classification edge cases, designing a sandbox-safe implementation, or checking pacing.

The procedure belongs in `../SKILL.md`. This file owns definitions, pattern reference, and design tests.

## 1. Development semantics

In *Scripting the Game*, a Development is the non-physical-conflict Beat that moves the storyline forward: clues, revelations, conversations, warnings, character developments, advantages, relationships, and other changes that redirect subsequent action.

Its job is not merely exposition.

A good Development changes what the characters can:

- know;
- want;
- fear;
- attempt;
- exploit;
- trust;
- oppose;
- obtain;
- reconsider.

The useful question is:

> **What becomes actionable after this Beat that was not actionable before it?**

That answer is the **Delta**.

### Development versus inactivity

A Development can contain:

- negotiation;
- investigation;
- deduction;
- social interaction;
- difficult choices;
- travel;
- training;
- discovery;
- emotional consequences;
- political maneuvering;
- puzzles;
- dangerous information.

"Non-action Beat" does not mean low stakes, passive narration, or a cutscene.

### Development versus Cliffhanger

Classify by the scene's dramatic engine.

**Development**

The engine is a change in knowledge, relationship, capability, opportunity, or situation.

**Cliffhanger**

The engine is an uncertain contest or physical peril: chase, pursuit, race, battle, duel, ambush, dangerous obstacle, or similar action.

A conversation normally remains a Development when its purpose is revelation, alliance, bargaining, warning, or relationship change.

*Scripting the Game* classifies **Confrontation** as a Cliffhanger even though it can be verbal: its engine is an uncertain contest of intimidation and dominance rather than information or relationship development.

Player action can transform one category into another at runtime. Prep the initial situation honestly and let actual play decide what happens.

## 2. The sandbox translation

The Beat Chart provides **dramatic architecture**. It does not grant the preparer ownership of player decisions.

Use four concepts:

| Concept | Question |
|---|---|
| **Gravity** | Why do these characters already care? |
| **Delta** | What can become different here? |
| **Surface** | What meaningful directions can the players take? |
| **Pressure** | What does the world do without waiting for them? |

This translates linear-looking Beat construction into sandbox-compatible situation prep.

### Irrigation

Brennan Lee Mulligan's player-agency framing treats the players as water moving toward what matters to them. Preparation shapes the terrain around those existing desires rather than dictating their route.

For a Development:

1. find something the players have already demonstrated investment in;
2. place a consequential change across that Gravity;
3. make the change legible;
4. give involved actors real motives;
5. let the players decide what to do with it.

The route remains theirs.

### Link of Relevance

Every prepared Development should be able to state why it intersects established player Gravity.

Useful anchors include:

- a stated PC goal;
- a fear;
- a rival or enemy from backstory;
- a loved or trusted NPC;
- a promise;
- an oath;
- a debt;
- a mystery the player has actively pursued;
- an identity question;
- an item or place the character values;
- a faction relationship;
- a decision made during play;
- a consequence the players caused;
- a current objective the party voluntarily adopted.

Prefer evidence of investment over generic importance.

Weak:

> The cult threatens the kingdom.

Stronger:

> The cult's new oracle bears the same mark [[Ilyra]] has been investigating since her sister disappeared.

The second does not require Ilyra to engage. It explains why engagement is predictably interesting.

## 3. Pattern picker

Choose the pattern whose **dramatic function** matches the Delta.

### Information and understanding

| Pattern | Use when |
|---|---|
| **Warning** | The characters should explicitly learn the nature of a coming threat. |
| **Hidden Weakness** | The characters can learn a specific exploitable weakness of the opposition. |
| **Revelation** | The characters can learn the broad truth of what is happening. |
| **Advantage Revealed** | An existing tool, ability, object, technique, or circumstance becomes newly useful. |
| **Clue** | One meaningful piece of the larger puzzle becomes available without revealing the whole answer. |
| **Foreshadowing** | The campaign needs an ambiguous sign of something still ahead. |
| **Lie Revealed** | Something previously accepted becomes demonstrably false without necessarily revealing the truth. |
| **Not What It Seems** | A previous event gains a different meaning that was already supported by the underlying fiction. |

#### Warning

A Warning is explicit.

The players should understand what the danger is, even if they do not know every detail.

Sandbox implementation:

- let a credible actor, document, event, or signal make the threat legible;
- let the players decide what priority the warning deserves;
- move the threatened actor or event forward if ignored.

#### Hidden Weakness

Reveal a concrete vulnerability: physical weakness, fear, dependency, traitor, special weapon, political liability, ritual condition, logistical constraint, or similar exploit.

The weakness creates an option. It does not decide whether the characters use it.

#### Revelation

Use when exposing the larger truth improves play rather than destroying a mystery whose uncertainty is still doing useful work.

A Revelation answers a major question.

It should generate action rather than simply end curiosity.

#### Advantage Revealed

The advantage already exists but becomes newly understood or accessible.

Strong uses often reward earlier player choices: an item they kept, ally they protected, fact they remembered, or technique they learned becomes valuable now.

#### Clue

A Clue reveals part of the truth.

It should narrow possibilities or create a new line of action.

Avoid treating a single fragile access method as the only way the campaign can continue. The Development is the availability of meaningful information, not the requirement that players guess one exact interaction.

#### Foreshadowing

Foreshadowing is ambiguous.

It works best early enough for anticipation to develop.

Dreams, strange behavior, environmental signs, rumors, symbolic repetition, or a non-hostile encounter with a future threat can all perform this function.

#### Lie Revealed

The Delta is:

> What we believed is false.

The truth may remain unknown.

This is especially useful for reopening a solved-looking problem without replacing one forced answer with another.

#### Not What It Seems

The reinterpretation should arise from facts already compatible with the original event.

Seed enough evidence that the later reinterpretation feels like revelation rather than retroactive replacement.

## 4. Relationship and allegiance patterns

| Pattern | Use when |
|---|---|
| **Alliance** | Outside help becomes available. |
| **Betrayal** | A trusted or cooperating actor acts against the party. |
| **Strange Bedfellows** | A common threat makes temporary cooperation with opposition plausible. |
| **Turnabout!** | Someone within the opposition chooses to aid the party. |
| **Romance** | A romantic relationship becomes relevant to the situation. |
| **Obsession** | An NPC develops persistent personal fixation on a character. |

### Alliance

Allies expand the party's resources without solving the campaign for them.

Give the ally:

- a reason to help;
- limits;
- interests of their own;
- something they can contribute that changes the Surface.

### Betrayal

A betrayal changes trust, information, resources, position, or relationships.

*Scripting the Game* explicitly expects the players to have a chance to stop the betrayer during the act or catch them soon afterward.

In sandbox prep, establish:

- what the betrayer wants;
- what they are willing to risk;
- what evidence their actions produce;
- how discovery can occur;
- what happens if they succeed;
- what happens if the party catches them early.

The betrayal is an NPC decision. The players' reaction remains open.

### Strange Bedfellows

The common threat must be compelling enough that cooperation is rational for both sides.

Give both parties independent reasons to cooperate and independent reasons to distrust each other.

Temporary cooperation should create choices, not erase previous conflict.

### Turnabout!

An opposing actor changes sides for their own reason.

Useful motives include:

- ambition;
- survival;
- conscience;
- revenge;
- rivalry;
- love;
- ideological fracture;
- fear of the greater threat.

The turn should belong to the NPC's established motive rather than appearing solely because the party needs help.

### Romance

Treat romance as relationship play, not a compulsory reward or complication.

Anchor it in established table boundaries and mutual fictional interest. Preserve player ownership over their character's feelings and response.

### Obsession

The obsessed NPC seeks proximity, attention, understanding, possession, revenge, or some other fixation while generally avoiding a straightforward attack.

Use the obsession to create recurring pressure and relationship complexity without deciding how the targeted PC feels about it.

## 5. Pressure and reversal patterns

| Pattern | Use when |
|---|---|
| **Retreat** | Opposition unexpectedly breaks off a previous action and withdraws. |
| **Hesitation** | Opposition suspends aggression without leaving and may negotiate. |
| **Personal Stake** | The existing conflict becomes specifically costly to a character. |
| **Second Chance** | A failed or missed path gains another legitimate opening. |
| **Sabotage!** | Something important to the party is secretly disabled or compromised. |
| **Framed!** | Convincing evidence makes authorities believe the party committed an offense. |
| **Back from the Dead** | A previously defeated opponent credibly returns. |
| **Rescuers!** | Someone meaningful needs rescue. |
| **Vengeance!** | An opponent begins specifically targeting a party member. |

### Retreat

Usually follows a Cliffhanger.

The opposition has a real reason to leave: changed orders, a more important threat, betrayal, depleted resources, discovery of new information, or another believable shift.

The delaying circumstance preventing immediate pursuit should emerge from the fiction rather than exist solely to deny player action.

### Hesitation

The opposition stops escalating but remains present.

This creates a Surface for negotiation, recruitment, bluffing, information exchange, repositioning, or betrayal.

### Personal Stake

Attach an existing conflict to something personally relevant.

Examples:

- someone valued is threatened;
- a PC is poisoned;
- the opposition compromises a PC's reputation;
- a character's homeland becomes involved;
- an unresolved backstory problem collides with the current situation.

Personal Stake is especially compatible with Gravity because its purpose is to convert abstract stakes into established character relevance.

### Second Chance

Use after a major setback, missed clue, or collapsed approach when the fiction contains another legitimate avenue.

The second opportunity should not erase the previous failure.

Good second chances change the price, timing, information, allies, or circumstances.

Use sparingly so failure continues to matter.

### Sabotage!

The party should have a meaningful possibility of detecting, preventing, mitigating, or repairing the sabotage.

Prepare the saboteur's method and evidence before deciding whether they succeed.

### Framed!

The frame is convincing to the relevant authorities while the party knows they did not commit the act.

Interesting play comes from what the characters do with this asymmetry.

### Back from the Dead

Establish a credible survival mechanism.

The return should alter the situation rather than merely replay an earlier fight.

*Scripting the Game* recommends using this rarely.

### Rescuers!

The endangered person needs a compelling Link of Relevance: emotional importance, necessary knowledge, political significance, debt, or another established reason.

The players still decide whether and how to intervene.

### Vengeance!

An opposition actor develops a specific vendetta against one character.

The Development establishes the targeting relationship and its consequences. Any resulting uncertain fight is a separate Cliffhanger.

## 6. Access, capability, and transitional patterns

| Pattern | Use when |
|---|---|
| **Secret Meeting** | A covert encounter can provide allies or information. |
| **Mistaken Identity** | A character is treated as an important different person. |
| **Villain's Monologue** | A secure opposition position makes revealing information plausible. |
| **Gain Mastery** | Training or practice can provide capability needed later. |
| **Hazardous Quest** | Reaching, finding, destroying, or recovering something requires an arduous journey. |
| **Puzzle** | Solving a non-opposed problem gates access or understanding. |

### Secret Meeting

The interesting material includes:

- arranging contact;
- deciding whom to trust;
- reaching the meeting;
- what the other party wants;
- what information or alliance is available.

An interruption by action can become a distinct Cliffhanger.

### Mistaken Identity

The mistaken identity should matter.

It may produce:

- access;
- danger;
- information;
- unexpected allies;
- romantic complications;
- enemies;
- authority;
- obligations.

Let players decide whether to correct, exploit, investigate, or avoid the mistake.

### Villain's Monologue

The original pattern assumes the opposition believes the characters are securely under control.

This is dangerous in sandbox prep if capture or helplessness has not actually happened.

Use it only when the fictional position already supports the villain feeling safe enough to talk. Never force capture merely to unlock exposition.

Information can instead emerge through documents, overheard discussion, defectors, interrogation, boasting in another context, or other fictionally supported channels.

### Gain Mastery

Compress repetitive practice and focus on meaningful moments:

- what is difficult;
- what the teacher demands;
- what the learner discovers;
- what new capability becomes available;
- what cost or relationship develops.

The Delta is capability, not completion of a mandatory training montage.

### Hazardous Quest

The journey itself changes access and situation.

The original pattern allows environmental hazards and minor threats unrelated to the primary opposition, and it may begin, end, or be interrupted by a separate action Beat.

When an individual physical danger becomes the dramatic centerpiece, split that portion into a Cliffhanger.

### Puzzle

A Puzzle has a real solution and sufficient clues to that solution in the current or earlier material.

The challenge is not an opposed contest.

Avoid hiding campaign continuation behind an unknowable answer. The puzzle can alter access, cost, advantage, or route without making one moment of player insight the only way the campaign remains playable.

## 7. Pattern combinations

One Development can contain several ingredients, but choose **one primary pattern**.

Examples:

**Secret Meeting → Alliance**

The scene is primarily about obtaining allies; the secret meeting is its delivery structure.

Primary pattern: **Alliance**

**Clue + Personal Stake**

The discovered clue implicates a PC's missing parent.

If the important Delta is new information:

Primary pattern: **Clue**

If the important Delta is that the conflict has become personally urgent:

Primary pattern: **Personal Stake**

**Lie Revealed + Not What It Seems**

Use **Lie Revealed** when the key change is knowing the old account was false.

Use **Not What It Seems** when the key change is understanding what the earlier event actually meant.

Primary pattern keeps the Beat legible to both agent and DM.

## 8. Surface design

A useful Surface does not predict every player choice.

It ensures the situation survives choices the preparer did not predict.

### Three-vector test

Privately test:

1. **Engage differently** — can the party approach the problem through at least two substantially different methods?
2. **Reframe** — can new information cause them to pursue a different objective from the one the preparer expected?
3. **Decline** — can they walk away, postpone, or prioritize something else while the world continues coherently?

Passing the test does not require writing exhaustive branches.

It requires enough NPC motive, world state, and Pressure that the DM can adjudicate unexpected action.

### Actor-first consequences

Prefer:

> Vesk needs the ledger destroyed before sunrise. If he learns the party has it, he first tries to buy it; if that fails, he alerts Captain Sera.

Over:

> The party refuses Vesk's offer, forcing them to meet Captain Sera next.

The first describes autonomous actors.

The second authors player behavior.

### Pressure

Pressure answers:

> What changes if the party does nothing?

Useful Pressure includes:

- an NPC leaves;
- a faction completes a move;
- evidence disappears;
- a relationship cools;
- authorities arrive;
- a rival obtains the opportunity;
- a rumor spreads;
- a deadline passes;
- the threat changes location;
- a resource becomes harder to access.

Pressure makes time meaningful while preserving refusal.

## 9. Beat rhythm

The core *Scripting the Game* structure is:

`Hook → Development ↔ Cliffhanger → Climax → Resolution`

Development and Cliffhanger provide alternating changes of dramatic texture.

Useful pacing relationships:

| Previous Beat | Development fit |
|---|---|
| Action-heavy Hook | Strong |
| Cerebral Hook | Usually prefer a Cliffhanger first |
| Cliffhanger | Strong / expected |
| Development | Avoid consecutive placement on the same authored path |
| Action-oriented Climax next | Development immediately before it fits well |

If the Climax is principally mental or revelatory, *Scripting the Game* recommends action immediately before it rather than another cerebral Beat.

A Development can causally seed another Development much later. Causal dependency does not require consecutive placement.

### Sandbox branching

A sandbox may have several prepared Beat candidates at once:

```text
                   ┌─ Development A ─ Cliffhanger A ─┐
Current Situation ─┤                                ├─ changed campaign state
                   └─ Development B ─ Cliffhanger B ─┘
```

The party chooses the route through play.

Do not reorder unrelated content behind the screen merely to make an abandoned route occur anyway.

Reintroduce material when its actors and causes would independently bring it back into the fiction.

## 10. Runtime budget

*Scripting the Game* gives approximately **30 minutes of table time per Beat** as a planning heuristic.

Use this as scale control, not a stopwatch.

A Development usually needs enough material for:

- orientation;
- meaningful interaction or discovery;
- player decision;
- a changed situation;
- an exit into continued play.

If the note contains several independent revelations, negotiations, relationship turns, and decisions that could each carry substantial table time, it may contain multiple Beats disguised as one.

## 11. Human-DM scan order

When repository templates permit discretion, put operational information first.

Recommended semantic order:

### At a Glance

- **Pattern:**
- **Gravity:**
- **Delta:**
- **Pressure:**

### Situation

What is true as the Beat becomes available.

### Actors

For each important actor:

- wants;
- knows;
- fears or constraints;
- what they do without PC intervention.

### What Can Change

The information, relationship, resource, capability, or circumstance available through this Development.

### Surface

Important affordances and obvious vectors of interaction.

Do not attempt to enumerate every possible player action.

### Conditional Reactions

Short actor/world reactions to likely categories of intervention.

### Carry Forward

Consequences, unresolved questions, action opportunities, or later Beats this Development may seed.

### Supporting Detail

Descriptions, dialogue cues, clues, mechanics, or other material that helps at the table but is not necessary to understand the Beat.

## 12. Canon and prep

A prepared Beat is not evidence that an event occurred.

Maintain the distinction:

**Compiled canon**

What authoritative campaign pages currently establish.

**Prepared state**

NPC intentions, opportunities, possible discoveries, contingencies, future pressure, and hypothetical outcomes.

**Played state**

What session evidence later establishes actually happened.

Write prepared actors positively and concretely:

> [[Sava]] intends to offer the map if the party protects her brother.

Write hypothetical outcomes conditionally:

> If the party accepts, Sava can reveal the western route.

After play, let the session-evidence ingest workflow determine which branches became fact.

Unused prep stays unused.

## 13. Quality test

A Development is strong when the answers are immediate:

**Gravity**  
Why are these characters likely to care?

**Delta**  
What can be meaningfully different afterward?

**Surface**  
Can the players engage in more than one meaningful way?

**Pressure**  
What happens if they choose something else?

**Agency**  
Does the page describe the world and its actors rather than authoring the PCs?

**Pacing**  
Does the Beat change texture from the surrounding action?

**Continuity**  
Do its consequences arise from established fiction?

**Runtime**  
Can the DM understand the playable core at a glance?

**Canon**  
Can every established fact be traced to maintained campaign material while future events remain conditional?

When all nine answers are clear, the Development is ready.
