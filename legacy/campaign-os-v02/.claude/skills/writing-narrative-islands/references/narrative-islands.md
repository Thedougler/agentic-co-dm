# Narrative Islands: Design Reference

This reference defines the structural model behind Campaign OS narrative islands.

Use it when:

- turning a linear adventure into sandbox material;
- deciding whether two situations should be separate islands;
- designing connections between islands;
- diagnosing hidden railroading;
- deciding how beats belong inside an island;
- auditing player agency;
- repairing islands that feel static, mandatory, or disconnected.

## Source note

**Narrative island** is the operational term used by this system.

The underlying philosophy is a synthesis of Brennan Lee Mulligan's documented approach to player agency and narrative structure, plus modular situation design for a persistent campaign wiki. The exact phrase *narrative island* should not be attributed to Mulligan unless a primary source establishing that terminology is added later.

In the *Game Masters of Exandria Roundtable*, Mulligan describes the identity and motivations established by the players as the source of the campaign's rails: **“The rails are gonna be who you tell me you are.”**

He later summarizes the PC side of the problem as **“Players are like water.”** The character pursues their goal as directly as possible, while the GM shapes a more interesting route through obstacles, complications, and consequences that remain compatible with that goal.

The practical consequence is fundamental:

> **Prepare the terrain through which desire moves. Do not prepare the route the player must take.**

## The archipelago model

A sandbox campaign is an **archipelago**.

Each narrative island is a locally coherent situation.

The sea between them represents player freedom: travel, investigation, downtime, changing priorities, unexpected plans, and the possibility of sailing somewhere the DM did not expect.

The campaign is therefore not:

> A → B → C → D → finale

It is closer to:

```text
          [B]
         /   \
[A] ----       ---- [E]
  \      [C]       /
   \      |       /
    -----[D]-----
```

Even this diagram is only a snapshot.

As play proceeds:

- islands change;
- bridges appear and disappear;
- new islands emerge;
- old islands resolve;
- factions move;
- consequences create new connections;
- players discover routes nobody prepared.

The graph records **possibility**, not destiny.

## Leading words

Use these words consistently. Each carries a specific design function.

### Island

A bounded situation worth engaging as a coherent dramatic unit.

An island may contain locations, NPCs, factions, encounters, secrets, hazards, and beats.

Its boundary is conceptual rather than necessarily geographic.

A royal succession crisis can be one island across an entire kingdom.

A single haunted manor can contain several islands if multiple independent situations operate inside it.

**Test:** if one part can substantially change or resolve without changing the others, they may be separate islands.

### Gravity

The existing desires that make an island attractive to the PCs.

Gravity comes from:

- character goals;
- relationships;
- curiosity;
- principles;
- debts;
- fears;
- resources;
- unresolved questions;
- prior consequences.

Gravity is stronger when the campaign does not need to invent a new reason for the PCs to care.

A player's character creation and subsequent choices are therefore campaign-planning inputs.

### Bridge

A fictional connection through which PCs can discover, enter, leave, or move between islands.

A bridge may be:

- a road;
- a rumor;
- an NPC;
- a faction;
- a clue;
- a ship;
- a political relationship;
- a shared enemy;
- an artifact;
- an obligation;
- the wake of another island.

Bridges belong to the fiction.

If the PCs burn one, the graph changes.

### Tide

The process by which the situation moves without the PCs.

A tide answers:

> What happens next if nobody stops this?

A tide may be discrete:

```text
1. Cult begins gathering components.
2. First disappearances occur.
3. Authorities impose a curfew.
4. Ritual site becomes operational.
5. Ritual begins.
```

Or reactive:

```text
If the duke learns the spies were discovered:
- relocate the prisoners;
- accuse the rival house;
- accelerate the vote.
```

Use the simplest representation that preserves meaningful change.

### Wake

Persistent consequences leaving the island.

A wake may alter:

- another island;
- an NPC;
- a faction;
- a settlement;
- political conditions;
- available resources;
- public knowledge;
- reputation;
- travel;
- future threats.

The wake is how isolated adventures become a campaign.

## The Mulligan tension

The player and the character want related but different things.

The **character** normally wants:

- safety;
- success;
- answers;
- victory;
- reunion;
- justice;
- money;
- escape;
- power;
- protection of someone they love.

They pursue these goals efficiently.

The **player** normally also wants:

- difficulty;
- discovery;
- reversals;
- meaningful choices;
- uncertainty;
- emotional consequences;
- earned victories;
- transformation.

The GM serves both.

If the character wants to reach the prince before the assassination, the useful preparation is not:

> The prince survives until scene six because the confrontation is scheduled there.

Useful preparation is:

- where the prince is;
- what the assassins know;
- how quickly they can act;
- who controls access;
- what routes exist;
- who can be persuaded;
- what warning signs appear;
- what happens if the party is early;
- what happens if they are late.

The character may then pursue the prince by the shortest route they can find.

The structure creates the adventure.

## Situation, not story

A **story** describes events after they happen.

A **situation** describes forces before the outcome is known.

Compare:

### Plot preparation

> The party investigates the missing miners, discovers a cult beneath the quarry, defeats the cultists, learns the mayor financed them, and confronts him at the festival.

The PCs already exist inside the verbs.

### Island preparation

> Miners are disappearing because the quarry guild is feeding prisoners to something beneath the old workings. The mayor secretly funds the guild because the creature predicts ore veins and the town depends on the resulting wealth. The guild needs six more victims before the midsummer survey.

Now the important verbs belong to the world.

The PCs might:

- rescue miners;
- expose the mayor;
- bargain with the creature;
- kill it;
- seize the operation;
- blackmail the guild;
- evacuate the town;
- join the conspiracy;
- ignore it;
- accidentally worsen it.

Preparation survives all of those choices.

## Island anatomy

A strong island usually contains seven interacting structures.

### 1. An unresolved question

Something important is genuinely undetermined.

Examples:

- Who gains control?
- Does the secret survive?
- Who gets rescued?
- What becomes of the weapon?
- Does the alliance hold?
- Is the settlement abandoned?
- Which faction gains legitimacy?

The answer must emerge from play.

### 2. Active forces

At least two things want outcomes that cannot all happen simultaneously.

These need not all be people.

Possible forces include:

- rival factions;
- natural hazards;
- limited time;
- law;
- disease;
- scarcity;
- a monster;
- supernatural rules;
- incompatible promises.

Conflict emerges from incompatible motion.

### 3. Gravity

The PCs possess reasons to care before the island asks them to.

### 4. Bridges

The situation can be discovered and approached from several directions.

### 5. Affordances

The world contains exploitable facts.

### 6. Tide

The situation evolves.

### 7. Wake

The resulting state matters elsewhere.

Remove several of these and the island begins behaving like a conventional scripted encounter.

## Affordances

An **affordance** is a fact that enables action.

Good preparation contains facts with verbs hiding inside them.

> The western wall is cracked.

Players may break it, listen through it, climb it, reinforce it, hide something inside it, or convince someone it is unsafe.

> Captain Varo owes the smugglers his life.

Players may pressure him, appeal to loyalty, expose him, protect the smugglers, fabricate evidence, or discover why the debt exists.

> The revenant cannot cross running water.

Players may flee across it, redirect it, trap the revenant, destroy a bridge, flood a chamber, or exploit the limitation in some unanticipated way.

Affordances produce improvisational capacity.

A prescribed solution produces one answer.

## Bridges and discovery

Meaningful sandbox choice requires **information**.

An undiscovered island cannot be chosen.

A bridge therefore performs two jobs:

1. it reveals that something exists;
2. it tells the players enough to judge whether they care.

Compare:

> A stranger asks you to investigate disappearances.

with:

> A quarry worker says three miners vanished after refusing midnight shifts. The guild doubled everyone's hazard pay the next morning.

The second bridge exposes:

- a mystery;
- an institution;
- suspicious behavior;
- money;
- a possible risk;
- several implied approaches.

It gives the players material from which to form intent.

## Redundant bridges

Important situations benefit from multiple discovery vectors.

This is not because players must eventually reach the island.

It is because robust fictional information should have multiple consequences.

If a gang is extorting an entire harbor:

- merchants complain;
- prices rise;
- guards are bribed;
- bruised dockworkers appear;
- rival criminals react;
- ships change ports;
- rumors spread.

Any of these can become a bridge.

If the entire island disappears because one messenger dies, the world was insufficiently connected.

## Choice changes position

Two choices are meaningfully different when they produce different:

- information;
- costs;
- relationships;
- timing;
- resources;
- risks;
- leverage;
- world states.

Two doors leading to the same room can still matter if entering through them changes the situation.

Two roads that secretly produce the same ambush, same NPC, same information, and same consequences are one road wearing two costumes.

## Anchoring

Prepared content should have a fictional home.

An NPC exists somewhere because that is where the NPC chose or needed to be.

A monster guards something because it has a reason to remain there.

A battle happens because forces collide.

A clue exists because some event produced evidence.

When content moves, let **causality** move it.

Example:

The players avoid the fortress.

The enemy commander may later leave the fortress because:

- they are pursuing the PCs;
- another faction summoned them;
- their plan entered a new phase;
- the fortress fell;
- they were reassigned.

That movement transforms the situation.

Simply placing the prepared fortress encounter in the next cave preserves prep by erasing player choice.

## The quantum island

A **quantum island** exists wherever the players choose to go.

It produces a false archipelago:

```text
Choose A → Prepared Encounter
Choose B → Prepared Encounter
Choose C → Prepared Encounter
```

The map displays three choices.

The world recognizes one.

Replace quantum structure with **anchored mobility**:

```text
Choose A → encounter A
Choose B → encounter B
Choose C → encounter C

Meanwhile:
Prepared enemy reacts according to its own agenda.
```

The reusable preparation is the **actor and situation**, not an encounter's forced location.

## The frozen island

A frozen island waits indefinitely for the PCs.

> The cult will perform the ritual the night after the PCs arrive.

Whether the party comes tomorrow or six months later, the same content waits untouched.

Replace the freeze with a **tide**.

The cult has a schedule determined by its own requirements.

Arrival time then changes the adventure:

- early: components are still being gathered;
- midway: disappearances expose the operation;
- late: defenses are complete;
- after completion: the campaign deals with the result.

Delay becomes a meaningful choice.

## The keyhole island

A keyhole island has one valid entrance.

Examples:

- one clue;
- one NPC;
- one password;
- one required skill check;
- one intended strategy.

Replace the keyhole with **redundant bridges and affordances**.

The objective may remain difficult.

Access to meaningful play should survive ordinary failure and creativity.

## The island prison

An island prison can be entered freely but only exited by completing the author's plot.

Replace the prison with **stateful departure**.

If the PCs leave:

- the antagonist advances;
- the victim remains captured;
- another faction intervenes;
- the problem travels;
- a relationship changes;
- the opportunity expires.

Leaving becomes part of the story.

## The choice mirage

A choice mirage presents alternatives whose consequences immediately collapse.

```text
Support the duke ─┐
Support the guild ├─> same battle, same ruler, same reward
Support neither ──┘
```

Replace the mirage with **persistent state**.

The campaign need not branch into three separate books.

Small persistent differences are enough:

- who trusts the party;
- who holds the key;
- what rumor spreads;
- which faction controls a district;
- who owes whom;
- what resource becomes available.

Persistent state makes choices accumulate.

## The lore island

A lore island contains information but no active situation.

It may be fascinating and still provide little play.

Convert lore into **pressure**.

Instead of:

> The two houses have hated each other for centuries.

Ask:

> What are they doing about that hatred this week?

Instead of:

> The lighthouse contains an imprisoned storm god.

Ask:

> Who currently benefits from keeping it imprisoned, and what just changed?

Lore becomes playable when somebody wants something now.

## Island density

An island should be large enough to generate choices and small enough to hold one coherent dramatic question.

Split an island when:

- its conflicts can resolve independently;
- separate actor groups have little causal interaction;
- one section can be ignored without affecting the rest;
- its tides operate on unrelated timescales;
- the DM cannot summarize the situation in one sentence.

Merge islands when:

- they share the same dramatic question;
- the same forces drive both;
- changing one necessarily changes the other;
- separate pages would repeat the same state.

The goal is not maximum atomicity.

The goal is **causal coherence**.

## Archipelago topology

Several recurring arrangements are useful.

### Open archipelago

Several islands are simultaneously available.

```text
     [B]
      |
[A]  [C]  [D]
      |
     [E]
```

Best for:

- exploration;
- faction play;
- city campaigns;
- regional sandboxes.

Player priorities determine campaign order.

### Braided structure

Several routes interact repeatedly.

```text
[A] ── [C] ── [E]
  \   /   \   /
   [B] ── [D]
```

Best for:

- mysteries;
- politics;
- rival factions;
- interconnected character arcs.

Actions in one island alter available approaches elsewhere.

### Gravity well

Several islands interact with one major situation.

```text
[A] \
[B] -- [CORE]
[C] /
[D]
```

Useful for wars, revolutions, elections, disasters, or major villains.

The core situation influences everything without requiring all paths to enter the same scene.

### Chain formed by play

A linear sequence is valid when the **players create it**.

```text
[A] → [C] → [F] → [H]
```

The distinction is historical:

- Before play, many possibilities existed.
- Player decisions made this route real.
- The wiki now records it as campaign history.

Emergent linearity is compatible with sandbox play.

Predetermined linearity is a different structure.

## Narrative convergence

Sandbox structure does not require endless divergence.

Independent paths may converge when the **fiction** creates convergence.

Examples:

- every faction needs the same artifact;
- a hurricane forces ships into one harbor;
- an election has a fixed date;
- an invading army reaches the capital;
- several investigations identify the same conspirator.

Convergence is strong when players can arrive:

- with different allies;
- with different information;
- at different times;
- with different resources;
- under different relationships;
- after causing different world states.

The location may converge.

The campaign state does not.

## Character-authored rails

Character creation supplies **gravity**.

If a player creates:

- a revolutionary, create institutions worth opposing;
- a detective, create truths worth uncovering;
- an heir, create claims and responsibilities;
- a coward seeking courage, create situations where fear matters;
- a cleric questioning their faith, create competing interpretations and consequences.

This does not mean assigning each PC a private plot.

Interweave gravity.

The strongest island often pulls several PCs for different reasons.

Example:

A corrupt admiral may simultaneously be:

- the rogue's former commander;
- the paladin's lawful superior;
- the wizard's only source for forbidden charts;
- the cleric's most influential political ally.

One island now creates several incompatible forms of gravity.

The DM does not need to compel participation.

The characters bring the rails with them.

## Obstacles as irrigation

An obstacle should deform the route without invalidating the goal.

The character wants the straight line.

The world supplies:

- scarcity;
- competing interests;
- incomplete information;
- moral cost;
- geography;
- hostile actors;
- social obligations;
- time;
- uncertainty.

The obstacle says:

> You may continue toward what you want, but the world makes that pursuit consequential.

A weak obstacle says:

> You cannot pursue what you want because the prepared adventure is elsewhere.

## Failure

Failure changes state.

A failed roll might produce:

- delay;
- exposure;
- cost;
- lost leverage;
- partial information;
- hostile attention;
- collateral damage;
- a harder position.

Use hard blockage when the fiction genuinely requires it.

The campaign then continues from the blocked state.

Failure should normally create **new topology**, not remove play.

## Ignoring an island

Ignoring content is one of the sandbox's most important choices.

When an island is ignored:

1. advance its tide when appropriate;
2. update its active forces;
3. propagate visible consequences;
4. create or destroy bridges accordingly;
5. let the resulting wake reach the rest of the campaign.

The consequence is not punishment for choosing incorrectly.

It is the world continuing to exist.

## Returning to an island

A revisited island should remember.

Update:

- actors;
- leverage;
- relationships;
- physical conditions;
- public knowledge;
- remaining opportunities;
- tide position;
- bridges.

A living campaign rewards memory because the world has memory.

## Narrative islands and Scripting the Game beats

*Scripting the Game* defines five Beat types:

1. Hook
2. Development
3. Cliffhanger
4. Climax
5. Resolution

Its formal Beat Chart begins with a Hook, ends with a Climax followed by Resolution, and uses Developments and Cliffhangers as its repeating middle rhythm.

Narrative islands use this vocabulary at a **different structural layer**.

### Island

Answers:

> What situation exists and how can it change?

### Beat

Answers:

> What dramatic function is this moment currently serving?

Do not turn every island into a mandatory Beat Chart.

Instead, prepare likely or useful beat **candidates**.

Example:

```markdown
## Candidate Beats

- **Hook:** A wounded customs officer offers the party a confiscated ledger.
- **Development:** The ledger identifies three captains paying the same unknown broker.
- **Cliffhanger:** The broker's people attempt to recover it if word of the ledger spreads.
- **Development:** A captured attacker reveals the broker is financing both rival fleets.
- **Climax:** Becomes possible when the party forces the broker and either fleet into open conflict.
- **Resolution:** Record who controls the shipping lanes and what becomes publicly known.
```

Those are prepared possibilities.

If the PCs burn the ledger immediately, the prepared Development disappears.

Update the situation.

Do not manufacture another copy of the ledger merely to restore the planned sequence.

### Formal Beat Charts inside islands

When a specific activated situation benefits from formal pacing, a Beat Chart may be composed locally.

Apply the source's structure to that active sequence:

```text
Hook
↓
Development / Cliffhanger
↓
alternating middle beats
↓
Climax
↓
Resolution
```

Player actions remain capable of:

- bypassing a beat;
- changing its participants;
- transforming its stakes;
- causing an earlier climax;
- avoiding the anticipated climax entirely.

When the state changes, recompose future beats from the new state.

The Beat Chart serves pacing.

The island preserves agency.

### Time budgeting

*Scripting the Game* offers roughly **30 minutes of real-world play per Beat** as a planning rule of thumb.

Use that only as a coarse capacity estimate.

An island's actual duration depends on player interest and approach.

Estimate how much prepared material could fit in a session.

Do not infer that the players must consume it.

## Session preparation

Session preparation should focus effort on islands the party can plausibly reach soon.

A useful priority order is:

1. currently occupied island;
2. islands connected by immediate bridges;
3. islands whose tides may create visible consequences;
4. islands strongly aligned with declared PC intentions;
5. distant possibilities requiring only enough definition to remain coherent.

This preserves sandbox breadth without fully detailing the entire world.

The farther an island is from likely interaction, the more abstract its preparation can remain.

## The human-facing page

The vault page serves a DM during live play.

The agent therefore compiles complexity into scan-friendly information.

Prefer:

```markdown
### Captain Varro
- **Wants:** Ledger destroyed before the tribunal.
- **Leverage:** Harbor watch, customs records.
- **Constraint:** Daughter serves aboard the accused ship.
- **Next:** Arrests the quartermaster at dawn.
```

over:

```markdown
Captain Varro is a complicated individual whose position within the political
landscape has caused him to become increasingly concerned with...
```

The first form exposes verbs.

Verbs run games.

## Wiki integration

The vault is a compiled knowledge artifact rather than a pile of session summaries. Planning should therefore reuse current canonical pages instead of re-deriving campaign state from memory.

### Link canonical entities

Use:

```markdown
[[Captain Varro]]
[[Blackwater Harbor]]
[[House Serrat]]
```

Obsidian resolves these as internal links, with aliases and heading links available when needed.

### Embed authoritative content

When another page owns information, embed or link it rather than maintaining another copy.

Examples:

```markdown
![[Captain Varro#Resources]]
![[Blackwater Harbor#Current Conditions]]
```

Use Base views for dynamic rosters and tables when available.

Static text belongs on the island page when the information is unique to the island.

### Separate canon from preparation

Existing world facts come from canon.

New secrets, motives, plans, and possible developments are DM preparation.

Follow repository conventions for player-known information, DM-only information, pending material, and canon status.

The distinction should remain obvious to later agents maintaining the wiki.

## Refactoring a linear quest

Given:

```text
King hires party
→ travel through forest
→ fight bandits
→ meet hermit
→ discover necromancer
→ enter tower
→ defeat necromancer
→ return to king
```

Extract the situation first.

### Forces

**King**
- wants border raids ended;
- believes the bandits are responsible;
- needs a quick political victory.

**Bandits**
- raid royal caravans;
- are actually refugees from villages emptied by undead attacks;
- want supplies and safe passage.

**Hermit**
- knows the dead are coming from the tower;
- refuses to approach the king after being exiled.

**Necromancer**
- excavates an ancient battlefield;
- requires corpses and uninterrupted time;
- considers both the bandits and kingdom irrelevant.

### Islands

```text
[Royal Border Crisis]
       |       \
       |        \
[Refugee Bandits] ---- [Ancient Battlefield]
       \                    /
        \                  /
          [Necromancer's Tower]
```

### Possible bridges

The PCs might learn about the tower through:

- captured bandits;
- undead tracks;
- the hermit;
- missing villagers;
- royal scouts;
- following corpse wagons;
- their own reconnaissance.

### Possible outcomes

They may:

- destroy the bandits;
- ally with them;
- expose the king's misinformation;
- ignore the political conflict;
- attack the tower early;
- bargain with the necromancer;
- evacuate threatened villages;
- convince the king to mobilize;
- seize the battlefield themselves.

The same prepared world supports all of them.

That is the purpose of the island transformation.

## Quality gradient

### Weak island

- exists only when PCs arrive;
- one hook;
- one entrance;
- one intended solution;
- actors wait;
- one expected climax;
- one expected resolution;
- consequences remain local.

### Functional island

- clear situation;
- multiple bridges;
- actors have goals;
- several approaches work;
- simple tide;
- several end states.

### Strong island

- gravity emerges from established PCs;
- actors collide without intervention;
- bridges cross-link existing campaign material;
- affordances support unexpected plans;
- ignored content evolves;
- outcomes reshape other islands;
- returning later reveals persistent change;
- the DM can run the situation from motives and state rather than scripted scenes.

## Final test

A narrative island is successful when the DM can answer:

> **What is happening?**

> **Who wants what?**

> **Why might the PCs care?**

> **How can they discover or approach it?**

> **What can they manipulate?**

> **What happens if they do nothing?**

> **What can genuinely change?**

> **What changes elsewhere afterward?**

And still cannot answer:

> **What will the players do?**

That unknown is the space where the campaign happens.
