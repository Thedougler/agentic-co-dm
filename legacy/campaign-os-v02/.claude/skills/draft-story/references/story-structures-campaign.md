# Story structures — campaign/sandbox shapes

Full shapes for the campaign/sandbox cluster of `.claude/skills/draft-story/references/story-structures.md`'s
catalog — patterns operating across sessions rather than within one scene.
Index and cross-links: `.claude/skills/draft-story/references/story-structures.md`. The quest
guide's `vault/refs/vault/quest/references/structure.md` operationalizes
several of these per-shape for quest pages.

## Episodic structure (session-to-season shape)

Loosely connected episodes, same characters — the natural shape of a
campaign season.

```text
Session 1: complete mini-arc
Session 2: different situation, same party
Session 3: another situation
Finale:    threads connect
```

**Use for**: season pages (the season guide's throughline + GM-planned
Finale), sandbox stretches where sessions self-contain but seed the
finale. Each episode still gets its own internal arc — the session-scale
shape and table-time budgets live in `.claude/skills/composing-beats/references/rhythm-and-pacing.md` § The Session
Arc; this file's session-scale variant is Brennan Lee Mulligan's tentpole
architecture — Narrative Islands — which ends on unresolved tension rather
than a tidy conclusion: a strong start drops players in medias res, 2–3
escalating scenes follow, and the long-term arc emerges from player choices
across islands, never a scripted path between them. The season-level
episodic structure above is what the beats on a season page sequence.

## Convergence Arc

3–5 independent threads — faction Fronts, PC backstory draws, world
events — each advance on their own logic and collide at a single
high-stakes point.

```text
Thread A: [Faction X pursuing Goal Y] — Timeline: 3 sessions
Thread B: [Player backstory draw — Z's brother] — Timeline: triggered by player action
Thread C: [World event — the blockade tightening] — Timeline: 2 sessions
Convergence point: Port city, when the blockade closes and the brother is in the city
```

**Don't plan the collision — plan the threads.** When 3+ threads approach
the same point in time or space, that's a convergence forming; recognize
it and lean in rather than scripting it. After convergence, threads don't
end, they transform — new threads emerge from the rubble of the old.

**Use for**: mid-campaign planning (sessions 5–15 range) — map active
threads and ask which are converging and on what timeline.

**Failure mode**: forcing the convergence by railroading threads toward
it. If players don't naturally encounter it, let the threads collide
off-screen and surface the aftermath instead.

## The Slow Burn

Seed → repetition → revelation → payoff, spread across many sessions. An
element introduced early (a name, a symbol, a rumor, an NPC quirk) recurs
in unrelated contexts until players connect it themselves.

**Don't plan the revelation — design for discoverability.** The best slow
burns pay off because players made choices that led them there, not
because the reveal landed on schedule. If players never pull the thread,
it enriches world texture instead.

**Use for**: campaign foundations — plant 2–3 slow burns in sessions 1–3.

**Failure mode**: seeding a slow burn and then forgetting it. Keep a
private list; surface repetitions deliberately.

## The Escalation Ladder

Stakes climb rung by rung as the party engages — personal → communal →
regional → existential.

```text
Rung 1 (Personal): One NPC is in danger / one player's secret is at risk
Rung 2 (Communal): A neighborhood, ship, or group faces a threat
Rung 3 (Regional): A city, faction, or institution is destabilized
Rung 4 (Existential): The world, planar reality, or a fundamental truth is threatened
```

Players climb the ladder by engaging — each engagement creates
consequences that raise the stakes one rung. Don't skip rungs; earned
escalation hits harder.

**Use for**: a single objective or threat that needs to feel like it's
building session over session, rather than flat difficulty scaling.

**Failure mode**: starting at Rung 4. Existential stakes with nothing
personal underneath read as abstract — always anchor the top rung back to
a specific PC or place.

## The Reversal Arc

A fortune or perception inversion, built as a structural condition, not
scripted as an event.

1. **Fortune reversal** — status, power, or circumstance inverts (the
   king becomes a fugitive).
2. **Perception reversal** — the player's understanding inverts (the ally
   was the enemy; the enemy was protecting them).

**Use for**: major NPC and faction design — every significant antagonist
should have at least one reversal built in as a hidden layer whose reveal
recontextualizes what the party has done so far. Build the conditions;
let players trigger it, or not.

## The Pyrrhic Arc

The party achieves the stated objective, but winning reveals or causes a
real cost.

**Never engineer this as punishment.** Pyrrhic outcomes emerge from real
consequences — if players burn down the guild, the power vacuum is real,
play it. The best pyrrhic arcs are ones players could have avoided with
different choices; the tragedy is visible in retrospect.

**Use for**: post-major-conflict prep. After any significant player
victory, ask what actually changes and who fills the vacuum.

## Parallel Threads

Several storylines advance between sessions — factions pursuing goals,
NPC lives continuing, world events advancing — whether or not players
observe them.

```text
Thread: [Name]
Current state: [What's true now]
Next step if unopposed: [What happens next session if players don't intervene]
Player draw: [How players could engage]
```

Surface threads through rumors, letters, secondhand reports; let players
choose which to engage. [[threads|Threads]] that
players ignore advance on their own timelines — this is what makes the
world feel alive.

**Use for**: world-state tracking between sessions. Maintain 3–5 active
threads and advance each by one step per session.

## The Mystery Box

A compelling unknown that creates forward momentum because players
*pursue* it — it's self-motivating, not assigned.

1. **Solvable** — a definitive answer players can find through
   investigation and deduction (needs the Three-Clue Rule,
   `.claude/skills/composing-beats/references/runtime-surface.md`).
2. **Atmospheric** — no answer, or the answer doesn't matter; it exists to
   make the world feel deep and strange.

**Use for**: world-building and location design — every major location
should have at least one mystery box of either type.

**Failure mode**: the answer is less interesting than the question. If you
can't think of a satisfying answer, make it atmospheric instead of forcing
a weak reveal.
