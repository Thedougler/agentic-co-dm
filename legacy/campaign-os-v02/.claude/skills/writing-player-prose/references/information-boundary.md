# Information Boundary

Before writing, divide all relevant facts into at least three classes.

## Hidden

Facts true in the world but not currently available to the characters.

Examples:

- the duke poisoned the wine,
- the statue is a disguised construct,
- three assassins are behind the wall,
- the priest is lying,
- the bridge collapses after 500 pounds crosses it,
- the smiling diplomat is terrified.

Do not expose these as facts unless some character has a legitimate way to know them.

## Perceivable

Facts available through the characters' senses or obvious circumstances.

Examples:

- bitter almond scent in the wine,
- fine seams around the statue's wrists,
- muffled movement behind the wall,
- the priest repeatedly glances toward the side door,
- the bridge groans beneath the fighter's weight,
- the diplomat's glass trembles against her teeth.

These form the basis of most player-facing prose.

## Knowable

Facts a character may recognize because of established knowledge, expertise, culture, background, magic, or prior experience.

Examples:

- a cleric recognizes forbidden funerary iconography,
- a sailor recognizes signs of an approaching squall,
- a noble recognizes an obsolete royal seal,
- a wizard identifies residue from teleportation magic.

If recognition is automatic for that character, state it plainly.

Do not convert obvious character competence into arbitrary guessing games.

# Perception Belongs to Characters; Deduction Belongs to Players

Default distinction:

> **Characters perceive. Players interpret.**

Describe:

- evidence,
- behavior,
- physical circumstances,
- recognizable facts,
- immediate consequences.

Avoid unnecessarily declaring:

- conclusions,
- beliefs,
- motives,
- emotional judgments,
- tactical decisions,
- moral interpretations.

Prefer:

> The rug is several inches out of line with the room. Fresh scratches arc across the floorboards beneath it, and a thread of cold air touches your ankle.

Over:

> You realize there is a secret door beneath the rug.

Unless the existence of the secret door is already obvious or automatically recognized.

# Do Not Withhold the Obvious

Preserving deduction does not mean being coy.

If the character plainly knows something, tell the player.

Bad:

> The enormous red-scaled reptilian creature opens leathery wings.

When everyone present knows what a dragon is.

Better:

> The red dragon unfolds its wings.

Likewise, do not hide basic geometry, conspicuous exits, obvious danger, known cultural information, or normal professional competence merely to manufacture mystery.

Mystery comes from **incomplete circumstances**, not incomplete communication.

# Lore Delivery

Do not bury encyclopedic exposition in narration.

Prefer:

- one useful fact,
- one name,
- one implication.

Then allow players to seek more.

When characters automatically know something, provide it cleanly:

> You recognize the uniform. These are Crown Auditors, not soldiers. Their authority begins where normal law ends.

That is superior to five paragraphs on the founding of the Royal Revenue Office.

# Proper-Noun Budget

Spoken narration becomes difficult when overloaded with unfamiliar names.

Do not introduce several new:

- people,
- nations,
- gods,
- factions,
- historical events,
- locations,

in one spoken unit unless unavoidable.

Anchor unknown proper nouns to comprehensible categories:

> Sister Malvera, the abbey's physician.

Later:

> Malvera.

# Secrets and Clues

Prepare secrets as portable facts rather than predetermined revelation scenes.

A clue may emerge through:

- investigation,
- conversation,
- magic,
- surveillance,
- combat,
- travel,
- environmental observation,
- faction activity,
- failure consequences.

Player-facing prose should present the clue through the channel that becomes true in play.

Do not make discovery dependent on the agent's imagined sequence.

# Foreshadowing

Foreshadow through truthful details.

Good foreshadowing is usually obvious in retrospect.

Examples:

- livestock refuse to approach the eastern field,
- the mayor keeps replacement windows stacked behind his house,
- every ship in harbor has removed its bell,
- the monastery owns six funeral carts but only twelve beds.

Do not label these as foreshadowing in player-facing text.
