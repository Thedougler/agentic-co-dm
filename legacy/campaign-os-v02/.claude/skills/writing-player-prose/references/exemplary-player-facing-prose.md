# Exemplary DM Player-Facing Prose

Use this reference when `writing-player-prose` needs a deliberate **delivery mode** derived from exemplary actual-play GM narration.

This file owns the distinction between two useful modes:

- **Mercer — scenic lens:** immersive accumulation, physical continuity, spatial grounding, embodied NPCs.
- **Mulligan — dramatic cut:** concentrated emphasis, character gravity, semantic contrast, rhythmic escalation, reincorporation.

The parent `writing-player-prose` skill remains authoritative for:

- information boundaries,
- player agency,
- reaction points,
- actionable information,
- spoken readability,
- sandbox integrity,
- scene-model structure,
- prose length.

Apply an exemplar mode **after** those requirements are satisfied.

These modes distill observable techniques. They are not instructions to reproduce signature phrases, catchphrases, verbal tics, or passages from either GM.

---

# Choose the Mode

| Need | Default mode |
|---|---|
| Establish a location players must inhabit and navigate | **Mercer — scenic lens** |
| Travel, arrival, exploration, environmental wonder | **Mercer — scenic lens** |
| Make an NPC feel physically present and socially distinct | **Mercer — scenic lens** |
| Translate mechanical action into continuous physical fiction | **Mercer — scenic lens** |
| Open on danger, revelation, or an emotionally loaded moment | **Mulligan — dramatic cut** |
| Deliver a major character-specific realization | **Mulligan — dramatic cut** |
| Make one image or sentence carry exceptional dramatic weight | **Mulligan — dramatic cut** |
| Reincorporate an earlier detail into a payoff | **Mulligan — dramatic cut** |
| Villain confrontation, thematic declaration, climax | **Mulligan — dramatic cut** |
| Ordinary sandbox play without a requested exemplar | Use the parent skill without forcing either mode |

A passage may change modes.

The strongest default blend is often:

> **Mercer for orientation; Mulligan for emphasis.**

---

# Mercer — Scenic Lens

## Core

> **Let the players inhabit the place before asking the place to become dramatic.**

Mercer's player-facing narration often behaves like a camera whose first responsibility is establishing a coherent physical world.

The description accumulates rather than detonates.

A scene becomes vivid through:

1. orientation,
2. spatial relationships,
3. concrete surfaces and objects,
4. ambient motion,
5. sound or atmosphere,
6. people behaving inside the environment,
7. the exceptional thing that breaks the pattern.

The resulting feeling is **presence**.

## Broad → Narrow

Mercer frequently establishes a larger geographic, political, or environmental frame and then narrows toward the immediate place where play begins.

Use:

> **world → region → location → room → person → action**

Only traverse the levels that matter.

For a major campaign arrival, several levels may earn attention.

For entering the next tavern, begin at the tavern.

The technique is not exposition for its own sake. Each narrowing step should make the next one easier to imagine.

### Pattern

```markdown
<where this place sits in the larger environment>

<what kind of place this is>

<what dominates the immediate view>

<what people or objects are doing>

<what now asks for attention>
```

---

# Physical Inventory

Mercer-style description benefits from a **small coherent inventory** of physically trackable facts.

Favor:

- entrances,
- elevation,
- central structures,
- light sources,
- crowds,
- furniture that affects movement,
- weather,
- visible magic,
- moving machinery,
- distinctive materials,
- the location of important people.

Relational language matters:

> behind the stage

> across the chamber

> along the upper balcony

> pressed against the eastern wall

> between you and the gate

The objective is not completeness.

The objective is to make later action feel as though it occurs in a place that was already there.

---

# Sensory Layering

Add sensory information **sequentially**, not as a checklist.

A Mercer-like scenic passage may establish the visual field, then allow sound, light, movement, temperature, or crowd behavior to deepen it as the scene continues.

This creates the sensation that the world keeps resolving into detail as the players attend to it.

Prefer:

> image → sound → motion

or:

> geometry → people → atmosphere

over:

> sight + smell + sound + touch + taste

Each new layer should modify the mental model already established.

---

# Ambient Life

A location becomes convincing when things exist there that are not waiting for the PCs.

Useful ambient behavior includes:

- patrons returning to conversations,
- children trying to see over adults,
- guards watching the wrong part of a room,
- merchants finishing transactions,
- rain running from awnings,
- servants correcting place settings,
- musicians continuing through an argument.

Ambient life supplies **scale and normality**.

That normality makes later disruption legible.

---

# Physical Continuity

Once a physical fact enters the scene, preserve it.

This is especially important in combat.

If an attack:

- breaks a railing,
- freezes a wound,
- knocks loose a tooth,
- ignites a curtain,
- overturns a table,
- leaves someone prone beside the stairs,

the changed state persists until something changes it again.

Mercer-style consequence narration excels when each mechanical result becomes another fact in the shared physical world.

Use:

> **mechanic → physical event → persistent state**

Example:

```markdown
The bolt catches the knight beneath the shoulder plate. Frost races across the steel, locking that arm against his side as he stumbles into the altar.
```

The next narration now inherits:

- damaged shoulder,
- frozen armor,
- knight beside altar.

---

# Motion Chains

For significant actions, describe causality in order.

> launch → travel → impact → reaction

> step → strike → displacement → new position

> spell → environmental interaction → consequence

This creates cinematic clarity without requiring ornate language.

Do not expand every routine action.

Spend the chain on actions whose physical sequence matters.

---

# NPC Embodiment

Mercer's own NPC guidance emphasizes knowing what drives the NPC, what the NPC wants from the current interaction, and using physical behavior to distinguish them.

Translate that into prose preparation as:

```markdown
**Drive:** <persistent desire>

**Here:** <what they want in this interaction>

**Body:** <posture / gesture / habitual physical signal>
```

Then let dialogue emerge from those three facts.

An NPC should become recognizable before their biography is explained.

Useful differentiators include:

- posture,
- tempo,
- eye contact,
- use of personal space,
- habitual hand movement,
- stillness,
- breath,
- whether they address the group or one person,
- what they do while listening.

A voice is optional.

Behavior is not.

---

# Mercer Sentence Rhythm

Default toward **flowing but concrete** sentences.

A sentence may carry several linked observations when the relationships are easy to hear:

> The gallery opens beneath a glass roof, rain ticking overhead while servants hurry between the banquet tables and a pair of armored guards watch the western doors.

The sentence works because every clause belongs to one coherent image.

Break the sentence when:

- the subject changes sharply,
- danger appears,
- an actionable fact deserves prominence,
- the players have reached a reaction point.

---

# Mercer Detail Test

A scenic detail earns its place when it makes the world:

- easier to navigate,
- easier to inhabit,
- easier to remember,
- more physically continuous,
- more socially credible.

Decorative detail that does none of these is excess.

**Completion criterion:** after the passage, the DM can sketch the immediate scene from memory and narrate the next physical action without inventing where everything is.

---

# Mercer Failure Mode: Saturation

The imitation error is **more description**.

That is not the technique.

Too much scenic accumulation produces:

- delayed agency,
- weak attentional hierarchy,
- decorative clutter,
- important objects buried among irrelevant ones,
- paragraphs optimized for readers instead of listeners.

Correct by identifying the **minimum sufficient world**.

Keep the details that make subsequent play feel physically inevitable.

---

# Mulligan — Dramatic Cut

## Core

> **Decide what this moment means, then spend attention accordingly.**

Mulligan frequently treats narration as **attention architecture**.

Routine information can move quickly.

A major moment may abruptly receive:

- a fragment,
- a pause,
- a repeated image,
- a viewpoint change,
- a conceptual statement,
- a sudden increase in descriptive resolution.

The resulting feeling is **significance**.

---

# Enter Late

When the dramatic fact is already happening, begin there.

Do not always approach an important moment through several orientation sentences.

A Mulligan-like opening may begin with:

> one noun

> one sensation

> one impossible fact

> one character's name

> one irreversible change

Then establish context around it.

### Pattern

```markdown
<Rupture.>

<immediate embodied fact>

<context required to understand it>

<new information>

<reaction point>
```

This works particularly well for:

- dreams,
- visions,
- catastrophes,
- betrayals,
- transformations,
- sudden discoveries,
- cliffhangers,
- climax beats.

---

# Splash Page

Treat attention as variable.

Most moments receive ordinary compression.

Important moments receive disproportionate space.

A **splash page** may use:

- a shorter first sentence,
- extra silence around one image,
- a slower reveal,
- a repeated motif,
- unusually precise sensory detail,
- a deliberate paragraph break,
- a final sentence isolated for impact.

The value comes from **contrast with the normal rhythm**.

If every sentence is a splash page, none is.

Reserve high-resolution prose for the moments the campaign wants remembered.

---

# Image ↔ Meaning

Mulligan-style narration often moves rapidly between a concrete image and the larger idea the image embodies.

Two useful movements:

> **image → meaning**

and

> **meaning → proof**

### Image → meaning

```markdown
Every balcony is packed. The servants are standing with the nobles now.

Whatever hierarchy governed this palace an hour ago is finished.
```

### Meaning → proof

```markdown
The city has begun preparing for war.

Temple bells are being melted in the square for arrowheads.
```

The semantic statement is useful when it gives the players something their characters could plainly understand from the evidence.

It must not steal a deduction that belongs to them.

---

# Contrast

Find the contradiction that defines the moment.

Examples:

- celebration during catastrophe,
- immaculate clothing over bloody hands,
- cosmic power used for a petty purpose,
- affectionate language carrying a threat,
- bureaucracy surrounding the miraculous,
- comedy inside sincere danger,
- beauty created by something terrible.

State or reveal the contrast cleanly.

Contrast compresses worldbuilding because two truths become memorable through their collision.

---

# Character Gravity

When a moment matters especially to one PC, route the description through what makes it matter **to that character**.

Use established:

- relationships,
- goals,
- fears,
- expertise,
- history,
- promises,
- symbols,
- unresolved questions.

The GM does not need to dictate the PC's emotion.

Instead, select the fact that makes emotional interpretation unavoidable.

Generic:

```markdown
The commander removes her helmet.
```

Gravity:

```markdown
The commander removes her helmet.

Tarin, you know that face. It is the woman whose portrait hung above your father's desk for twenty years.
```

The prose supplies relevance.

The player supplies the response.

---

# Editorial Cut

For significant transitions between PCs or distant simultaneous events, an explicit **cut** can be useful.

The cut communicates:

> this viewpoint matters now.

Possible forms:

```markdown
Across the city—
```

```markdown
At that exact moment, beneath the palace—
```

```markdown
Serena. While they are hearing this, you are somewhere else entirely.
```

Explicit cinematic language is optional.

Use it when the scene benefits from deliberate montage or parallel action.

For ordinary spatial movement, remain inside character perception.

---

# Pattern

Repetition creates meaning cheaply.

Reincorporate:

- a person already encountered,
- an earlier location,
- a repeated phrase,
- an object previously treated as trivial,
- a social custom established casually,
- an environmental detail,
- a joke,
- a promise,
- a visual motif.

The second appearance should change the meaning of the first.

This is stronger than continually inventing new symbols.

### Pattern

```markdown
first appearance → recognition → altered context → payoff
```

The ideal reaction is not:

> That is a new cool thing.

It is:

> Oh. **That** thing.

---

# Rhythmic Escalation

Sentence length should respond to dramatic pressure.

### Low pressure

Allow complete explanatory sentences.

### Rising pressure

Reduce clauses.

Increase verbs.

Repeat important nouns.

### Rupture

Use fragments when the fragment itself carries information.

```markdown
The bell rings once.

Again.

The western tower folds in half.
```

### After impact

Expand again only after the players understand what changed.

Shortness is emphasis, not a permanent voice.

---

# Verbal Pressure

In social scenes, dramatic prose often comes from an NPC pursuing a clear objective through **argument**, not exposition.

Build important dialogue around:

1. what the NPC wants,
2. what the PC wants,
3. the value they disagree about,
4. the leverage presently available.

Then let the NPC attack the disagreement.

A strong antagonist does not need to announce their philosophy.

Their philosophy becomes visible through what they:

- justify,
- dismiss,
- offer,
- threaten,
- refuse to understand,
- cannot admit.

This supports long-form rhetorical NPC dialogue without turning the NPC into a lore terminal.

---

# Earnestness Before Release

Let sincere moments finish.

When humor follows dramatic weight, place the joke **after** the emotional or threatening fact has landed.

Useful rhythm:

> sincere beat → silence/reaction → comic release

not:

> sincere beat → immediate self-undercutting gag

Likewise, ridiculous NPCs should pursue their ridiculous goals sincerely.

The comic energy comes from the collision between premise and conviction.

---

# Mulligan Detail Test

A dramatic detail earns its place when it increases:

- significance,
- character relevance,
- contrast,
- recognition,
- escalation,
- thematic clarity.

**Completion criterion:** remove the names and lore from the passage; the remaining rhythm should still make clear which sentence or image the players are supposed to remember.

---

# Mulligan Failure Mode: The Monologue Machine

The imitation error is **making everything profound**.

That produces:

- constant rhetorical escalation,
- NPCs who all sound equally articulate,
- themes explained instead of dramatized,
- excessive fragments,
- forced callbacks,
- premature emotional conclusions,
- every moment competing to be iconic.

Correct by restoring **contrast**.

Most speech is ordinary.

Most descriptions are functional.

Most actions are quick.

The exceptional moment becomes powerful because the surrounding language allows it to be exceptional.

---

# Blending the Modes

Do not choose one GM for an entire campaign.

Choose according to the current communication problem.

## Mercer Base → Mulligan Punctuation

Useful for exploration-heavy sandbox play.

1. **Scenic lens:** establish the location and physical affordances.
2. Let play proceed normally.
3. **Dramatic cut:** sharply increase resolution when a revelation or emotionally loaded change occurs.
4. Return to ordinary scene language after the reaction.

Example shape:

```markdown
[grounded room and inhabitants]

[player interaction]

[one previously ordinary object becomes significant]

[short high-emphasis reveal]

[stop]
```

---

# Mulligan Frame → Mercer Grounding

Useful for hooks and sudden threats.

1. **Dramatic cut:** open on the rupture.
2. Identify the character-specific significance.
3. **Scenic lens:** establish the physical facts needed for action.
4. Hand control back.

Example shape:

```markdown
The bridge moves.

Not sways. Moves.

Twenty feet below you, something the width of the river is pushing its back against the pilings. The eastern span has already lifted three feet, throwing carts toward the northern rail. The stone stairs behind you still reach the bank.

What do you do?
```

The opening creates significance.

The grounding makes it playable.

# Same Scene, Two Modes

The following examples are original synthesis, not quotations.

### Scene state

- abandoned astronomical observatory,
- circular chamber,
- storm outside,
- brass star-map turning backward,
- open western balcony,
- someone is climbing toward the balcony.

### Mercer — Scenic Lens

> The observatory is a circular chamber of pale stone, open to the storm through a wide balcony on the western wall. Rain blows across the floor in silver sheets, running beneath the great brass star-map that occupies the center of the room. Its nested rings are still turning despite the abandoned machinery around it—and every one of them is rotating backward. From somewhere below the balcony comes the scrape of metal against stone.

The location exists first.

The anomaly emerges inside it.

### Mulligan — Dramatic Cut

> The stars are wrong.
>
> At the center of the abandoned observatory, the brass map of the heavens is turning backward. Rain pours through the western balcony—and beneath the thunder, you hear metal strike stone below you.
>
> Someone is climbing up.

The anomaly exists first.

The world resolves around it.

Neither is inherently superior.

They spend attention differently.

---

# Transformation Heuristics

When converting neutral prose toward **Mercer — scenic lens**:

> add relational space → add ambient life → connect sensory layers → preserve physical continuity

When converting neutral prose toward **Mulligan — dramatic cut**:

> identify significance → move it forward → sharpen contrast → vary rhythm → reincorporate existing meaning

When blending:

> **ground → strike → ground**

---

# Style Selection by Beat

Use these as defaults, not requirements.

**Hook**
- Mercer when fascination with a place is the hook.
- Mulligan when disruption, revelation, or personal relevance is the hook.

**Development**
- Mercer for exploration, travel, investigation, and inhabited social spaces.
- Mulligan for discoveries, confrontations, thematic turns, and character revelations.

**Cliffhanger**
- Mercer for kinetic geography and continuous physical peril.
- Mulligan for the instant the situation changes.

**Climax**
- Mercer for battlefield continuity.
- Mulligan for decisive reversals and high-emphasis images.
- Blend aggressively.

**Resolution**
- Mercer when showing the world returning to ordinary life.
- Mulligan when one image or callback crystallizes what the events meant.

The beat determines **what happens**.

The mode determines **how attention is spent while communicating it**.

---

# Final Review

Do not ask whether the prose “sounds like Mercer” or “sounds like Mulligan.”

Ask:

### Scenic lens
Can the players inhabit and track the world?

### Dramatic cut
Do they know what changed and why this moment deserves attention?

### Gravity
Does the passage emphasize what matters to these characters rather than to an abstract audience?

### Contrast
Has exceptional language been reserved for exceptional moments?

### Originality
Are the techniques present without copying recognizable phrasing, catchphrases, or verbal tics?

If the answers are yes, the mode is functioning.

# Research Basis

This reference distills observed player-facing technique from:

- **Matthew Mercer — *Critical Role*, Campaign 2, Episode 1, “Curious Beginnings”**
  - broad-to-narrow world and location framing,
  - explicit spatial relationships,
  - layered spectacle through sound, lighting, motion, and crowd behavior,
  - mechanical results translated into persistent physical consequences.

- **Matthew Mercer — conversation on bringing NPCs to life**
  - persistent NPC drive,
  - interaction-specific goals,
  - body language as characterization,
  - improvisation from the NPC's underlying root rather than a prepared speech.

- **Brennan Lee Mulligan — *Exandria Unlimited: Calamity*, Episode 1, “Excelsior”**
  - compressed in-medias-res opening,
  - embodied character viewpoint,
  - abrupt changes in descriptive resolution,
  - explicit editorial movement between character perspectives.

- **Brennan Lee Mulligan — discussion of comics and storytelling**
  - rhythm as an attention mechanism,
  - deliberate disproportionate emphasis,
  - the “splash page” principle,
  - pattern and reincorporation as efficient meaning-making.

- **Game Masters of Exandria roundtable**
  - modular preparation,
  - player motivation and backstory as reliable dramatic anchors,
  - preserving agency by building situations around what characters already care about.

These sources establish the techniques.

The operational rules above are a synthesis for agent use, not claims that either GM follows a formal written system.
