# Detail and Imagery

## Semantic Compression

Use strong category anchors.

A concise category lets the players' imaginations supply generic details.

Examples:

- drowned chapel,
- decadent hunting lodge,
- bureaucratic necromancer's office,
- war-torn fishing village,
- overcrowded goblin market,
- immaculate execution chamber,
- abandoned children's theater,
- ostentatious new-money ballroom.

Then specify only what:

- breaks the expected pattern,
- matters to play,
- establishes tone,
- distinguishes this place,
- can become relevant,
- rewards attention.

Do not describe every chair.

## Detail Budget

Treat player attention as finite.

Every detail consumes bandwidth.

A default scene introduction usually needs:

1. **orientation**
2. **one defining image**
3. **important affordances**
4. **the thing demanding attention**

More may be added as players interact.

Do not dump the entire room database into the entrance narration.

### Good progressive disclosure

**Arrival**

> The archive is a circular chamber cut into black stone. Shelves climb three stories around you, but every book within arm's reach has been burned. At the center, a brass lift descends into darkness.

**After approaching the shelves**

Reveal relevant shelf details.

**After inspecting the lift**

Reveal its controls, damage, residue, markings.

**After listening**

Reveal the distant machinery below.

Description continues through play.

## Attentional Signaling

Specificity implies importance.

So do:

- unusual detail,
- sentence position,
- repetition,
- contrast,
- naming,
- extra word count,
- sensory emphasis.

Use this intentionally.

If a clue must be noticeable, give it appropriate prominence.

If a clue should remain discoverable without screaming *CLUE*, establish enough ordinary detail that it can exist within the scene's normal descriptive texture.

Do not accidentally make the only named object in a room the secret murder weapon unless that degree of spotlight is intended.

## Specificity

Choose a small number of specific details rather than many generic details.

Weak:

> The fancy room is decorated with expensive furniture.

Stronger:

> The chairs are carved ebony. Each has a different guest's name inlaid in mother-of-pearl.

Specificity creates handles for memory and interaction.

But do not overload descriptions with decorative specificity that will never matter.

## Dynamic Description

Prefer things that are:

- moving,
- changing,
- reacting,
- deteriorating,
- approaching,
- making noise,
- interacting.

A dynamic detail naturally invites play.

Static:

> A chandelier hangs from the ceiling.

Dynamic:

> The chandelier sways although the air is still.

Dynamic description can simultaneously communicate atmosphere and information.

## Sensory Detail

Use senses as information channels, not a checklist.

Consider:

- sight,
- sound,
- smell,
- temperature,
- touch,
- vibration,
- balance,
- pressure,
- taste when relevant,
- magical or supernatural perception where established.

Select a sensory detail when it:

- distinguishes the place,
- communicates hidden causes indirectly,
- establishes mood efficiently,
- provides actionable evidence,
- reinforces an important environmental truth.

Do not mechanically include one detail from every sense.

Bad agent habit:

> You see moss, smell earth, hear dripping water, feel cool air, and taste mineral moisture.

This is sensory inventory, not narration.

Better:

> The cave breathes warm air against you, carrying the sweet rot of fruit from somewhere deeper inside.

Two details imply an ecosystem and a direction.

## Viewpoint

Narration may reflect what a particular character naturally notices.

This is especially useful for:

- experts,
- specialists,
- characters with strong backgrounds,
- culturally embedded characters,
- moments of personal relevance.

A sailor entering a harbor may notice:

- wind,
- current,
- rigging,
- hull design,
- tide,
- harbor discipline.

A thief may notice:

- sightlines,
- patrols,
- locks,
- valuables,
- exits,
- concealment.

A noble may notice:

- status,
- protocol,
- fabrics,
- heraldry,
- hierarchy,
- social violations.

Do not use viewpoint to invent information unavailable to the character.

Use it to **select and emphasize truthful details**.

## Reincorporation

Important environmental facts should recur naturally.

If a scene is defined by:

- ankle-deep blood,
- deafening machinery,
- violent crosswinds,
- crushing heat,
- unstable gravity,
- festival crowds,

allow those facts to reappear during actions.

Example:

> The shot goes wide, swallowed by the machine hall's roar.

> You shove through the revelers and lose sight of him beneath a forest of paper lanterns.

Reincorporation keeps the environment present without repeatedly re-describing it.
