# Climax Modes

Read only the branch selected during `writing-climax-beats` step 5.

The classical Climax has two primary forms:

- **Final Battle** — the decisive question is settled through active opposition.
- **Final Revelation** — the decisive question is settled by establishing the truth.

For sandbox campaign play, use **Convergent Climax** when those mechanisms are causally inseparable.

---

## Final Battle

Use when overcoming, preventing, surviving, escaping, capturing, protecting, or otherwise acting against opposition is what makes the situation decisive.

"Battle" describes the decisive conflict, not a requirement that every character attack until one side reaches zero hit points.

### Build around an objective

Give the conflict a purpose beyond defeating available enemies.

Examples:

- prevent a ritual;
- hold a gate until civilians escape;
- capture someone alive;
- seize control of a ship;
- rescue a prisoner;
- stop evidence from being destroyed;
- reach an artifact first;
- protect two incompatible objectives;
- survive until a political or magical condition changes;
- force an opponent to retreat;
- escape before the environment becomes unsurvivable.

Combat remains meaningful because it affects those objectives.

### Battle opposition

The opposition should enter with:

- a concrete objective;
- an initial plan;
- assets appropriate to what survived previous play;
- weaknesses the party may have discovered;
- relationships and morale that reflect prior events;
- reasons to change tactics when the fictional state changes.

Scale from **campaign causality**, not from the desired ending.

If the party destroyed the villain's army earlier, the army remains destroyed.

If the party recruited the villain's lieutenant, the lieutenant does not reappear as a loyal henchman merely because the final encounter needs another combatant.

### Battlefield

Prepare actionable features rather than scenic description.

Useful features can:

- separate or connect objectives;
- create movement decisions;
- expose or protect actors;
- interact with established abilities;
- threaten resources;
- alter visibility or information;
- change as the situation escalates;
- become leverage through player ingenuity.

A feature earns space on the Beat page when the DM can answer:

> What can someone do with this?

Keep maintained location lore on the location page.

### Escalation

Prefer escalation that changes the problem.

Examples:

- a bridge collapses and divides allies;
- the antagonist abandons combat to complete the real objective;
- a faction realizes it has been deceived;
- fire reaches the archive containing needed evidence;
- the creature protecting the ritual breaks free;
- reinforcements arrive because an alarm was never disabled;
- reinforcements fail to arrive because the party previously destroyed their route;
- the battlefield begins moving, flooding, falling, burning, opening, or transforming.

Each escalation must follow from an established cause or current actor.

### Victory and defeat

Treat both as world states.

A Final Battle can end with:

- decisive victory;
- costly victory;
- objective achieved while the antagonist escapes;
- antagonist defeated while the larger objective fails;
- negotiated cessation;
- faction realignment;
- party retreat;
- capture;
- partial evacuation;
- loss of the immediate objective;
- an unexpected solution that makes the battle irrelevant.

The Climax records the causal consequences of these states.

The following Resolution determines what they mean for the campaign.

## Final Revelation

Use when the decisive act is uncovering, proving, exposing, correctly interpreting, or publicly establishing the truth.

The mystery may have a fixed truth.

The **players' discovery of it** remains play.

### Evidence before conclusion

Retrieve the evidence already established in the campaign.

Determine:

- what the party actually knows;
- what evidence they possess;
- what remains inference;
- which actors possess missing pieces;
- which false beliefs remain active;
- what evidence could persuade relevant observers;
- what opposition is doing to hide, destroy, discredit, reinterpret, or outrun the truth.

Do not replace missing evidence with a cutscene simply because the Climax needs the answer.

### The revelation question

Distinguish three different things:

**Truth**  
What actually happened.

**Discovery**  
Whether the characters correctly understand it.

**Establishment**  
Whether the truth can be proven, communicated, or made consequential to the people who matter.

These may resolve differently.

The party can discover the killer and still fail to prove it.

They can expose the conspiracy after the conspirators have already achieved their objective.

They can prove the truth to one faction while another refuses it.

They can realize their supposed enemy was correct and completely reframe the confrontation.

This keeps a fixed underlying mystery compatible with an open campaign state.

### Revelation opposition

A revelatory Climax still needs pressure.

Opposition may attempt to:

- destroy evidence;
- flee;
- discredit a witness;
- complete an objective before exposure matters;
- frame another actor;
- force a public decision before the truth is known;
- separate the characters from the audience they need;
- exploit genuine ambiguity in the evidence.

The opposition acts because of its goals, not because the script requires another complication.

### Player action

Players should be able to affect the revelation through established capabilities and choices:

- questioning witnesses;
- comparing clues;
- confronting contradictions;
- protecting evidence;
- choosing whom to trust;
- deciding when and where to reveal information;
- persuading an audience;
- sacrificing secrecy for credibility;
- recovering missing proof;
- forcing an opponent to respond publicly.

The answer should emerge from evidence and action rather than GM fiat.

### Fallout

Once the underlying truth is established, the mystery itself may be resolved.

The consequences remain open.

Record dimensions such as:

- who learns the truth;
- who believes it;
- what proof survives;
- who is implicated;
- which institution loses legitimacy;
- who escapes before consequences arrive;
- what relationship changes because of the truth;
- whether the revelation occurred early enough to change the underlying event.

Pass those facts to the Resolution.

## Convergent Climax

Use when active confrontation and revelation transform one another so strongly that separating them would make either half artificial.

Examples:

- evidence revealed during a battle can cause enemy forces to defect;
- defeating a guardian gives access to the proof needed to expose the true antagonist;
- a public trial becomes an assassination attempt while testimony is still unfolding;
- the antagonist's identity changes what the party is willing to destroy;
- a ritual can be stopped by force, but understanding its true purpose creates a different objective;
- the presumed enemy arrives to fight, while discoveries made during the confrontation reveal a common threat.

A Convergent Climax is not simply "combat plus lore."

Each side must change the other.

### Build coupled state

Define at least one relationship of each kind:

**Revelation → conflict**

A truth changes:

- allegiance;
- target priority;
- objective;
- willingness to fight;
- available leverage;
- interpretation of the threat.

**Conflict → revelation**

Events in the confrontation change:

- access to evidence;
- survival of a witness;
- time available to reason;
- who can hear the truth;
- credibility of a claim;
- which hidden fact becomes observable.

If neither relationship exists, choose Final Battle or Final Revelation instead.

### Keep both open

Do not require the party to uncover every truth before acting.

Do not require them to win the physical conflict before a revelation can matter.

Let incomplete information, premature action, unexpected persuasion, tactical success, and costly discovery produce different campaign states.

## Pressure Components

Use these components selectively. They are a vocabulary, not a required checklist.

### Objective

What an actor wants to make true.

### Counter-objective

Something another actor wants that cannot remain simultaneously true without compromise.

### Clock

An established or newly necessary representation of progress toward a consequence.

Reuse an existing clock when one already owns that state.

### Threshold

The point after which returning to the old status quo is impossible.

### Handle

Something the players can materially affect.

### Leverage

A handle whose usefulness exists because of prior play.

### Cost

Something valuable that may have to be risked, spent, abandoned, exposed, damaged, or traded.

A cost is strongest when the players decide whether it is worth paying.

### Escalation trigger

A fictional state change that causes actors or mechanisms to respond.

### Realignment

An actor changes behavior because its goals, beliefs, relationships, or circumstances changed.

## Failure Modes and Replacements

### Predetermined finale → decisive situation

Replace:

> The party defeats the villain and saves the city.

With:

> The villain is activating the sea wall while evacuation ships are still inside the harbor. The wall protects the city from the coming wave but traps every ship behind it.

Now the GM knows the situation. The players determine the ending.

---

### Mandatory route → independent handles

Replace a single required solution with multiple parts of the situation that can be affected independently.

Examples:

- ritual conductor;
- power source;
- political sponsor;
- hostage;
- unstable ally;
- terrain;
- evidence;
- evacuation route.

These are not player-facing choices. They are simulation handles.

---

### Frozen boss room → actors in motion

Begin with actors already pursuing objectives.

The antagonist does not wait for a speech.

The army does not remain outside because the initiative scene has not started.

The ritual does not pause while the party debates.

The situation continues to exist between player decisions.

---

### HP objective → mission objective

Creature defeat may be one means of achieving the objective.

It should not automatically be the objective unless killing or defeating that creature is genuinely what resolves the situation.

---

### Decorative callback → causal payoff

A returning NPC, object, location, phrase, or backstory element should alter:

- what can happen;
- what someone wants;
- what someone knows;
- what the party can attempt;
- what the consequences mean.

Recognition alone is not payoff.

---

### Cutscene revelation → actionable evidence

Place enough information in the established world that players can discover, connect, protect, challenge, expose, or misinterpret it through play.

The GM owns the truth.

The players own the process by which their characters reach it.

---

### Rescue from nowhere → established causality

When outside intervention occurs, derive it from something already in motion:

- an alliance the party earned;
- a message they successfully sent;
- an enemy they previously spared;
- a faction pursuing its own goal;
- an established environmental process;
- a consequence deliberately triggered earlier.

Unexpected to the characters can still be causally earned.

Unexpected to the campaign state is deus ex machina.

---

## Final test

A strong Climax can answer all of these before play:

- What is happening right now?
- What does each consequential actor want?
- What happens if the party does nothing?
- Why do these characters care?
- What earlier choices matter here?
- What can be pushed on?
- What changes when it is pushed?
- What is the irreversible Threshold?
- Which outcome facts will the Resolution need?

It deliberately cannot answer:

> What will the players do?

If the prepared material answers that question, the Climax has become a script instead of a sandbox.
