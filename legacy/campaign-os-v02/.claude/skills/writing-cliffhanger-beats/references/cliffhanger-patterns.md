# Cliffhanger Patterns

Load this reference when choosing, classifying, revising, or reviewing the **pressure pattern** of a Cliffhanger.

The patterns below derive from the *Scripting the Game* Cliffhanger catalogue, adapted operationally for sandbox play.

A pattern describes how pressure works.

It does not prescribe the players' response or the encounter's outcome.

## Chooser

| Pattern | Core pressure |
|---|---|
| Chase | PCs must catch a moving target |
| Pursuit | A superior or dangerous force is chasing the PCs |
| Race | PCs and opposition compete to reach an objective first |
| Fist Fight | Ritualized or bounded unarmed physical confrontation |
| Dogfight | Mobile aerial combat |
| Confrontation | Immediate non-physical showdown backed by serious leverage |
| Duel | One-on-one combat under understood terms |
| Battle | Both sides knowingly enter open combat |
| Monster | A non-intelligent creature or creature-force creates the danger |
| Ambush | One side attempts to begin from surprise |
| Obstacle | Trap, terrain, hazard, confinement, or physical problem |
| Contest | Ritualized competition with a winner and loser |
| Skirmish | Limited combat in which withdrawal remains likely |

Choose by dramatic mechanics rather than aesthetics.

A fight on horseback may still be a **Race** if reaching the bridge first is what determines the outcome.

A dragon encounter may be a **Pursuit** if survival depends on escaping it.

A royal audience may be a **Confrontation** if an overwhelmingly powerful ruler is applying immediate coercive pressure.

---

## Chase

**Pressure**

The PCs pursue opposition that is moving away from them.

### Requires

A credible reason the characters already want to catch the target.

Gravity should usually already be established by prior play, often through a kidnapping, theft, revelation, attack, escape, or personal stake.

**Prep**

Define:

- quarry objective;
- quarry destination or escape condition;
- initial lead;
- meaningful terrain;
- what the quarry sacrifices to gain distance;
- what happens if the quarry escapes.

Provide multiple ways to reduce the lead.

The quarry should make choices according to its objective rather than following a fixed obstacle course.

### Handoff

Typical changed states include:

- quarry captured;
- quarry escaped but revealed a destination;
- dropped evidence recovered;
- chase enters hostile territory;
- third party becomes involved;
- PCs reach a location they otherwise would not have found.

---

## Pursuit

**Pressure**

The opposition chases the PCs.

The threat should be sufficiently serious that escape, evasion, negotiation, concealment, terrain use, or another alternative to simply standing and fighting is meaningful.

**Prep**

Define:

- pursuer objective;
- why it wants the PCs;
- relative advantage;
- what lets the PCs gain distance;
- what lets the pursuer close;
- safe-enough escape conditions;
- consequences of capture.

Superior force should be legible from the fiction.

The DM should not need to tell the players "you are supposed to run."

**Sandbox check**

Standing to fight remains a valid player decision.

Resolve the resulting fiction honestly.

Do not make the pursuers invulnerable merely because pursuit was the prepared pattern.

---

## Race

**Pressure**

PCs and opposition compete to reach a person, place, object, position, or other objective first.

**Prep**

Define:

- shared objective;
- why each side wants it;
- current relative position;
- routes or approach vectors;
- what each side knows;
- interference each side can credibly attempt;
- consequence of arriving first.

A Race is strongest after information establishes why the objective matters.

**Sandbox check**

Players may abandon the Race, redefine what "winning" means, sabotage the objective, negotiate with the opposition, or choose to let the opposition arrive first for strategic reasons.

Know what happens when they do.

---

## Fist Fight

**Pressure**

A bounded physical contest fought without conventional battlefield escalation.

It can represent wrestling, boxing, martial arts, tavern brawling, ritual combat, or another form of weaponless confrontation.

**Prep**

Define:

- ritual or social rules;
- stakes;
- permitted participants;
- boundaries;
- what counts as victory;
- consequences for breaking the rules;
- what the opponent actually wants.

**Sandbox check**

The ritual must matter beyond "the adventure needs a fight."

Winning, losing, refusing, cheating, exposing cheating, or disrupting the contest should produce coherent states.

---

## Dogfight

**Pressure**

Combat occurs through flight: vehicles, mounts, creatures, magic, personal flight, or another aerial medium.

**Prep**

Define:

- objectives beyond simple destruction where possible;
- altitude/terrain constraints;
- relative mobility;
- escape conditions;
- environmental hazards;
- consequences of losing mobility;
- what each side is protecting or trying to reach.

**Sandbox check**

Keep spatial stakes intelligible enough that the DM can adjudicate unconventional movement.

Aerial scenery that does not change decisions is decoration, not preparation.

---

## Confrontation

**Pressure**

The heroes and opposition meet in an immediate **non-physical fight of leverage, threats, intimidation, demands, or brinkmanship**.

The opposition normally has a serious advantage but a reason not to convert that advantage immediately into violence.

**Prep**

Define:

- demand;
- leverage;
- why the opposition is talking instead of attacking;
- what it fears losing;
- deadline or threshold;
- what causes escalation;
- what concession would satisfy it;
- what refusal changes.

**Sandbox check**

Conversation must be able to change the state.

The opposition's restraint needs a fictional reason.

If nothing the PCs say or do can matter and combat is inevitable regardless, the scene is functioning as scripted preamble rather than meaningful Confrontation.

---

## Duel

**Pressure**

Two individuals resolve a conflict through one-on-one combat under understood terms.

Weapons or capabilities are normally comparable enough for the contest to be recognized as a duel rather than an execution.

**Prep**

Define:

- participants;
- stakes;
- rules;
- boundaries;
- victory condition;
- consequence of refusal;
- consequence of interference;
- whether either participant intends to honor the outcome.

**Sandbox check**

The party can interfere, refuse the social premise, expose cheating, negotiate different terms, or accept the consequences of breaking custom.

Prepare those consequences rather than preventing the choices.

---

## Battle

**Pressure**

Both sides know open combat is occurring and commit forces to it.

Unlike an Ambush, the core dramatic fact is not surprise.

Unlike a Duel, multiple participants can freely engage.

### Escalation principle

Repeated Battles should rise in opposition commitment.

A useful conceptual progression is:

**Grunts → Minions → Henchmen → Villain**

Treat these as dramatic roles, not mandatory game-stat categories.

Early Battles establish what ordinary opposition can do.

Later Battles expose more capable agents, scarcer resources, stronger positioning, higher stakes, or direct villain involvement.

**Prep**

Define:

- each side's objective;
- terrain that affects decisions;
- opposition plan;
- reinforcement/escalation triggers;
- retreat conditions;
- objective state;
- consequences of either side controlling the field.

**Sandbox check**

"Kill everyone on the other side" is sometimes valid, but objective-driven Battles usually create richer agency.

The opposition can retreat, surrender, bargain, change objectives, or exploit an opening when its motives support doing so.

---

## Monster

**Pressure**

A non-intelligent or non-reasoning creature-force threatens the characters.

The creature can be an animal, magical beast, mindless undead, construct, swarm, environmental organism, or another threat whose behavior follows instinct/programming rather than strategic negotiation.

**Prep**

Define:

- behavioral drive;
- territory;
- senses;
- triggers;
- target preference;
- fear/avoidance behavior;
- environmental interaction;
- what ends the threat.

**Sandbox check**

Because the Monster is not strategic opposition, make its behavioral logic especially clear.

Players should be able to exploit what they learn about that behavior.

If the creature is reasoning, bargaining, planning, and politically motivated, another pattern may classify it better.

---

## Ambush

**Pressure**

One side attempts to gain the initial advantage through surprise.

### PCs ambush opposition

Make the opportunity legible:

- opposition is nearby;
- opposition is currently unaware;
- terrain or timing enables setup.

Let the players design the ambush.

Do not pre-script their trap.

### Opposition ambushes PCs

Establish:

- why the attackers knew where to strike;
- concealment;
- attack objective;
- detection cues;
- what happens when surprise fails.

Use the system's actual detection mechanics.

An ambush opportunity is not guaranteed surprise.

**Sandbox check**

Discovery before the attack should transform the situation naturally into Confrontation, Battle, Pursuit, escape, counter-ambush, or another state.

Do not force the surprise round because the beat was labeled Ambush.

---

## Obstacle

**Pressure**

A trap, confinement, terrain feature, environmental hazard, collapsing structure, magical hazard, or other physical problem threatens progress or safety.

### Required solvability

A designed trap or puzzle-like obstacle needs a solution.

Clues necessary to discover that solution must be available in the current situation or established earlier.

**Prep**

Define:

- hazard;
- trigger;
- warning signs;
- consequence;
- underlying mechanism;
- ways to detect or understand it;
- at least one valid solution;
- how creative alternatives can interact with the mechanism.

**Sandbox check**

Prep the mechanism rather than a single expected answer.

If the players devise a different solution that genuinely defeats the mechanism, it works.

---

## Contest

**Pressure**

Two sides engage in a ritualized competition with a winner and loser.

The Contest may test strength, memory, riddles, skill, cleverness, endurance, performance, or another measurable ability.

**Prep**

Define:

- participants;
- rules;
- stakes;
- victory condition;
- adjudication method;
- consequence of refusal;
- consequence of cheating;
- opponent capability.

### Fairness

Resolve the Contest using the actual game state, player reasoning, or appropriate mechanics.

The outcome is not set by GM fiat.

**Sandbox check**

The PCs may reject the contest's premise and accept the social or practical consequences.

---

## Skirmish

**Pressure**

A limited battle erupts without either side necessarily committing to total victory.

This is especially useful when the dramatic purpose is:

- testing strength;
- stealing something;
- delaying the PCs;
- probing defenses;
- forcing movement;
- creating cover;
- capturing rather than killing.

### Opposition behavior

When winning, opposition may capture, wound, drive off, seize an objective, or otherwise fulfill its limited purpose.

When losing, it may disengage through prepared or situational means.

Withdrawal methods must exist in the fiction.

**Sandbox check**

An intended withdrawal is an **objective**, not guaranteed immunity.

If the players genuinely prevent the escape, preserve that success and update the campaign state.

---

## Hybrid Patterns

Use a secondary pattern when the interaction produces a genuinely distinct layer of pressure.

Examples:

### Race + Obstacle

Both sides need the relic before the chamber collapses.

### Pursuit + Monster

The party flees through territory occupied by something neither side controls.

### Battle + Duel

A battlefield continues while two champions choose to resolve a critical objective personally.

### Chase + Confrontation

The quarry reaches temporary safety and gains enough leverage to force negotiation.

For hybrids, identify one **primary pattern**.

The primary pattern answers:

> What must the DM understand first to know how this danger moves?

---

## Pattern Escalation

Across multiple Cliffhangers, vary more than raw lethality.

Escalate along dimensions such as:

- stronger opposition;
- smarter opposition;
- scarcer time;
- more important objectives;
- worse terrain;
- higher collateral stakes;
- greater political exposure;
- more personal Gravity;
- deeper enemy commitment;
- fewer clean outcomes;
- stronger competing obligations.

The best later pressure often combines increased external danger with something the characters care about more deeply.

## Boundary Cases

### Action-heavy Hook

A Hook can use Cliffhanger machinery such as Ambush, Confrontation, or Fist Fight.

Hook still owns the scene because its primary function is initial engagement.

The recurring rhythm should normally move from that action-heavy Hook into Development.

### Climactic Battle

A battle that resolves the adventure's central dramatic question is **Climax**, not Cliffhanger.

Classification follows dramatic function.

### Ending Cliffhanger

A new danger revealed after the Climax as the adventure's final tag is an **Ending Cliffhanger Resolution**.

Route it to the Resolution skill.

### Hazardous travel

A dangerous journey is not automatically one giant Cliffhanger.

Use Cliffhanger when immediate physical peril becomes the active beat.

Use Development when travel's primary function is information, character interaction, discovery, warning, or other non-conflict progression.

---

## Pattern Review Questions

When a Cliffhanger feels weak, ask in order:

1. **Classification** — what actually generates the pressure?
2. **Gravity** — why do these PCs care?
3. **Objective** — what does the active force want?
4. **Trajectory** — what happens without intervention?
5. **Agency** — can the situation survive a different approach?
6. **Risk** — what meaningful state can be lost?
7. **Escalation** — what changes while unresolved?
8. **Fallout** — what states can emerge?
9. **Handoff** — what does the next Development have to work with?

Repair the first failing layer before embellishing later ones.
