# Hook Beat Reference

Reference for selecting, shaping, and repairing Hook beats.

The operational skill lives in `../SKILL.md`. This file owns the detailed Hook model so the main workflow stays short and predictable.

## Hook invariant

A Hook is the opening beat of its containing dramatic unit.

Its job is to get the players involved immediately through a situation that presents meaningful **threat or interest**. It can begin with action or with a quieter development. It may expose the main conflict directly or begin with a locally self-contained event whose consequences point outward.

For sandbox play, preserve the same function while changing the control model:

> **Strong attraction; open route.**

The GM prepares a compelling situation around things the characters already care about. The players decide how, whether, and at what cost they engage with it.

The Hook therefore establishes momentum without establishing the solution.

---

## The gravity model

Player gravity is the reliable connection between prepared material and an investment the player has already established through their character.

Backstory matters because it supplies interests that do not need to be invented at the moment the Hook appears. Goals, fears, loyalties, enemies, promises, unresolved history, and relationships can perform the same function when they are established in play or character creation.

The important distinction is between:

**Established gravity**

> [[PC]] has sworn to find their missing sister, and the Hook reveals evidence that she passed through this town.

and:

**Assumed gravity**

> [[PC]] is heroic, so surely they will help these strangers.

The first predicts attention from recorded character material. The second predicts player behaviour from a stereotype.

Use established gravity.

### Link of Relevance

Every Hook records at least one **Link of Relevance**:

> The specific established character element this prepared situation connects to.

A useful Link of Relevance has three parts:

`[[Character]] → established investment → connection to this Hook`

Example shape:

- `[[Mara Venn]]` → promised never to abandon another member of her old crew → the missing courier is the crewmate who once covered her escape.

The Link explains why the preparation belongs near this character. It does not dictate what the character must do.

### Multiple gravity anchors

Use additional anchors when they arise naturally.

A strong party Hook might touch:

- one PC's enemy;
- another PC's desired information;
- a third PC's threatened community.

This produces convergent interest without requiring identical motives.

Each anchor must remain true independently. Do not manufacture weak connections merely to mention every PC.

## The Hook kernel

Build the underlying situation before selecting its trope.

### Catalyst

The change that makes **now** different from five minutes ago.

Examples:

- someone arrives;
- something disappears;
- violence begins;
- a warning becomes concrete;
- evidence surfaces;
- a deadline is announced;
- a dormant threat moves;
- an ally behaves unexpectedly.

A Catalyst turns background lore into playable present tense.

### Gravity

The established reason this party has to care.

Gravity answers **why these characters?**

### Pressure

The reason waiting changes the situation.

Pressure may come from:

- danger;
- a rival acting first;
- deteriorating conditions;
- political response;
- an approaching departure;
- disappearing evidence;
- a vulnerable person;
- a closing opportunity;
- escalating public consequences.

Pressure should move the world. It need not punish the players for thinking.

### Vectors

The different directions player action can push the situation.

A vector is broader than a choice on a menu. It is a class of response the prepared world can support.

Examples include:

- learn what happened;
- follow the responsible actor;
- protect the threatened target;
- bargain with an interested faction;
- exploit the disruption;
- expose the event publicly;
- escape before consequences arrive;
- refuse involvement and pursue something else.

Vectors are evidence that the Hook contains a situation rather than a single prescribed route.

### Consequence

What develops when actors continue pursuing their goals.

The consequence answers:

> If the PCs do not produce the expected rescue, victory, answer, or intervention, what changes in the world?

Consequences make refusal and failure playable.

## Hook architectures

The following architectures adapt the Hook families from *Scripting the Game* for an agency-preserving sandbox.

Choose the architecture that best expresses the Hook kernel already established.

## Kidnapping

Someone important is taken, or the opening premise places the PCs in captivity.

**Strong when**

- an established relationship creates immediate gravity;
- the captor's identity or goal opens wider questions;
- time pressure matters.

**Sandbox treatment**

When someone else is taken, support responses beyond direct pursuit: investigation, bargaining, interception, political leverage, rescue, deception, or even choosing another priority.

When captivity is the opening premise, begin at the first meaningful decision inside that premise. Prep guards, factions, constraints, opportunities, and consequences rather than a mandatory escape sequence.

**Watch for**

A kidnapped stranger with no gravity is merely an assignment.

---

## Coronet Blue

The PCs begin in a dangerous or difficult situation without understanding how they arrived there or what others expect of them.

**Strong when**

- missing context itself is the mystery;
- the PCs possess clues through objects, injuries, relationships, or immediate reactions around them;
- discovering what happened changes present decisions.

**Sandbox treatment**

Seed several independent ways to learn or act. Confusion creates interest; it should not remove every basis for choice.

The PCs should know enough about the immediate danger to do something meaningful even while the larger explanation remains hidden.

---

## Action Opener

The Hook begins inside immediate peril: confrontation, ambush, chase, fight, disaster, or another physical emergency.

**Strong when**

- the campaign benefits from immediate kinetic energy;
- the opposition, threat, or ally can be revealed through action;
- the encounter itself creates unanswered questions.

**Sandbox treatment**

Prep the danger and the actors' intentions rather than a mandatory combat result.

Where the fiction supports them, escape, surrender, negotiation, trickery, pursuit, protection, or environmental interaction may be as consequential as defeating the opposition.

**Handoff**

An action-heavy Hook normally earns a quieter Development afterward: discovery, conversation, investigation, warning, or revelation.

---

## Development Opener

The Hook begins through a socially or informationally charged event: a secret meeting, mistaken identity, sudden relationship, treachery, or another non-physical disruption.

**Strong when**

- information changes what the PCs want;
- an NPC interaction places immediate stakes on the table;
- social uncertainty creates several possible responses.

**Sandbox treatment**

Put the destabilizing fact into play quickly. The scene is a Hook because something changes, not because an NPC delivers a long explanation.

**Handoff**

A low-action Hook often benefits from rising physical danger or confrontation later.

---

## Discovery

The PCs find an important object, place, creature, phenomenon, message, or piece of evidence.

**Strong when**

- the discovery has immediate meaning to an established PC interest;
- several parties may want it;
- understanding or possessing it creates new problems.

**Sandbox treatment**

The discovery is a new fact in the world, not an instruction.

Support keeping it, hiding it, selling it, studying it, destroying it, returning it, publicizing it, ignoring it, or using it when those responses make fictional sense.

**Weak form**

"You find a mysterious object."

**Strong form**

"You find the object your rival spent three sessions trying to locate, and someone else has already noticed you have it."

---

## Crisis

The PCs enter play while a major disaster or active emergency is already underway.

**Strong when**

- immediate triage forces meaningful priorities;
- the crisis directly touches existing gravity;
- several people or factions want incompatible things from the response.

**Sandbox treatment**

Give the crisis multiple fronts.

The players should decide what matters most: whom to save, what to protect, what to pursue, when to withdraw, and what losses they will accept.

A crisis becomes a rail when only one action counts as participation.

---

## Revelation

A previously hidden fact abruptly changes the PCs' understanding of their lives or situation.

**Strong when**

- the fact intersects directly with established identity, history, goals, or relationships;
- knowing it creates immediate decisions rather than merely lore.

**Sandbox treatment**

Reveal the fact; leave its meaning and response open.

"The duke is your father" is a revelation.

"Therefore you must overthrow him" is a plotted conclusion.

---

## Murder

Play begins with a killing or the discovery of one.

**Strong when**

- the victim has established relevance;
- the circumstances intersect with an active conflict;
- different actors have different reasons to shape the story of what happened.

**Sandbox treatment**

Keep every response on the list below valid; investigation becomes the sole required response only when the campaign premise already establishes that obligation.

The killing might lead to pursuit, protection, concealment, political exploitation, vengeance, flight, negotiation, or investigation.

The world should respond even if the PCs never identify the killer.

---

## False Accusation

The PCs are blamed for something they did not do.

**Strong when**

- reputation, law, faction standing, or a valued relationship already matters;
- the accusation creates conflicting pressures;
- evidence can be investigated, manipulated, escaped, or confronted.

**Sandbox treatment**

Prep the accusation's evidence, believers, opportunists, skeptics, and consequences.

The useful question is not "How do the PCs clear their names?" but:

> What do the relevant actors do now that the PCs stand accused?

The party may clear itself, flee, expose a larger crime, accept outlaw status, bargain, retaliate, frame someone else, or discover the accusation serves a purpose more important than innocence.

---

## Looming Threat

The danger has not fully arrived, but credible signs show that it is approaching.

**Strong when**

- anticipation matters;
- different factions disagree about what to do;
- preparation itself exposes values and priorities;
- acting early can meaningfully change the eventual crisis.

**Sandbox treatment**

Make the threat concrete enough to support action now.

A vague prophecy supplies atmosphere. Troop movements, missing ships, strange deaths, evacuated villages, failing wards, or a rival purchasing critical supplies create actionable pressure.

The important question becomes:

> What will the PCs do with the time they still have?

---

## Pacing after the Hook

*Scripting the Game* uses contrast to preserve pacing.

An action-heavy Hook naturally creates appetite for a quieter beat involving information, conversation, discovery, warning, character interaction, or consequence.

A cerebral Hook naturally creates appetite for greater danger, confrontation, pursuit, or another active problem.

Treat this as **dramatic counterpoint**, not sequence enforcement.

The players may create a different transition through their choices. Prep enough causal material to follow them while keeping the intended contrast available.

A Hook should also avoid reproducing itself immediately.

If the opening is:

> assassins attack;

then the next prepared escalation should usually reveal, complicate, contextualize, or redirect the situation rather than simply becoming:

> more assassins attack.

## Sandbox stress tests

Use these when reviewing a Hook.

## Bite test

For each Link of Relevance:

> What established fact makes this PC care?

A concrete answer indicates gravity.

A personality assumption indicates guesswork.

---

## Route test

Can you identify at least three materially different response classes that the prepared world can support where the fiction permits them?

Examples:

- investigate;
- attack;
- bargain;
- pursue;
- protect;
- flee;
- expose;
- exploit;
- seek help;
- ignore.

Three versions of "go to the villain's fortress" count as one route.

The point is not to guarantee every conceivable action. The point is to make sure the Hook was not secretly built around a single required choice.

---

## Refusal test

Suppose the players say:

> No.

What happens next?

A healthy answer describes other actors continuing to act and the world changing.

A weak answer describes how the GM forces the party back onto the intended mission.

Refusal may have serious consequences. Consequence preserves agency because the player's decision still changes the fiction.

---

## Outcome test

Ask whether the prepared Hook remains coherent if the party:

- succeeds immediately;
- fails badly;
- compromises;
- joins an unexpected side;
- discovers an unplanned solution;
- walks away.

The world does not need to become easy. It needs to remain causally playable.

---

## Information test

At the first meaningful decision, can the players perceive enough to choose intentionally?

They do not need the answer to the mystery.

They need:

- a concrete present situation;
- observable stakes;
- at least one actionable fact;
- some basis for distinguishing possible responses.

Mystery is strongest when players understand the question but not the answer.

---

## Pressure test

What changes if the PCs spend time doing something else?

If the answer is "nothing until they return to the plot," the Hook is static.

Give relevant actors goals and let conditions develop.

---

## Gravity test

Remove the PC names and replace them with a random adventuring party.

If the Hook works identically, its personal gravity may be weak.

Generic threats can still work, particularly at campaign scale, but when character-specific preparation is available, use it.

## Common failures and repairs

## Generic mystery

**Symptom**

Something strange happens, but no established character investment connects the party to it.

**Repair**

Attach the event to an existing goal, fear, relationship, enemy, obligation, or unresolved consequence.

Keep the mystery. Strengthen why this party notices.

---

## Quest-board gravity

**Symptom**

The Hook works only because an NPC asks the PCs to accept a mission.

**Repair**

Move the underlying problem into the world.

Let the NPC's request become one vector among several rather than the source of all momentum.

---

## Exposition before motion

**Symptom**

The DM must explain substantial history before the players understand why they should act.

**Repair**

Begin with the Catalyst.

Surface only the information required for the first decision. Put deeper lore on linked pages or reveal it through later Developments.

---

## Single-door Hook

**Symptom**

The apparent problem has one prepared destination, one prepared method, or one useful NPC.

**Repair**

Add independent vectors around the situation:

- another source of information;
- another interested actor;
- another target;
- another kind of leverage;
- a meaningful way to refuse.

Make each door lead somewhere materially different; doors that share a destination collapse into one.

---

## Predetermined capture

**Symptom**

The Hook appears to offer resistance, but the preparation requires the PCs to be captured regardless of what they do.

**Repair**

Choose one honest structure.

If capture is a framing premise, begin after the capture at the first meaningful choice.

If avoiding capture is genuinely possible, play the attempt and support the resulting branches.

---

## Stakes without gravity

**Symptom**

The danger is enormous but emotionally remote.

**Repair**

Connect the large-scale problem to something already local and meaningful to the characters.

A threatened kingdom becomes immediate when the evacuation road passes through a PC's home, an enemy profits from the panic, or someone the PC promised to protect cannot leave.

---

## Gravity without pressure

**Symptom**

The party cares, but there is no reason to engage now rather than several sessions later.

**Repair**

Give the relevant actors motion.

Someone departs, evidence decays, a vote occurs, a rival advances, conditions worsen, or an opportunity closes.

---

## Pressure without choice

**Symptom**

The deadline exists only to force one immediate action.

**Repair**

Make the pressure create tradeoffs.

The party may still face a harsh deadline, but it can decide what to sacrifice, protect, prioritize, risk, or delegate.

---

## Repeated action

**Symptom**

An action Hook is followed by substantially the same action again.

**Repair**

Change dramatic texture.

Use the aftermath to reveal information, create social consequences, expose a clue, introduce an ally, force a decision, or show why the first attack mattered.

---

## DM-facing Hook body

Use the repository's existing frontmatter template. The following contract governs the body.

```markdown
# <Hook Title>

## Hook

<Put the immediate playable situation here. Lead with what changes now. Keep this usable as the DM's opening frame rather than as a synopsis of the planned story.>

## Player Gravity

- [[PC]] — <established investment> → <why this Hook touches it>
- [[PC]] — <optional additional Link of Relevance>

## First Look

- <observable fact>
- <observable stake>
- <actionable clue, actor, danger, or opportunity>

## Pressure

- **Now:** <what makes the situation active>
- **Next:** <what changes if time passes>

## Open Vectors

These are prep coverage, not a player-facing menu.

- **<approach class>:** <what part of the prepared world supports it>
- **<approach class>:** <what part of the prepared world supports it>
- **<approach class>:** <what part of the prepared world supports it>

## If Unanswered

<Describe what relevant actors do and how the situation changes if the party delays, refuses, fails, or leaves.>

## Handoff

**Likely:** [[Development Beat or Cliffhanger Beat]] — <how pressure changes texture>

**Alternate:** <different transition created by a materially different player response>
```

## Runtime compression

The DM-facing note is an execution surface, not a design essay.

Prioritize:

1. what is happening;
2. why the party cares;
3. what the players can perceive;
4. what changes with time;
5. what the world supports if they surprise the prep.

Keep extensive biography, faction history, location description, mechanics, and reusable lore on their owning pages and reach them with `[[wikilinks]]` or precise embeds.

The beat page owns only what is unique to **this occurrence of this situation**.

## Reference basis

This framework combines two compatible models.

## Beat function

*Scripting the Game* defines the Hook as the opening beat and treats its purpose as immediate player involvement through threat or interest. It permits action-heavy Hooks, development-shaped Hooks, and dedicated patterns including kidnapping, dangerous disorientation, discovery, crisis, revelation, murder, false accusation, and looming threat. It also recommends changing dramatic texture after the Hook rather than stacking equivalent beats.

## Player gravity and agency

The supplied Brennan Lee Mulligan material frames players as pursuing the routes that make sense to their characters while the GM shapes the surrounding terrain. Preparation becomes reliable when conflicts and obstacles attach to things the characters already care about. Backstory, goals, and fears therefore provide reliable gravity without converting the resulting route into a rail.

The operational synthesis used by this skill is:

> **Anchor deeply. Pressure honestly. Leave the route open.**

That is the target state of every Hook.

## Strong starts (session openers)

Read when authoring the session's opening moment (`moment-01-<slug>.md`) —
`composing-beats` picks which moment opens the night and hands off here;
every "live thread" a start connects to is found by
`.claude/skills/draft-content/references/run-guide.md` § Standard queries
(loose-thread greps, faction fronts, PC Arc Notes), never a hand-maintained
state file.

The Strong Start is the first 30 seconds of the session. The party is
already mid-action. There is no briefing, no recap beyond the run guide's
own Recap box handing off into this moment's opener — no "you're at the
inn." The world moved while the players were away, and they're catching
up.

Brennan Lee Mulligan's formulation is the standard this section holds to:
open on an active scene that demands a response from the characters, never
an out-of-character summary, and **dig a hole the players can jump into**.
The opener's job is to land the stakes emotionally before a word of
exposition — a party that feels why tonight matters does not need to be
told.

That is also the test for a start that is technically active and still
weak. A scene the party can watch is not a hole. The hole is the thing they
have to answer.

Get close to the action and the dice — when in doubt, start with combat; it
needs the least setup and gets the table moving fastest
(`.claude/skills/composing-beats/references/runtime-surface.md`, whose eight-step prep
sequence still owns everything around the opener).

### The five types

Pick one per session. **Vary across sessions** — if the last session opened
with a chase, this one opens with a discovery or a standoff.

#### 1. In Medias Res — Already Moving

The party is mid-action when the session opens. Running, climbing, falling,
sailing into something. No setup.

**Works when:** the last session ended on a cliffhanger, the party is
traveling, or you want to skip the boring "what do we do next" phase.

**Example:** *"Your boots hit wet stone as the tunnel slopes downward.
Behind you, the sound of boots — six, maybe eight — gaining. Ahead, the
passage forks. Left runs dark. Right glows amber. You have about four
seconds."*

**Failure mode:** if the party needs a lore dump to understand why they're
running, you started too far in. "People are chasing us" is clear. Naming a
faction's internal doctrine is not.

#### 2. The Interrupted Routine

The party is doing something mundane when the world intrudes. Breakfast,
shopping, repairs, downtime — then the knock, the explosion, the body in
the water.

**Works when:** transitioning from downtime into action, or the party just
arrived somewhere new and normalcy is worth establishing before breaking
it.

**Example:** *"The coffee is good here. The first good cup in two weeks.
Then the harbor bell starts ringing — not the hour bell, the other one. The
one they ring for fires. And for ships that aren't supposed to be here."*

**Failure mode:** if the interruption doesn't connect to an open thread
(a loose-thread grep hit, a live front), it feels random. The knock must be
from someone the party recognizes or fears.

#### 3. The Standoff — Already Committed

The party arrives and finds two forces already in tension. They're the
variable. Both sides want them, or neither trusts them, or their presence
destabilizes the balance.

**Works when:** a faction front is live, the party is known locally, or you
want to force an early choice.

**Example:** *"The market is too quiet. Half the stalls are shuttered. At
the north end, six soldiers. At the south end, four dockhands with
crowbars, standing in front of a warehouse door. Both groups are looking at
you."*

**Failure mode:** if neither side has a personal connection to a PC, the
party shrugs and leaves. At least one side needs that connection — check
`vault/campaigns/shattered-sea/pcs/*.md` Arc Notes before writing this one.

#### 4. The Discovery — Already Wrong

The party finds something that shouldn't be there, or something missing
that should be. The discovery is the hook — they're now the ones who know.

**Works when:** revealing a conspiracy, advancing a mystery, or showing the
consequences of a past choice.

**Example:** *"The ship is exactly where you left it. The crew is not. The
gangplank is down. On the deck, in chalk, someone has drawn the same
symbol you found three days ago."*

**Failure mode:** if the discovery requires explanation, it's exposition,
not a discovery. The wrongness must be visceral and immediate.

#### 5. The Return — It's Not Over

Something the party thought resolved has come back, changed. The villain
they spared, the deal they made, the ally they left behind.

**Works when:** showing the world reacting to the party's choices, or
reintroducing a thread that went cold — check the loose-thread REVIEW grep
first; a thread with an open `REVIEW` line is a strong candidate.

**Example:** *"You recognize the voice before you see the face. He's
standing in the road ahead of you, and he's not alone. He's not smiling the
way he used to."*

**Failure mode:** if the callback doesn't relate to a choice the party
made, it's just a returning character, not a "Return." The power of this
type is showing agency shaped the world.

---

#### Cold open — hand-off

A session that opens before the PCs' own Hook, from a borrowed NPC
perspective, is a cold open — sole authority:
`.claude/skills/writing-cold-opens/`.

### Strong Start Quality Checklist

- [ ] Can you describe the situation in one sentence without lore?
- [ ] Does the party need to choose within 30 seconds of the opener box ending?
- [ ] Does it connect to an open thread from the Standard queries (a loose
      thread, a live front, a PC's Arc Notes)?
- [ ] Is the opener box read-aloud ready (`dnd5e-scene-narration`'s voice —
      sensory, present tense, second person)?
- [ ] Would the situation still make sense if the party forgot everything
      from last session?

If any answer is "no," rework the opener.

### Anti-patterns

| Anti-Pattern | Why It Fails | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| **The Recap Start** | "Last time, you..." puts players in listening mode, not acting mode | Open mid-action. The recap is implicit in what's happening (the run guide's Recap box already carried the handoff). |
| **The Lore Gate** | Situation requires knowledge the players might not remember | Make the pressure physical and immediate. Context can come later through play. |
| **The Gentle Nudge** | "A messenger arrives with a letter..." is passive; the party is receiving, not doing | The messenger is bleeding. The letter is on fire. Make arrival an event, not a delivery. |
| **The Split Party Opener** | Opening in a situation that only involves part of the table | Open with the full party present, or at minimum the majority. Split after, not before. |
| **The Assigned Beat** | A line apiece handing each named PC their own private thing turns the opener into a queue and decides in advance what each player will care about | Open one situation that reaches the whole table at once. Name a PC to aim the world at them, never to issue them a thread of their own; one PC named and the rest standing in it is fine. |
| **The Consequence-Free Hook** | "You hear a rumor about..." — the party can just ignore it | Make ignoring it visibly costly. The rumor walks through the door. |
| **The Over-Authored Start** | 8 paragraphs of read-aloud before the party can act | A tight opener — every sentence past what the moment needs is the DM talking to themselves. |

### Inspiration bank

Forty example openers grouped by location, from Sly Flourish's LGMRD. Raw
material to reskin against this campaign's specifics, never to copy
verbatim — check it for a jumping-off point when a session's location is
set but the opening beat isn't coming.

**Cities and towns** (combat or roleplaying):

1. The characters interrupt bandits breaking into a shop.
2. Something slithers out of a nearby sewer.
3. A noble lord bumps into one of the characters and threatens to have them arrested.
4. A group of cultists kindly ask for a sample of a character's blood.
5. A hooded patron visits the characters, asking the characters to kill them in two days.
6. A riot draws the local watch away, whereupon a squad of hired killers descends on the characters.
7. The campaign's main villain shows up and invites the characters for a drink.
8. A scarred explorer offers to sell one of the characters a map to a site of a lost or stolen ancestral heirloom.
9. A golem from a wizards' academy goes on a rampage.
10. The local monarch is assassinated and a villain takes over the government.

**Sewers** (numerous monsters and hazards):

1. A flood of poisonous water flows past the characters' position.
2. The sewer collapses into deeper tunnels sealed up for centuries.
3. A wererat approaches the characters, offering to sell valuable information.
4. A pack of ghouls chase a young couple reported missing days ago.
5. A legendary giant crocodile stealthily stalks the characters.
6. The characters find a powerful magical dagger sought by a guild of wraith assassins.
7. Swampy sewer gas gives one of the characters supernatural visions of the villain's master plan.
8. The characters meet an eccentric wizard farming mushrooms for spell components.
9. A wall collapses, revealing a hidden temple of the god of slimes and oozes.
10. A flood of water draws the characters into a dangerously large mechanical sluice system.

**Wilderness** (action or mystery):

1. A nearby tree opens up, and a satyr steps through and says "Hi!"
2. A rampaging werebear storms through the area, mistaking the characters for the hunters who killed their mate.
3. Night falls, revealing an alien starscape above.
4. The characters see a tall humanoid with antlers stalking from the shadows, carrying a large scythe in one hand and three humanoid heads in the other.
5. The ground suddenly churns, bringing the body of a long-lost elf king to the surface. The king's eyes open.
6. The characters stumble upon a nest of skeletal pixies surrounding a desecrated fey gate.
7. A golden-antlered stag leaps into the characters' camp and asks to be defended from the hunters chasing it.
8. An old woman greets the characters, offering them candy and baked treats if they will come to her nearby cottage.
9. A skeleton hanging from a tree begs the characters to right the wrong it committed while alive.
10. A sinkhole opens up, revealing the tunnels of long-forgotten burial chambers.

**Dungeons, caves, and caverns** (widest range of strong starts):

1. A vampire appears from a sudden rise of mist, introduces herself, and asks the characters for a favor.
2. An ancient statue turns its head toward the characters and whispers a valuable secret.
3. The floor collapses, revealing even deeper tunnels long forgotten.
4. Through a cracked wall, the characters spot a gateway flanked by two huge obsidian statues, and featuring a set of stairs leading down.
5. The characters come across two bands of goblins fighting each other for the favor of a hag named Auntie Chiptooth.
6. An eyestalk swells out from an oozy patch on the wall, beholds the characters, and then disappears back into the wall.
7. A wounded knight collapses near the characters, begging them to find her lost love before she dies.
8. The ground cracks open and a pillar of chipped obsidian juts out, projecting a prophecy in red Infernal glyphs on the walls of the chamber.
9. Stars swim in a moonlit well, then rise up to reveal themselves as will-o'-wisps.
10. A spectral hound guides the characters to the camp of a reclusive mage.
