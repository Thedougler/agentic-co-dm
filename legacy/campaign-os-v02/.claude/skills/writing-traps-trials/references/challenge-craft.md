# Challenge craft

Detailed design reference for steps 4–9 of the writing-traps-trials
workflow. SKILL.md carries the step summaries and completion criteria;
this file supplies the craft each step draws on.

## Telegraph

### Reveal ladder

#### Immediate

Facts a character can notice simply by being present.

Use sensory evidence and material consequences:

* scorch marks;
* displaced dust;
* grooves;
* corpses;
* snapped chains;
* damp stone;
* humming crystals;
* repeated iconography;
* unnatural silence;
* pressure changes;
* movement in machinery;
* prior damage.

Severe threats receive stronger immediate telegraphing.

#### Investigated

Information gained by interacting with the environment, asking sensible
questions, using tools, consulting lore, or succeeding on appropriate
checks.

Reveal actionable information rather than labels. Prefer:

> The western tiles sit a finger-width lower than the others.

over:

> You detect a pressure-plate trap.

#### Understood

Deeper investigation reveals:

* likely trigger;
* mechanism;
* pattern;
* timing;
* vulnerable component;
* safest region;
* reset behavior;
* previous attempts;
* creator's assumptions.

#### Mastered

Exceptional investigation, prior knowledge, experimentation, or resource
expenditure can reveal an advantage:

* control point;
* shutdown;
* safe timing window;
* alternate route;
* reversibility;
* way to redirect the effect;
* way to weaponize it.

Essential information must remain obtainable after a failed check. Use
checks to improve position, precision, speed, or certainty rather than
to decide whether play continues.

## Interaction

### Affordances

List the physical or fictional things characters can actually act upon:

* mechanisms;
* doors;
* tiles;
* statues;
* pipes;
* runes;
* vents;
* chains;
* supports;
* magical fields;
* moving terrain;
* creatures;
* control interfaces;
* environmental sources;
* sacrificial objects;
* witnesses;
* ritual stations.

Give each important element one or more affordances.

### Solution vectors

Test distinct solution vectors:

* **avoid** — go around, over, beneath, or away;
* **disable** — stop the mechanism;
* **solve** — understand and satisfy its logic;
* **redirect** — change its target or output;
* **endure** — accept the cost and push through;
* **shield** — mitigate its consequence;
* **outrun** — beat its timing;
* **exploit** — turn the challenge against another target;
* **alter** — reshape terrain, machinery, magic, or environment;
* **satisfy** — meet the terms of a trial;
* **subvert** — satisfy the literal mechanism while violating its assumed method.

These are vectors, not an exhaustive solution list.

When a player proposes something plausible that the prepared answers did
not anticipate, adjudicate from the established mechanism. Strong ideas
can bypass substantial preparation. That is a successful interaction with
the world.

### Roll criteria

Use a roll only when:

1. the attempted action is possible;
2. meaningful uncertainty remains;
3. success and failure both produce interesting states.

Automatic success is appropriate when the fiction has removed uncertainty.

## Consequences

### Consequence types

Choose consequences that arise from the fiction:

* damage;
* conditions;
* lost time;
* noise or attention;
* enemy reinforcement;
* resource consumption;
* separation;
* displacement;
* damaged equipment;
* changed terrain;
* lost opportunity;
* compromised stealth;
* altered information;
* release of a contained threat;
* temporary route closure;
* permanent world-state change.

Prefer consequences that create new decisions.

### Partial states

Use partial states where useful:

* success with cost;
* incomplete disarm;
* delayed trigger;
* reduced area;
* one component disabled;
* safe passage for only part of the group;
* correct answer with an unintended revelation.

For puzzles and trials, a wrong answer should reveal information,
increase pressure, consume a resource, or transform the problem rather
than merely returning to the starting state.

## Escalation

Use escalation when time or repeated interaction should matter.

Escalation changes the problem:

* water rises;
* safe terrain shrinks;
* mechanisms accelerate;
* visibility worsens;
* enemies become aware;
* magical instability spreads;
* exits close;
* resources become contaminated;
* parts of the puzzle become inaccessible;
* the trapped creature awakens;
* structural damage propagates.

Avoid repeated identical checks against an unchanged obstacle.

If using rounds, turns, stages, countdown segments, or event triggers,
make each stage runnable from the page.

## Spotlight

### Contribution types

Check whether the challenge can reward different kinds of contribution:

* perception and investigation;
* lore and memory;
* tool use;
* spellcasting;
* strength and mobility;
* social reasoning;
* protective abilities;
* resource sacrifice;
* pattern recognition;
* scouting;
* teamwork;
* lateral thinking.

A challenge may spotlight one archetype without reducing everyone else
to spectators.

Treat character abilities that cleanly overcome the challenge as earned
advantages. If a spell, class feature, item, language, background, or
prior alliance solves the relevant problem, let that capability matter
unless the fiction already establishes a reason it behaves differently.

## Sandbox

### Stress-test questions

Test the challenge as a world object:

* What happens if the party reaches it much earlier than expected?
* What happens if they reach it after its creator is dead or defeated?
* Can they approach from the opposite direction?
* Can they leave and return?
* Can they bypass it entirely?
* Can they destroy the surrounding architecture?
* Can they send a creature, summon, familiar, object, or enemy through first?
* Can they manipulate enemies into triggering it?
* Can they use its machinery or magic for another purpose?
* Can a relevant spell or feature trivialize part of it?
* What persists after it fires?
* What does the rest of the world notice?
* Does the challenge still make sense after nearby faction or location state changes?

Resolve these questions by clarifying the mechanism, not by adding
arbitrary immunities.

The PCs are the protagonists. Preparation supplies pressure, information,
and consequences; player choices determine the story produced from them.
